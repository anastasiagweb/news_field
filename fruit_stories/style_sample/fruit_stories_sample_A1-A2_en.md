# Fruit Stories

## 1. The Blood Orange Incident

Derek is a quiet man. He likes quiet mornings, quiet tea and quiet toast. He does not like surprises.

Today, his daughter Sophie has left a bag of fruit on the kitchen table. There is a note: "Try these, Dad. They're blood oranges. DELICIOUS."

Derek picks one up. It looks like a normal orange, but smaller and darker.

He cuts it in half.

The inside is dark red. Very dark red.

Derek drops the orange.

"What on earth," he says.

He picks it up again and looks more carefully. The fruit is deep red, like wine. It does not look like food. It looks like something from a horror film.

Derek is not a dramatic man. But he phones Sophie.

"Sophie. The oranges. They're red inside."

"Yes, Dad. They're blood oranges. That's normal."

"It is not normal. Oranges are orange. That is why they are called oranges."

Sophie sighs. "Just taste it."

Derek takes a very small bite. His eyes go wide.

It is sweet. It is sharp. It is possibly the best orange he has ever tasted.

He eats the whole thing in two minutes. Then he eats a second one.

His wife, Barbara, comes into the kitchen. Derek is standing at the counter. His hands are dark red. His chin is dark red. There is red juice on his white shirt.

Barbara screams.

"I'm fine!" Derek says. "It's an orange!"

"You are covered in red!"

"It's a blood orange! They're red inside!"

Barbara does not believe him. She looks at his hands, his shirt, the red juice on the counter. She picks up her phone.

"I'm calling Sophie."

"She told me to eat them!"

Sophie answers. Barbara puts her on speaker.

"Sophie, your father looks like he has been in a fight with a tomato."

"He found the blood oranges, then?"

"He found them."

Derek eats a third orange while they talk. He does not care about his shirt anymore. The oranges are too good.

"I need more of these," he says.

"There were six in the bag, Dad."

Derek looks at the bag. It is empty.

"There were six," he says quietly.

Barbara looks at the empty bag, then at Derek's red hands, then at his red face.

"You ate six blood oranges in fifteen minutes?"

"They're very small."

That evening, Sophie visits. She brings more blood oranges. Derek opens the door. He is wearing a clean shirt.

"I have decided," he says, "that blood oranges are my favourite fruit."

"What happened to 'oranges should be orange'?"

"I was wrong. I am big enough to admit that."

Barbara appears behind him. "He has already ordered a box online. Five kilograms."

Derek smiles. "They were on sale."

Some mornings are quiet. Some mornings, you discover blood oranges. Derek's mornings will never be the same.

Derek: 1. Blood Oranges: 6.

## 2. Fig Roll

Wendy has never eaten a fresh fig.

She has eaten fig rolls from a packet. She has seen figs in paintings — usually in a bowl with grapes and a dead bird. But she has never held a real fig in her hand.

Today, her neighbour Martin gives her a bag.

"From my tree," he says proudly. "First year it's had fruit."

Wendy looks in the bag. The figs are small, soft and purple. They look like little plums that have given up.

"Thank you," she says. "What do I do with them?"

Martin looks surprised. "You eat them."

"Just... eat them?"

"Cut them open. They're beautiful inside."

Wendy takes the figs into her kitchen. She puts one on a plate and cuts it carefully in half.

Inside, the fig is pink and red, full of tiny seeds. It looks like a small, edible sunset.

"Oh," Wendy says.

She takes a bite. It is soft, sweet, and unlike anything she has expected. It tastes like honey and jam and summer all at once.

Wendy eats three figs very quickly.

Her husband, Arthur, comes in.

"What are you eating?"

"Figs. Martin gave them to us. Arthur, they are incredible."

Arthur does not like new food. Arthur likes toast, chips and the occasional biscuit. He has eaten the same lunch for thirty years.

"No thank you," he says.

"Just try one."

"I don't like figs."

"You've never had one."

"I know I don't like them."

Wendy puts a fig half on a small plate and leaves it on the table. She says nothing more. She knows Arthur.

Twenty minutes later, she hears a noise from the kitchen. She walks in quietly.

Arthur is standing at the table. The small plate is empty. He is cutting a second fig.

"I thought you didn't like figs," Wendy says.

Arthur does not turn around. "I'm doing research."

"Research?"

"I need more data before I form an opinion."

Wendy watches him eat three more figs. His research is going very well.

By the evening, the bag is empty. They have eaten twelve figs between them.

"We should tell Martin they were nice," Wendy says.

"We should ask Martin for more," Arthur says.

The next morning, Wendy sees Arthur in the garden. He is talking to Martin over the fence. They are having a long, serious conversation.

"What were you talking about?" she asks when Arthur comes inside.

"Fig trees," Arthur says. "How to grow them. What kind of soil they need. When to plant them."

"You're growing a fig tree?"

"Martin says it takes three years to get fruit."

"Three years?"

Arthur looks at her. "Wendy, those figs were extraordinary. Three years is nothing."

He has already ordered a tree online. It arrives on Thursday.

Some people discover figs and eat a few. Arthur discovered figs and changed his entire garden.

Arthur: 1. Figs: 12.

## 3. The Grape Situation

Nobody knows how it started. But now, ten-year-old Sam and his eight-year-old brother, Oliver, are throwing grapes at each other in the kitchen.

Their mother, Helen, is upstairs. She is on a video call with her manager. She cannot hear the grapes.

Sam throws a green grape. It hits Oliver on the nose.

"Direct hit!" Sam whispers.

Oliver throws a red grape. It misses Sam and hits the window.

"You throw like a penguin," Sam says.

"Penguins don't throw."

"Exactly."

Oliver takes two grapes and throws them both. One hits Sam's ear. The other lands in the dog's water bowl.

Baxter, the dog, looks at the grape in his water. He looks at the boys. He eats the grape from the water and wags his tail.

"Baxter is on my team," Oliver says.

The grapes fly faster. Green ones go left. Red ones go right. Some hit the fridge. Some hit the floor. One lands perfectly on top of the pepper pot.

"That was amazing," Sam says.

"I was aiming for your head."

Helen's meeting finishes. She walks downstairs.

The kitchen floor is covered in grapes. Some are whole. Some are not. There is grape juice on the table, the wall and one of the chairs. Baxter is eating grapes from the floor with great enthusiasm.

Helen stands in the doorway. She counts to five.

"Boys."

Sam and Oliver freeze. Sam has a grape in his hand. Oliver has two.

"What happened here?"

"We were having a grape battle," Sam says.

"A grape battle."

"Oliver started it."

"You started it!" Oliver says.

"Nobody is starting anything ever again," Helen says. "Both of you. Kitchen. Clean. Now."

The boys clean the floor. Baxter tries to help by eating more grapes. This makes the cleaning slower, not faster.

Helen inspects the kitchen. It is mostly clean. One grape is stuck behind the radiator. She decides to leave it.

"Can I ask," she says, "why grapes?"

Sam thinks. "They're the right size."

"The right size for what?"

"For throwing."

Helen cannot argue with this logic, so she doesn't try.

That evening, Helen's husband, Greg, comes home. He opens the fridge.

"Where are all the grapes? I bought two bags yesterday."

Helen looks at the boys. The boys look at the floor.

"There was an incident," Helen says.

"What kind of incident?"

"A grape incident."

Greg closes the fridge. "I'm not going to ask, am I?"

"You are not."

Greg goes to the shop and buys more grapes. He puts them on the top shelf of the fridge, behind the milk.

"These are for eating," he says. "Not for battles."

"There won't be any more battles," Helen says.

The next morning, Helen finds three grapes behind the sofa cushion.

She eats them. They are fine.

Grapes bought: 2 bags. Grapes eaten in the normal way: very few.

## 4. The Pear Problem

Nigel does not understand pears.

Every week, his wife Linda buys pears. Every week, Nigel watches them. On Monday, they are hard as stones. By Friday, they are brown and soft. There is never a Wednesday.

"When do you eat them?" Nigel asks.

"When they're ready," Linda says.

"But when are they ready?"

"You just know."

Nigel does not just know. Nigel has a system. He buys four pears on Saturday and puts them in the fruit bowl. He checks them every day.

Monday. Hard. He taps one with his finger. It makes a sound like a door.

Tuesday. Still hard. He tries to bite one. It is like biting a cricket ball.

Wednesday. He checks in the morning. Hard. He checks after lunch. Hard. He goes for a walk. When he comes back, two of the pears are soft, brown and finished.

"How?" Nigel says to the empty kitchen. "I was gone for forty minutes!"

The other two pears are still hard. Nigel puts them on the windowsill, where he can see them.

Thursday morning. He looks at the pears. Hard.

He goes to make tea. The kettle takes three minutes. He comes back.

One pear is perfect. Golden, soft, ready to eat. The other is mush.

"This is impossible," Nigel says.

He eats the perfect pear. It is delicious. Sweet juice runs down his chin. He closes his eyes.

"That," he says, "was worth the wait."

Linda finds him in the kitchen, holding a pear core and looking very happy.

"Good pear?" she asks.

"Excellent pear. But I lost three."

"Lost them?"

"They went from stone to soup. I missed the middle."

Linda laughs. "Pears are like that. You have to catch them."

"Catch them?"

"The perfect moment. It's about five minutes long."

"Five minutes?"

"Maybe ten."

Nigel decides to take this seriously. The next week, he buys six pears. He puts them on the counter. He sets alarms on his phone. Every two hours: CHECK PEARS.

His phone buzzes at work. His colleague, Dave, looks at the screen.

"'Check pears'? What does that mean?"

"Exactly what it says."

"You have a pear alarm?"

"I have six pear alarms."

Dave stares at him. Nigel does not explain further.

By Thursday, Nigel has caught three perfect pears. He has eaten them all with great satisfaction. The other three went from hard to brown while he was in the shower.

"Three out of six," he tells Linda. "Fifty percent success rate."

"That's actually quite good for pears."

Nigel writes this in his notebook. He has started a notebook about pears. He does not tell Dave about the notebook.

Some men climb mountains. Some men sail oceans. Nigel catches pears at the perfect moment, and he is very proud of himself.

Pears bought: 10. Pears caught at the right moment: 4. Nigel calls this progress.

## 5. The Apple Argument

It starts on a Tuesday, like all good arguments.

"Braeburn," says Margaret.

"Granny Smith," says her sister, Dorothy.

They are standing in the fruit section of the supermarket. They have been standing here for four minutes. The other shoppers walk around them.

"Braeburn is the best apple," Margaret says. "It's sweet. It's crisp. It's perfect."

"Granny Smith is the best apple. It's sharp. It's fresh. It wakes you up."

"I don't need an apple to wake me up. I have tea."

"Your tea is too weak."

"My tea is not part of this conversation."

Dorothy picks up a Granny Smith and holds it like a trophy. "This apple has character."

Margaret picks up a Braeburn. "This apple has flavour."

A shop assistant named Kevin walks past. He makes the mistake of stopping.

"Can I help?" he asks.

"Yes," Margaret says. "Tell my sister that Braeburn apples are better than Granny Smith."

Kevin looks at Margaret. He looks at Dorothy. He looks at the exit.

"They're both very popular," he says carefully.

"That is not an answer," Dorothy says.

Kevin excuses himself and walks quickly to the bread section.

Margaret puts two bags of Braeburn apples in the trolley. Dorothy puts two bags of Granny Smith apples in the trolley. Neither removes the other's apples. This is progress.

At Margaret's house, they make tea. Margaret makes hers strong because of what Dorothy said. She does not mention this.

"Try one," Dorothy says, offering a Granny Smith.

Margaret bites it. Her face changes.

"Fine," she says. "It's not terrible."

"Not terrible? It's wonderful."

"It's acceptable." Margaret pauses. "Try my Braeburn."

Dorothy bites the Braeburn. She chews slowly. She says nothing for a moment.

"Well?" Margaret asks.

"It's... fine."

"Fine? You're smiling!"

"I am not smiling."

"You are definitely smiling."

"It's the tea."

They eat three apples each and do not agree on anything. But the plate is empty, and they are both having a very good afternoon.

Margaret's husband, Robert, comes home. There are apple cores everywhere.

"Apple tasting?" he asks.

"Apple discussion," Margaret says.

"Did you reach a conclusion?"

Margaret and Dorothy look at each other.

"No," they say together.

Robert picks up a red apple from the fruit bowl. "I like Pink Lady," he says.

The silence is extraordinary.

"Get out," Margaret says.

Robert takes his apple and goes to the garden. He enjoys it very much.

The apple argument has been going on for thirty years. It will continue for thirty more. Margaret and Dorothy would not have it any other way.

Braeburn: excellent. Granny Smith: also excellent. Pink Lady: do not mention it.

## 6. The Cherry Thieves

Patricia's cherry tree is the best tree on Elm Road. Every June, it fills with bright red cherries, and Patricia makes pies, jam and crumbles.

There is one problem. Birds.

"They eat half the cherries before I can pick them," Patricia tells her husband, Frank.

"Put a net over the tree," Frank says.

"I tried. I got stuck in the net."

"That was funny."

"It was not funny. The neighbours saw."

This year, Patricia has a new plan. She buys a plastic owl from the garden centre. She puts it on a stick next to the tree.

"Birds are scared of owls," she tells Frank.

"It's bright yellow."

"It was on sale."

The first morning, Patricia looks out the window. Three pigeons are sitting on the tree. One is sitting on the owl's head.

"It's not working," Frank says.

"Give it time."

By the afternoon, two blackbirds and a robin have joined the pigeons. They are eating cherries and ignoring the owl completely.

Patricia moves the owl closer to the tree. The pigeons do not move. One pigeon looks at Patricia and eats a cherry very slowly.

"That bird is doing it on purpose," she says.

"It's a pigeon, Pat. It doesn't have a plan."

"You don't know that."

Patricia tries a different approach. She hangs old CDs from the branches. The light will scare the birds away.

The birds love the CDs. They sit on top of them. One blackbird studies its reflection for ten minutes.

"The blackbird is admiring himself," Frank says from the window.

Patricia goes outside with a tin tray and a wooden spoon. She bangs them together. The birds fly away.

They come back in three minutes.

"Right," Patricia says. She picks up a basket and starts picking the cherries herself. If she can't stop the birds, she can be faster than them.

She picks for two hours. Her fingers are red, her back hurts and she has one large basket of cherries.

She goes inside. She makes a cherry pie. It is the best pie she has made all year.

Frank eats two pieces. "This is delicious."

"Thank you."

"Maybe the birds should get some? As a thank you?"

"The birds got plenty."

She looks out the window. The pigeons are still in the tree. They look happy. The yellow owl looks embarrassed.

That evening, Patricia posts a photo of her pie on the Elm Road group. Everyone asks for the recipe.

"Secret recipe," she types.

The secret is: pick the cherries before the birds eat them all. That's it. That's the recipe.

Cherries picked by Patricia: one basket. Cherries eaten by birds: two baskets. Score: Birds 2, Patricia 1.

## 7. The Perfect Peach

It is the hottest day of the summer. Thirty-two degrees. Ben is lying on a blanket in the garden. He cannot move. The sun is too hot. The air is too thick. Even the bees look tired.

"I need something cold," he says.

His girlfriend, Amy, comes out of the house. She is carrying two peaches.

"We don't have ice cream," she says. "But we have these."

She gives Ben a peach. It is heavy, soft and warm from sitting in the fruit bowl.

"A warm peach?" Ben says. "On the hottest day of the year?"

"Just eat it."

Ben bites into the peach.

The juice runs down his chin. It is sweet and warm and tastes like the whole summer in one bite. It is soft, but not too soft. The skin has a little roughness, then the flesh is smooth and golden.

Ben closes his eyes.

"Good?" Amy asks.

"I think this is the best thing I have ever eaten."

"It's a peach, Ben."

"It's not just a peach. It's a perfect peach."

They sit in the garden and eat their peaches slowly. There is no wind. The only sound is the bees and the juice dripping onto the blanket.

"We should have put a plate down," Amy says, looking at the blanket.

"Too late now."

Ben holds up his peach stone. He has eaten every piece of fruit from it. The stone is completely clean.

"I think I want to grow peaches," he says.

"You killed the basil last month."

"Basil is difficult."

"It's the easiest herb."

"For some people."

Amy finishes her peach. She looks at the stone. "You know, my grandmother had a peach tree. She used to say the best peaches are the ones you eat outside."

"Your grandmother was right."

"She also said the second-best thing about peaches is the mess."

Ben looks at his hands. They are sticky. There is juice on his shirt, his chin and the blanket.

"The mess is part of it," he agrees.

They go inside and wash their hands. Ben looks at the fruit bowl. There are two more peaches.

"Save them for tomorrow?" Amy asks.

"What if tomorrow isn't as hot?"

"It's supposed to be thirty-four degrees."

"Then yes. Save them."

The next day is hotter. They eat the peaches at noon, standing in the kitchen because the garden is too bright.

They are just as good.

"I don't want summer to end," Ben says.

"It won't end for weeks."

"But the peaches might."

"Then we buy more peaches."

Ben smiles. Some problems have very simple solutions.

Peaches eaten: 4. Peaches enjoyed: 4. Success rate: 100%.

## 8. The Plum Situation

It is September, and Harriet's plum tree has gone mad.

There are plums on every branch. There are plums on the ground. There are plums on the garden path, in the flower beds and on the front step.

Harriet picks plums every morning. She fills baskets, bags and boxes. But the tree keeps making more.

"I have too many plums," she tells her neighbour, George.

"I can see that," George says. He is standing on a plum.

Harriet gives George a bag of plums. She gives a bag to Mrs Chen across the road. She gives three bags to the school. She leaves two bags outside her gate with a sign: FREE PLUMS.

The bags are still there at the end of the day. Nobody wants plums. Everybody has plums. September is plum season and every garden on the street has a plum tree.

"What am I going to do?" Harriet asks her daughter, Rose.

"Make jam?" Rose suggests.

Harriet makes jam. She makes twelve jars. Her kitchen smells like sugar and fruit. The jam is dark purple and looks beautiful.

She still has three baskets of plums.

"Make a crumble?" Rose suggests.

Harriet makes four crumbles. She gives two to the neighbours. She puts two in the freezer.

She still has two baskets of plums.

"Make a cake?"

Harriet makes a plum cake. Then a second one. Then a plum tart.

The plums keep coming.

"The tree is doing this on purpose," Harriet says.

"Mum, it's a tree."

"It's a very productive tree."

By the end of the week, Harriet has made twelve jars of jam, four crumbles, two cakes, a tart and a large bowl of stewed plums. Her freezer is full. Her neighbours are full. The school has sent a polite note asking for no more plums, please.

But every morning, there are more plums on the ground.

Harriet sits in her garden with a cup of tea. She looks at the tree.

"You win," she says.

The tree says nothing. A plum falls onto the grass.

George comes to the fence. "Still got plums?"

"George, I have plums coming out of my ears."

"My wife made plum gin last year. Very easy. You just put plums in a bottle with gin and sugar."

Harriet looks at him. "How much gin?"

"As much as you want."

Harriet goes to the shop. She buys six bottles of gin.

By October, Harriet has twenty-four bottles of plum gin in her cupboard. They glow purple in the light. They will be ready by Christmas.

"I think," she tells Rose, "that the plum tree has finally met its match."

At Christmas, Harriet gives plum gin to everyone. The neighbours love it. The school teachers love it. George and his wife love it.

"This is the best Christmas present I've ever had," Mrs Chen says.

Harriet looks at her tree through the window. It is bare now, resting for winter.

"Same time next year," she says to the tree.

She is already planning. Thirty bottles should be enough.

Plums from the tree: hundreds. Plum gin bottles: 24. Harriet's Christmas popularity: through the roof.
