# Mentalité Doctrine — No Modern Minds in Ancient Heads

Read before drafting any premise, story card, framework, or prose, and before every audit. This is a **hard rule** of the Living History line: characters and narrator must think inside their world. A single modern thought placed in an ancient head breaks the spell for the native reader faster than a wrong object does.

*Mentalité* is the historians' word for the habits of mind of a period: what people took for granted, feared, hoped, valued, found funny or shameful, and could not even imagine. Objects can be checked in a museum; minds have to be checked against this document.

---

## Why this matters more than objects

A wrong object is a mistake. A wrong mind is a lie about the people. When a Bronze Age dyer drafts "a proposal with numbers and projections" about sustainable harvesting, the reader no longer meets a person of 1500 BCE — they meet a modern consultant in costume. The story stops being history and becomes flattery of the present.

Equally, the opposite failure — making past people stupid, superstitious in a sneering way, or uniformly brutal — is a mentalité error. They were as intelligent, funny, and complicated as we are. They simply thought with different furniture.

## The core principle

**The world as they saw it is the world of the story.** If they believed the goddess sent the snake, then in the story the snake is the goddess's messenger — for the characters, and for the narrator speaking from inside their view. The narrator does not wink, explain, or correct. Explanation, where needed, belongs in the Historical Note, in plain words, after the story is over.

## The modern-leak catalogue

Every audit role checks premises and prose against these families. Each entry gives typical leaks and the period-true direction. The series bible extends this list for its civilisation.

### 1. Progress, innovation, and the future
- **Leak:** characters value novelty for itself; talk of "improving the system", "new ideas", "innovation", "the future of our craft"; a craftsman who "invents" a technique that later made history; narrator remarks such as "none of them knew they were building democracy", "archaeologists not yet born would wonder…", "a technique that would become the signature of…".
- **Period-true:** in most premodern cultures, the old way is the good way; authority comes from ancestors, gods, and custom. Change happens, but people justify it as a return to the proper way, a gift from a god, a trick learned from a stranger, a necessity. No narrator foresight, ever.

### 2. Economics and management
- **Leak:** "efficiency", "productivity", "profit margin", "investment", "capital", "percentages", "supply and demand", "market forces", "business plan", "budget", "proposal", "projections", "quarterly", "sustainability", "resource management", "deadline", "schedule" in the modern sense.
- **Period-true:** obligations, shares, portions, gifts, debts, oaths, tribute, rations, what is owed to the god, the house, the lord. Calculation existed and could be sharp — but in the period's terms and units.

### 3. Politics and rights
- **Leak:** speeches about equality, human rights, freedom of the individual, representation, proportional taxation, accountability, "having a voice" as a value in itself, written constitutions before writing, democracy before democracy, citizens of states that are really kinships or chiefdoms.
- **Period-true:** loyalty to kin, house, lord, city, god; honour and shame; precedent and custom; the duties of rank. Ordinary people resist, complain, bargain, flee, and rebel — for bread, land, honour, justice as their world understood it — not in the vocabulary of modern political theory.

### 4. Gender and social justice rhetoric
- **Leak:** a girl or woman who argues against "the old rules" in modern terms ("that knowledge doesn't care whether you're male or female"; "the old rules belonged to the old world"); men who reflect on gender equality; enslaved characters who speak of universal human rights; narrators who editorialise on injustice.
- **Period-true:** women and unfree people had real agency, skill, influence, cunning, and inner lives — exercised **inside** the period's structures: through kin, property rights where they existed, religious roles, crafts, alliances, persuasion, refusal, and endurance. Injustice is shown, not preached; the reader draws the conclusion. A father who teaches his daughter the craft does it because he has no son, because she is good, because the house needs it — not because he has a theory of equality.

### 5. Religion, scepticism, and the supernatural
- **Leak:** an agnostic builder who doubts the gods "if they exist"; ritual treated as "useful superstition"; characters who explain divine signs in natural terms; a narrator who treats belief as quaint; any "it was just the wind" debunking of the sacred.
- **Period-true:** the gods, spirits, ancestors, omens, and pollution are real features of the world for the characters. Scepticism did exist in some periods and milieux (e.g., some Greek thinkers from the 6th century BCE), and can appear where attested and appropriate — but as that period's scepticism, in its own terms, and never as the default mind of an ordinary person.

### 6. Psychology and the self
- **Leak:** therapy-speak ("processing her grief", "closure", "trauma", "boundaries", "self-esteem", "identity", "finding herself", "healing journey"); characters who reflect on their own personality as a project; motivational aphorisms ("Fear makes you run. Respect makes you sing."; "Desperation is the mother of innovation").
- **Period-true:** feelings are real and strong, but named and understood through the period's frame — the body (liver, heart, breath, bile), the gods (a god sent madness, courage, desire), honour and shame, duty, fate. Most of the time, show feeling through action and body and leave it unnamed.

### 7. Childhood, age, and the life course
- **Leak:** childhood as a protected stage of play and schooling everywhere; teenagers as a social category; "careers" chosen by personal passion; retirement; romantic love as the normal basis of marriage in cultures where marriage was arranged.
- **Period-true:** children work early, die often, are loved deeply; adulthood comes with rites, not birthdays; work is inherited from the house; marriage is an alliance of houses — affection may grow or be present, but it is not the expected foundation. Romance stories are allowed and welcome, but they must feel the weight of these structures.

### 8. Time, number, and measurement
- **Leak:** minutes, seconds, exact hours where no clocks existed; "three o'clock"; "a week" in cultures without a seven-day week; calendar dates in our era ("1500 BCE") in dialogue or narration; statistics, averages, percentages; "miles", "pounds", "feet", "acres" in narration of non-English cultures; birthdays and exact ages where ages were not counted that way.
- **Period-true:** dawn, midday, the lamp-lighting, the second watch; days to the next festival or moon; the period's own units, or neutral English for them ("a day's walk", "a basket", "two hands deep"). Our-era dates appear only in front matter and Historical Notes.

### 9. Knowledge of the world
- **Leak:** germs, hygiene as disease prevention, atoms, the shape of continents, modern country names (Afghanistan, Turkey, Italy as a unit, "Europe" as an identity), "Europe's first civilisation", awareness of their own period label ("the Bronze Age", "the Dark Age", "Minoan", "Mycenaean"), knowledge that iron is the future.
- **Period-true:** their geography, their names for peoples and places (or neutral descriptions), their medicine, their cosmos. Our period labels and scholarly names appear only in front matter and Notes.

### 10. Institutions that did not exist yet
- **Leak:** guilds in the Bronze Age, police, detectives, intelligence services, banks, schools for all, hospitals, prisons as punishment (in cultures that used other penalties), licensed traders, organised crime, "the market supervisors", "official recognition" as modern certification.
- **Period-true:** the institutions attested for that window (palace administrations, temple economies, councils of elders, assemblies where attested, courts as they actually worked, ordeals, oaths, family vengeance and its settlement), described in the period's own terms or plain English.

### 11. Language register of the narrator and dialogue
- **Leak:** modern idiom and slang ("OK", "no pressure", "team", "focus", "stress", "process", "manage", "issue", "handle it", "deal", "upgrade", "the bottom line"), modern sarcasm patterns, modern jokes ("Zeus throwing furniture"), therapy and corporate vocabulary, legal-bureaucratic modern phrasing ("pending inspection", "emergency protocols", "unauthorised operations"); also fake-archaic English ("thee", "thou", "forsooth", "methinks").
- **Period-true:** plain, timeless English; images taken from the characters' own world (animals, weather, crops, crafts, gods); jokes that work from inside their life. See `cefr_register_<LANG>.md` → *Period-neutral English*.

### 12. Morality and sentiment
- **Leak:** modern attitudes to animals (pets as family in cultures without that idea; vegetarian ethics), to nature (nature as fragile, "the planet"), to violence (shock at practices that were normal), to death (death as unusual). Equally: modern cynicism ("Image matters more than reality"; "Truth is what powerful men say it is") presented as worldly wisdom.
- **Period-true:** their ethics — sometimes harsh, sometimes tender, often surprising. Characters can be shocked, kind, or cruel, but for their own reasons.

## How to write the different mind well

1. **Start from what they took for granted.** Before drafting, list five things the protagonist would never question (the gods of this place, the rank of their family, the duty to the dead, the meaning of a bird's flight, the rightness of the king's share). Let the story rest on them.
2. **Let belief do work.** A ritual, an omen, a curse, a vow should change what characters do. Belief is a motor of plot, not decoration.
3. **Give them their own intelligence.** Characters are shrewd in their own terms: they read weather, animals, faces, debts, the moods of officials. Cleverness is shown through the period's tools.
4. **Find period-true conflict.** Most good stories in this line turn on conflicts the period itself recognised: debts, inheritance, marriage alliances, boundary stones, shares of a harvest, pollution and purification, a broken oath, an angry god, a stranger's arrival, a lost animal, a theft, a false weight.
5. **Translate feelings into the period's body and words.** "His liver burned" may be too strange for a B1 reader; "His face grew hot and he could not speak" is universal and period-safe. Do not modernise feeling — just show it.
6. **Keep the narrator inside.** The narrator knows only what a person of that world could know, plus the immediate physical scene. No narrator commentary on the period from outside. No foresight. No irony at the characters' expense for not knowing what we know.
7. **Let the Note do the outside work.** Where a modern reader needs context (why a woman could not testify; what the curse tablet was), the Historical Note gives it, plainly and briefly.

## Tests for auditors

- **The consultant test.** Could this line be spoken in a modern office, therapy session, or political rally without sounding odd? If yes, it is probably a leak.
- **The costume test.** Remove names and objects. Does the character still think like a person of that world, or like a modern person?
- **The hindsight test.** Does anyone — character or narrator — know or hint at what history will bring?
- **The sneer test.** Does the text treat the period's beliefs as foolish, or its people as primitive?
- **The sermon test.** Does any passage explain the moral of the story or of history?

## Severity guidance

- **Critical:** a premise or resolution that only works with a modern mind (the plot depends on a modern value, institution, or knowledge); narrator foresight of later history in the story text; a sermon ending.
- **Major:** a character's speech or thought built on a modern concept (rights rhetoric, management language, therapy-speak, secular debunking); modern idiom in narration; a sneer at belief; repeated minor leaks in one story.
- **Minor:** an isolated modern-tinged word that does not carry a concept ("focus", "okay-ish" phrasing) — fix in revision.
- **Nit:** borderline word choice that a careful editor would vary.

The mentalité gate in every audit is **zero Critical, zero Major**.
