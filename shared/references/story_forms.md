# Story Forms — Genre Palette, Shapes, Openings, Endings, Links, Plausibility

Read before designing slates and story cards (series), before frameworks (book), and before every variety or plausibility audit.

The Living History line will hold thousands of stories. The reader must never be able to guess the next one. Variety is not decoration; it is the reason a native reader keeps going after the third story. The previous generation of this kind of book collapsed into one template — *wake before dawn, impossible order from the palace, midday crisis, clever fix, praise, knowledge passed on, "tomorrow there would be new challenges, but tonight…"* — and its best moments came precisely where it broke the template: an intrigue in a treasury, sabotage in a chariot workshop, a merchant's dangerous road, a city falling in the night. Those plots were welcome. What weakened them was contrivance. This document gives the palette and the contract that keeps plotted stories plausible.

---

## 1. The form palette

Twenty forms. Each story has one primary form (and may borrow a flavour from a second).

### Quiet and human forms
1. **Craft and mastery.** A thing must be made, grown, mended, or saved; skill meets material, and something is at stake beyond the object. *Watch for:* the deadline-from-authority template; the triumph-and-praise ending.
2. **Rite of passage.** A first time: first harvest, first voyage, initiation, first blood, first festival role, marriage, a funeral one must lead.
3. **Family drama.** Inheritance, dowry, adoption, a son who will not take up the trade, a second wife, siblings, a parent's debt, an aged parent.
4. **Courtship and love.** Love inside the period's structures of marriage, honour, and kin; longing, arrangement, elopement, a forbidden match, a late love.
5. **Friendship and rivalry.** Two people bound or set against each other: apprentices, neighbours, athletes, singers, co-wives, ship-mates.
6. **The stranger.** A foreigner, captive, envoy, trader, refugee, or returning exile; the meeting of two worlds, seen from one side.
7. **Departure and homecoming.** Leaving forever, coming back changed, finding home changed.
8. **Loss and mourning.** A death, its rites, and the living who must go on. Shown through ritual and body, not through talk about grief.
9. **Comedy and trickster.** A ruse, a misunderstanding, a boast punctured, a clever servant, a festival gone wrong — humour that works from inside the period's life.
10. **The choice.** A moral dilemma with no clean answer, in the period's terms: duty to kin against duty to the god; an oath against a friend; mercy against law.

### Plotted forms
11. **Mystery.** Something has happened — a death, a disappearance, a theft, an omen — and someone must find out who or why, using the means of the time. Fair to the reader.
12. **Theft and the small heist.** A theft committed, planned, prevented, or uncovered: grain from a storeroom, silver from a shrine, a sacred object, false weights, a forged seal.
13. **Intrigue and deception.** A lie, a hidden plan, a rivalry inside a household, a workshop, a village, a sanctuary, a palace seen from below. The scale fits an ordinary person.
14. **Dispute and judgement.** A quarrel brought before elders, a council, a judge, an oracle, a god: boundary stones, debts, inheritance, an accusation of sorcery, a broken contract.
15. **Journey and adventure.** Road, river, sea, mountain pass, caravan, pilgrimage; weather, beasts, bandits, pirates, strangers, the unknown.
16. **Survival and disaster.** Storm, earthquake, flood, fire, famine, epidemic, raid, siege — and the hours in which ordinary people decide what to save.
17. **Chase and escape.** Flight from raiders, a runaway, a pursuit, a hiding, a race against time.
18. **Contest.** Games, races, singing or poetry contests, festival competitions, wagers — the contest is the frame; the person is the story.

### Framing forms
19. **The uncanny.** A ghost, a curse, an omen, a dream, a god's visit — told entirely from inside the culture's beliefs and never explained away.
20. **The told tale and the document.** An elder tells, a letter is written, a testimony is given, a prayer is spoken — the story reaches us through a voice inside the world. Use where the culture had such voices (letters only where writing existed).

## 2. Shapes

A form says *what kind of story*; a shape says *how it moves*. Each story declares one.

- **Single day.** Dawn to night, one day that changes something. (Strong, but the default trap — limit it.)
- **Countdown.** Hours or days to a fixed event: a festival, a sailing, a judgement, a birth.
- **Journey.** From A to B; each stage a test.
- **Investigation.** A question, a trail of clues, an answer.
- **Reversal.** The situation turns upside down at the midpoint.
- **Circle.** The ending returns to the opening image, changed.
- **Single scene.** One continuous scene in nearly real time: a trial, a meal, a night watch, a birth.
- **Seasons.** One year or several months in four or five moments.
- **Then and now.** A present moment opening into one remembered event and back.
- **Told tale / document.** A framing voice: a story told at a hearth, a letter, an oath, a testimony.

## 3. Openings

The first sentence does work: it raises a question, places us in a specific body, or shows a specific thing. Opening devices:

- An action already under way.
- A line of dialogue.
- A striking object.
- A sound or smell.
- A problem stated plainly.
- A rule or custom, as the character knows it.
- A place at a precise moment (not a weather report).
- A character's odd habit or gesture.
- A question the character asks themselves.
- A first line of a told tale or document.

Orientation (who, where, roughly when, what world) must be clear within the first 150–200 words — through concrete detail, not an information paragraph.

## 4. Endings

The last paragraph lands on an image, an act, or a line of dialogue — never on a moral, a summary, or a reflection about generations. Ending types:

- **Quiet image.** An object, a gesture, a light, a sound that holds the story.
- **Decision.** The character chooses; we see the first step.
- **Reversal.** The final turn changes what we understood.
- **Gain with a cost.** Something won, something paid.
- **Loss accepted.** Something lost, and life going on.
- **Comic payoff.** The joke lands.
- **Open question.** The story stops before the answer — deliberately, not by accident.
- **Circle.** The opening returns, changed.
- **Revelation.** A truth comes out, quietly.
- **Departure.** Someone leaves, and the door stays open.

Banned codas (see `anti_formula.md`): "tomorrow there would be new challenges… but tonight…"; the chain of generations; the knowledge-passed-on summary; narrator foresight ("none of them knew…"); the moral of the story; praise from authority as the final note.

## 5. Links between stories

Light links reward the reader of the whole book and must be invisible to the reader of one story. Permitted links:

- **Shared place:** the same harbour, sanctuary, village, or street.
- **Travelling object:** a jar, a seal, a cloak, a song that appears in two stories.
- **Glimpsed character:** a secondary character of one story passes through another, unnamed or briefly named, with no need for introduction.
- **Two sides of one event:** a festival, a storm, a raid seen from different stories.

Rules: each link is optional to understanding; no story spoils another; no link requires the reader to remember earlier details; a book uses links in at most four stories; links are recorded in the framework.

## 6. The plausibility contract for plotted stories

Plotted forms (11–18) are welcome. They must not be contrived. Every plotted story card and framework states how it meets each clause; auditors check each clause.

1. **Rooted in period reality.** The trouble is a kind of trouble the record shows for this world: thefts from storerooms or shrines, false weights, forged or broken seals, boundary disputes, debts and debt bondage, piracy and raiding, runaway slaves, accusations of sorcery or poisoning, adultery and honour, inheritance quarrels, disputed sacrifices, disasters. The card names this **realism anchor** and its evidence.
2. **Scale fits the protagonist.** Ordinary people meet local trouble. State conspiracies, royal assassinations, spy networks, and battles are almost never plausible as an ordinary person's plot — they may appear only seen from far below (a servant who overhears, a soldier in the ranks, a family hiding in the storeroom). At most one story per book touches elite-level intrigue, and then from below.
3. **Period motives.** Hunger, debt, honour and shame, revenge, inheritance, marriage, fear of the gods, loyalty to a house, ambition within one's rank. Not modern motives (career, ideology, self-realisation).
4. **Period means — of discovery and of resolution.** Witnesses; oaths sworn before gods; seals and tallies; records where writing existed; weights and measures; knowledge of craft, animals, weather, tracks; the fear of divine punishment; divination as social pressure; ordeal where attested; the judgement of elders, a council, a lord. No forensic science, no detective profession where none existed, no CSI logic.
5. **Coincidence budget: one.** At most one coincidence per story, and only to create trouble — never to solve it.
6. **Proportionate consequences.** Punishments, rewards, and outcomes follow period norms. Victories cost something. No "payment doubled", "official recognition", or happy-ever-after by decree as an automatic reward.
7. **Authorities act like authorities.** The powerful do not explain themselves to commoners, do not visit workshops at night for heart-to-heart talks, do not reason like modern managers, and do not change their minds because a commoner made a good speech.
8. **No speech solves everything.** Climaxes are actions, tests, choices, objects, or silences — not persuasive modern-sounding speeches.
9. **Antagonists are people.** Their motives are understandable in the period's terms. No villain monologues, no cynical one-liners about power.
10. **Fair play.** In mysteries and intrigues, every clue the solution depends on is on the page before the solution.
11. **Violence mostly off the page.** Deaths may happen; no gore, no torture shown; see `respect_and_darkness.md`.

## 7. The variety contract — quotas for one book of ten stories

The framework and the audits check these numbers. A slate or framework that breaks one is a Major finding; breaking three or more is Critical.

**Forms**
- At least 6 different forms; no form more than twice.
- At least 3 plotted forms (11–18) and at least 3 quiet forms (1–10).
- At most 1 story touching elite-level intrigue (seen from below).
- At most 2 "craft and mastery" stories.

**Shapes**
- At least 4 different shapes; "single day" at most 3 times.

**Openings and endings**
- At least 7 different opening devices across the ten stories; at most 1 story opens with waking, dawn, or "before the sun rose".
- At least 5 different ending types; no type more than twice; at most 1 ending in which an authority praises or rewards the protagonist.

**People**
- At least 3 female and 3 male protagonists (where the evidence allows; the bible records exceptions).
- At least 1 child or young adolescent protagonist and at least 1 old protagonist.
- At least 2 unfree or dependent protagonists where the society had slavery, serfdom, or bondage.
- At most 2 elite protagonists; the majority are ordinary.
- At least 1 outsider (foreigner, captive, migrant, returning exile) where plausible.
- No occupation category (e.g., potters/smiths/weavers as "craftspeople"; priests; traders; farmers; herders; soldiers) more than twice; craftspeople of any kind at most 3.
- A mentor–apprentice relationship central to at most 2 stories.

**Premises and settings**
- The premise "an authority demands an impossible task by a deadline" at most once per book.
- At least 4 setting types (palace or temple, town, village and fields, sea or river, road or mountain, household, workshop, sanctuary, wild land).
- At least 3 seasons.

**Narration**
- Default: past tense, third person, one point-of-view character per story.
- First person at most 2 stories per book; present tense at most 1.

## 8. Series-level variety

Across a series, the slate also checks:

- No two stories in the series share the combination *protagonist occupation + primary form + central object or event*.
- Every form in the palette appears at least once across any run of three consecutive books, unless the bible excludes it for this civilisation (with reason).
- No more than one story per series built on the same famous artefact or site (one Dolphin Fresco story, not three).
- The line registry (if supplied) is checked so that the most common concepts across civilisations ("the potter's workshop", "the harvest", "the market day") are not repeated in the same shape everywhere: if a series uses one, it must use a different form or shape from the last three series that did.
