# Historical Note Craft

Read before planning a Note (book framework), before writing one (book draft), and before fact-checking or auditing one. The Note is where the line keeps its promise of honesty. It is also the most dangerous place in the book: a false sentence in a story is a mistake inside fiction; a false sentence in a Note is a lie told as history.

---

## What a Note is for

The reader has just finished a story and wants to know how much of it was real. The Note answers in 120–180 words (band-adjusted per `book_format.md`), in plain language, at the story's level or slightly above it (B2 for a B1–B2 book).

A good Note does four things:

1. **Names the evidence** the story grew from — the kind of source we actually have — and, where useful, how far that evidence is from the story's date.
2. **Says what is known**, plainly, without superlatives.
3. **Says what is uncertain or debated**, honestly, without hedging every sentence.
4. **Says what was invented** when the invention is central to the story (a custom, an event, a technique, a festival, a law). Minor invented details need no comment.

It may end on one concrete, checked detail that gives the reader something to carry beyond the book.

## What a Note is not

- Not a summary of the story.
- Not a lecture on what the period teaches us.
- Not a list of superlatives or "firsts".
- Not academic prose: no jargon without a plain explanation, no footnotes, no citations in the text.
- Not a place to smuggle in facts the story got wrong. If the story is wrong, fix the story.
- Not a place for speculation stated as fact.

## Shape

There is no fixed template — ten identical Notes are another formula. A reliable order is: the evidence → what it tells us → what remains uncertain → what the story invented (if central) → an optional concrete echo. Vary the openings across a book.

## Language

- Length within the band's budget; never more than 200 words.
- Average sentence 14–18 words; nothing over 30.
- Period terms: only those already met in the story, plus at most two new ones, each explained in a few plain words.
- Dates in the series' chosen format; centuries written as words.
- Modern place names and scholarly period labels may be used in Notes to help the reader — never in the stories.

## Checking

Every sentence of a Note is a claim (`claim_taxonomy.md`). The fact-check must find each one VERIFIED, or fixed, or honestly framed as uncertain. Any OVERSTATED or CONTRADICTED sentence in a Note is **Critical**. A Note is written from the book's verified dossier and the framework's Note plan, never from memory.
