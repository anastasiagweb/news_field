# Newton's Graft at Woolsthorpe

Every family in Lincolnshire with a decent sideboard owns a piece of Newton's apple tree, in the same way that every church in medieval Europe owned a piece of the True Cross, and for much the same reasons. Professor Daniel Okafor, who held a chair in the history of physics at Trinity College, Cambridge, and who had been sent photographs of eleven such pieces in the past six years, had developed a short and courteous letter in reply. It thanked the owner for their interest, observed that the tree at Woolsthorpe Manor had blown down in about 1816 and had shown a commendable reluctance to stay down — rerooting from its fallen trunk and growing, rather crookedly, into the tree that visitors photograph today — and suggested, with regret, that the timber in question was therefore most likely to be pear, or elm, or wishful thinking.

The twelfth piece he went to see, because of the writing on it.

---

It belonged to a Mrs Thursby, a retired schoolteacher in Grantham, who kept it in a drawer in her dining room wrapped in a pillowcase. It was a quadrant rule — the long, flat, graduated arm of an astronomical quadrant, about eighteen inches long, in a close-grained, honey-coloured wood that had darkened at the edges to the brown of strong tea. It was finely made. Along one face ran a scale of degrees in tiny, precise, hand-cut divisions; along the other was a line of writing, burned into the wood with a hot point.

*Cut from ye old tree at Wolstrope by leave of Mr Newton, 1721. R.T.*

"Richard Thursby," said Mrs Thursby. "He was a carpenter. We've always said he made instruments for Mr Newton's family, though I'm sure I don't know if it's true. My grandmother used to say Sir Isaac sent him the branch himself."

Okafor turned the rule over in his hands. In 1721 Newton had been seventy-eight, President of the Royal Society, Master of the Mint, and living in London; but the manor at Woolsthorpe was still his, and still let to tenants, and there was nothing absurd in the idea that a dead branch had been cut from an old tree in the garden there, with the owner's permission, by a craftsman who knew what he was cutting. The date was before the fall. The wood was apple — he was nearly sure of it, and a friend at Kew confirmed it a fortnight later from a shaving the size of a fingernail. And the inscription, as far as he could tell, was as old as the scale.

None of this proved anything, of course. It merely failed to disprove it. Okafor had spent thirty years explaining to undergraduates that these are not the same thing, and he found it unexpectedly hard to remember the difference now.

So he did what he had always told his students to do, and went looking for a reason to say no. He spent a week in the county record office at Lincoln, among parish registers and account books that smelled of mice. He found a Richard Thursby, joiner, baptised at Colsterworth in 1689, which is the parish of Woolsthorpe. He found him paid four shillings in 1719 for *mending ye stair* at the manor. He did not find a reason to say no, and he began, against every instinct of his profession, to hope.

When he went back to Grantham, it was to ask a favour. "May I borrow it?" he said.

---

It was the scale that kept him awake.

The rule was graduated in degrees from nought to ninety, as a quadrant should be. But along its outer edge, almost invisible, were five extra marks — tiny notches, cut more deeply than the rest and unevenly spaced, like the marks a man makes on a doorframe to measure a child. They corresponded to no star and no ordinary angle that Okafor could think of. He measured them, and measured them again, and wrote the numbers on a card, and propped the card against his kettle, where it stayed for a month.

It was his wife who said, looking at it over breakfast, "That looks like an apple."

He said it did not look like anything.

"Not the numbers. The way you've drawn them." She took the pencil and drew five lines from a point, at the angles he had written down. "Cut an apple across the middle," she said, "and look."

He went to the market that afternoon and bought, from a stall that specialised in old varieties, half a dozen Flower of Kent — the cooking apple that grows at Woolsthorpe, a big, lumpy, greenish, pear-shaped fruit that tastes, raw, of very little, and that the stallholder sold him with a look of mild pity. He took them back to his rooms in Trinity and cut them across the equator, one after another, with a bread knife on his desk.

Every apple has its seeds in five chambers, arranged in a star; every child who has ever cut an apple sideways knows that. What Okafor had not known — what he doubted anyone had ever bothered to measure — was that in the Flower of Kent, with its lopsided, pear-like shape, the star is not quite regular. The five chambers are pushed slightly out of true by the shape of the fruit, so that the angles between them are not seventy-two degrees each, as in a perfect star, but something a little more and something a little less, in a pattern that repeated, near enough, in every apple on the desk.

He laid the rule beside the third apple and looked at the five notches for a long time.

---

What came next took him most of a year, and occupied a great many more pages than this story has room for, and I give it to you as he gave it to me, at dinner, over a very good Burgundy, with the warning that it may be the most beautiful nonsense in the history of science. I do not vouch for it. Neither, if he was honest, did he.

Newton, in the last decades of his life, spent more time on ancient chronology than he ever spent on gravity. He wanted to fix the dates of the ancient kingdoms — Egypt, Assyria, Greece — by astronomy, using the slow wheel of the equinoxes against the stars to correct the boastful lists of kings; and he left behind him, among his papers, hundreds of pages of tables and calculations, some of which he published and some of which he very definitely did not. Among the ones he did not, in a manuscript now in a library in Jerusalem, there is a table of numbers that has never made sense to anyone: columns of figures with no heading, in an order that seems to follow no rule.

Okafor read them, column by column, through the angles of the apple.

It is very hard to explain, he said, without a blackboard. But if you take the five irregular angles of the star as a key — as a kind of turning wheel, the way a cipher wheel turns — the columns fall into order. They become dates. A second chronology of the ancient world, quieter and stranger than the published one, which Newton seems to have kept to himself because it did not agree with Scripture, and Newton never willingly disagreed with Scripture in public.

"It's a game," Okafor said to me. "It must be. Nobody would build a cipher on the inside of an apple."

"Newton might," I said.

"Newton might," he agreed. "That's the trouble."

---

He has not published it. He says he will, when he is sure, which is a phrase physicists use when they mean *never*. The quadrant rule has gone back to Mrs Thursby, who keeps it in the same drawer in the same pillowcase, and who has, he tells me, been offered a quite remarkable sum for it by a man from Texas, and refused.

The tree at Woolsthorpe is still there, crooked and propped, putting out each autumn its crop of large, plain, unremarkable apples. Visitors photograph it from the path. They are told — accurately, so far as anyone knows — that it is the tree which gave a young man an idea about the moon.

Nobody tells them it may have given an old man somewhere to keep a secret. Nobody, as far as I know, has ever asked the tree.
