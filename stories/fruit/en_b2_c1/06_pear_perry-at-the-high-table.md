# Perry at the High Table

Kerne College stands in the Leadon valley in Gloucestershire, three miles from Newent, in the kind of countryside that the English are always describing as unspoilt because nobody has yet found a reason to spoil it. It was founded in 1784 by Sir Richard Kerne, a West India merchant with a bad conscience and a good library, *for the education of twelve poor scholars in Divinity, Mathematicks, and the Husbandry of Fruit*; and although the poor scholars are now forty and mostly study economics, the College has never quite let go of the fruit. Its orchards run down to the river on three sides. Its arms show three pears on a field of green. And at its Founder's Feast, every November, it serves at High Table a perry made from its own trees — Blakeney Reds, a hundred years old and more, standing in the long grass below the chapel like a congregation of bent old men who have forgotten why they came.

It was at the Founder's Feast, therefore, between the pheasant and the pudding, that the Dean chose to deal with Mr Lionel Kerne.

---

Mr Lionel Kerne was not, strictly speaking, a member of the College, though he had spent eighteen months behaving as if he owned it, which he hoped shortly to do. He was a distant collateral descendant of the Founder — a great-great-great-great-nephew, the College genealogist had calculated, with a certain amount of distaste — and he had arrived the previous spring with a solicitor, a leather case, and a letter.

The letter, he said, had been found among family papers in a house in Cheltenham. It was written in a fine eighteenth-century hand on paper that had been examined and found to be of the right age, and it was addressed by Sir Richard Kerne to his brother in 1791. In it, Sir Richard described the delight of having entertained his first scholars in the orchard that summer, and his determination that the College lands — all of them, orchards and farms and river meadows — should revert to his brother's heirs *should the College ever cease to keep the Feast in the manner I have ordained*. And the manner he had ordained, the letter went on, was the manner of that very summer afternoon: *the scholars seated in the grass beneath my old Barland, and each of them with a fresh pear from it in his hand, sweet as honey, so that they might taste the fruits of learning, as I did at their age.*

The College had kept the Feast for two hundred and forty years. It had never, at any point, handed its scholars a fresh pear. Mr Lionel Kerne's solicitor was of the opinion that the lands were therefore his client's, and had written to the College at length to say so.

"The letter's genuine," the Bursar said miserably, in the Senior Common Room, in June. "The paper's genuine. The ink's genuine. Our lawyers say so, his lawyers say so, a man from the British Library says so. And Sir Richard did have a Barland. There's one in the orchard still, behind the chapel, that's supposed to be a grafted descendant of it."

"Yes," said the Dean. "I know."

The Dean was a mild, round, white-haired woman called Dr Ruth Ashby, who had read Classics at Somerville in the sixties and had been at Kerne for so long that undergraduates assumed she had come with the buildings. She had a passion for the Founder's memoirs, which ran to three volumes in manuscript and had never been published, chiefly because they were almost entirely about fruit.

"I'll need until November," she said.

---

The Founder's Feast was a grand affair. The Hall was lit with candles, the portraits looked down on the long tables with eighteenth-century reserve, and at High Table the Master, the Fellows and their guests — among them, at the Dean's particular request, Mr Lionel Kerne and his solicitor — sat beneath the Founder himself, painted by Romney in a blue coat, holding, as it happens, a pear.

The perry came in with the pheasant, in tall glasses. It was pale gold and faintly sparkling, with a scent of wet orchards and honey, and it was, Mr Kerne admitted, very fine.

"It's made from our Blakeney Reds," said the Dean, who was sitting beside him. "And a few Barlands, for backbone. They're perry pears, you know. Quite different from the pears in the shops. My great passion, I'm afraid. I'd so like you to try one."

And she nodded to the butler, who set before Mr Kerne a small silver plate. On the plate was a single pear: small, rough-skinned, a dull greenish-russet, picked that afternoon from the old Barland behind the chapel.

"Fresh from the tree," said the Dean. "The way your ancestor gave them to his scholars."

There was a certain amount of quiet along High Table, which Mr Kerne, to his credit, did not appear to notice. He picked up the pear, smiled at the Dean, and — because it was a fresh pear, from his ancestor's tree, and there were people watching — bit into it.

---

The Dean had seen many faces in her life. She had seen undergraduates told that they had failed their finals and Fellows told that they had not been elected Master. She had never, she said afterwards, seen anything quite like Mr Lionel Kerne in the four seconds after that bite.

His mouth stopped. Then it puckered, slowly and completely, inward, as if a drawstring had been pulled tight behind his lips. His eyes filled. His cheeks hollowed. The fruit in his mouth was hard, and gritty — as gritty as if he had bitten a mouthful of wet sand — and over the grit came a dryness so total and so violent that his tongue seemed to stick to the roof of his mouth and his teeth to his tongue. He reached for his glass, and found that he could not quite close his hand on it.

"Tannin," said the Dean kindly, passing him the water. "Perry pears are full of it — far more than any eating pear. It's what makes perry keep, and what gives it that lovely dry finish. And the grittiness is stone cells. *Sclereids*, the botanists call them. Little hard knots of wood all through the flesh, so tough you can feel them between your teeth. A perry pear is barely a fruit at all, when it's fresh. It's a sort of raw material. For three hundred years in this county nobody has ever eaten a Barland off the tree, Mr Kerne, except as a dare. You put them through a stone mill and press them and wait."

Mr Kerne made a noise, which might have been agreement.

"The Founder knew that perfectly well," the Dean went on, "because he says so. Volume two of the memoirs, page three hundred and eleven. He gave his first scholars a picnic in the orchard in the summer of 1791, exactly as your letter says. It was a very happy afternoon; he describes it with great feeling. They had cold mutton and bread and a cask of his perry from the year before, and he showed them the old Barland, and he let each boy try to bite a pear from it, and he laughed so much at their faces that he had to sit down in the grass." She smiled. "*The fruits of learning*, he writes. *Bitter in the mouth, and sweet only after long pressing.* It's one of his favourite jokes. He makes it six times in three volumes."

---

There was a little more, in the weeks that followed, about who had written the letter and when — a clever Victorian great-uncle, it eventually appeared, with a grievance and a good hand, who had known about the picnic from family stories and had never in his life been to Gloucestershire to taste the pears. But nobody at Kerne ever paid much attention to that part. The case was withdrawn before Christmas, quietly, by a solicitor who had begun to find the whole thing embarrassing.

Mr Lionel Kerne left Gloucestershire and did not come back. He did write to the Dean once, a short and not ungracious letter, to say that he hoped there were no hard feelings.

The Dean wrote back by return. She enclosed, with her best wishes, a bottle of the College perry.

It was, she said, the same fruit. It had simply been given time.
