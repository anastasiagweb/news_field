# The Porcelain Peach of Chelsea

The private view at Fennimore's was held, as it always was, on the evening before the sale, in the long upstairs room in Albemarle Street with the good carpet and the bad sherry, and it was attended, as it always was, by the same forty people, every one of whom was pretending not to want the lot that every other one of them had come to see.

Lot 112 stood alone in a glass case at the end of the room. It was a tea caddy of Chelsea porcelain, made in about 1755, in the shape of a peach.

It was not large — it would have sat comfortably in two cupped hands — and it was not, to the untrained eye, especially grand. But to the forty people in the room it was something close to a miracle. The Chelsea factory in the years of the Red Anchor, when its mark was a tiny anchor painted in red on the base, made some of the loveliest soft-paste porcelain in Europe: plates shaped like leaves, tureens shaped like rabbits and cauliflowers, scent bottles shaped like everything; and among them, it was known from the old sale catalogues, a few tea caddies shaped like fruit. This was the only peach known to survive. It was creamy white, flushed on one cheek with a rose-pink so delicate it seemed to have been breathed on rather than painted, and it lay on a little bed of green leaves modelled in relief, each vein picked out with care. The lid was the top of the fruit, and its handle was the stalk — a small twisted twig of brown, with a single leaf curling from it.

Dr Clare Whitlock, who was a curator of ceramics at the Victoria and Albert Museum and had come, officially, to look at something else, stood in front of it for twenty minutes.

---

"Exquisite," said a voice beside her. "Such a pity about the lid."

Marcus Dene was a dealer in Bond Street, and a good one, and Clare had known him for fifteen years in the way one knows a wasp at a picnic. He was smiling at the caddy with an expression of tender regret.

"The lid?" said Clare.

"Broken and mended. Didn't you know? I had it out of the case this morning. There's a glued seam right round the stalk. Old repair — someone dropped it, I should think, a couple of hundred years ago, and stuck it back with whatever they had. It'll knock a third off the price." He sighed. "Of course, Fennimore's haven't put it in the catalogue. They never do. I've mentioned it to one or two people, just so nobody's disappointed."

By the end of the evening, Clare reflected, he would have mentioned it to everyone. And by the morning the price would have fallen, and Marcus Dene, who did not care in the least about mended lids, would buy the peach for a great deal less than it was worth and sell it in a year to an American for a great deal more.

"May I see it?" she said to the porter.

---

They took it into a small back office for her, with a desk lamp and a green blotter, because Fennimore's had known Clare for a long time and the V&A is the V&A. She looked at the lid first in daylight, through a glass, and Marcus was right: around the base of the porcelain stalk, where it met the top of the lid, there was a seam — very fine, very neat, the colour of weak tea. It was the sort of repair that every eighteenth-century household with a clumsy maid knew how to make.

Then she took from her bag a small ultraviolet torch, of the kind that every curator carries and every forger fears, and switched off the lamp.

Under ultraviolet light, porcelain glows. Different glazes give a different colour, and so do repairs, and an expert who knows what she is looking for can read the history of an object in the dark like a page of print. Modern glues show up blue-white, or barely at all. The seam round the stalk of the Chelsea peach lit up a bright, warm, unmistakable orange.

Shellac.

It was the glue of the eighteenth century — a resin, brought from India, melted and run into a crack while warm. It was what you would expect in an old repair. But Clare looked at it for a long time in the purple dark, and what she noticed was not the colour of the line but its shape. It went all the way round the stalk in a perfect, even ring. There was no crack running off it into the lid, no chip, no missing flake of glaze. Things that are dropped do not break like that.

Things that are opened do.

---

She bought the peach the next morning, for the Museum, for rather less than it was worth, because Marcus Dene had done his work well and nobody else in the room wished to pay full price for a mended lid. Marcus himself bid twice and then stopped, with a small, puzzled frown, when he saw who was bidding against him. He had wanted the peach; he had not wanted the V&A to want it. It is very difficult, in the trade, to know which kind of wanting is more dangerous.

It was three weeks before the conservators were ready. They worked in the studio on the fourth floor of the Museum, under north light, with the peach in a padded cradle. Shellac softens with gentle heat, and they warmed it very slowly, with a small heated spatula and great patience, a millimetre at a time, until at last, late on a Friday afternoon, the stalk moved.

It came away in Clare's fingers with a soft click, like a door being unlocked after a long time. Beneath it, in the top of the lid, was a little round hollow, much deeper than the stalk could ever have needed; and in the hollow, wrapped in a scrap of faded silk, was a disc of ivory no bigger than a coin.

She unwrapped it on the bench.

It was a miniature portrait, painted in watercolour on the ivory with that astonishing fineness which the miniaturists of the eighteenth century took for granted and nobody since has quite managed. It showed a young man, fair-haired, in a powdered wig and a blue sash, with a star on his breast and a soft, rather weak, rather beautiful face; a face that had been painted, copied and engraved in its own time a thousand times over, and that every Scottish household in the 1740s either hated or loved; and that it had been, for a good many years after 1746, distinctly dangerous to own.

"Charles Edward Stuart," said the senior conservator quietly. "Good heavens."

---

The Young Pretender had come to Scotland in 1745 to win back his grandfather's throne for his father, and had nearly done it, and had lost everything at Culloden the following spring. After that, to keep his portrait in one's house was to keep a small flame of treason, and the Hanoverian government did not care for flames. The sympathisers kept him anyway. They hid him in the backs of watches, on the bottoms of drinking glasses, under the lids of snuffboxes. And one of them, it seemed, a few years later, had sent to Chelsea for a porcelain peach, and had given his prince a home inside it, under a stalk sealed round with shellac. And there it had sat, on a tea table in some drawing room, in front of soldiers and magistrates and the vicar, being admired.

Clare thought about that owner for a long time. She thought of them pouring tea for their guests with the caddy at their elbow, and smiling at a compliment on the pretty fruit, and saying, perhaps, that it was from Chelsea, and that the stalk was a little loose.

---

The peach is on display now, in the British Galleries, with the miniature in a small case beside it, and a label of seventy words that took the Museum five weeks to agree.

Marcus Dene came to see it on the day it went on show. He stood in front of it for a long time, and then he found Clare in her office, and congratulated her, very handsomely, on her purchase.

"I always said that lid was interesting," he said.

"You did," said Clare. "You told everyone."
