# The Rubies of Kandahar

Sir Hector Ambrose had retired from the diplomatic service in 1919 with a knighthood, a liver, and a collection, and of the three it was the collection he talked about. He had served in Tehran, in Kabul, and for one unforgettable season in Herat, and he had come home to Chester Square with enough Persian miniatures, Afghan daggers and Mughal jade to furnish a museum, and enough opinions to furnish a second. His dinners were famous for two things: the excellence of his cook and the length of his after-dinner tours of the study, which his friends endured as the price of the cook.

The study was guarded. This was the first thing Sir Hector told new guests, and he told them with satisfaction. There were bars on the windows, a lock on the door from Chubb's that had been described to him as *unpickable by any gentleman*, and in the hall outside, on the nights when the collection was shown, a former sergeant of the Indian Army who sat on a hard chair and looked at people's hands.

"And this," said Sir Hector, on the evening in question, lifting the lid of a small ebony box with the air of a conjurer who has done the trick before and still enjoys it, "is the Kandahar Ruby."

It lay on black velvet, the size of a pigeon's egg, and it was very red.

There were nine at dinner. Three of them gasped, which was the correct number; a fourth, a young man from the Foreign Office who hoped to be posted to Kabul, said "Good Lord" with great feeling. Lady Vane, who was sitting nearest the box, leaned forward, looked at the stone for perhaps five seconds, and leaned back again.

"How lovely," she said. "What a pity it's a spinel."

---

Lady Vane was forty-four, a widow, and by common consent the most dangerous woman in Belgravia, though nobody could ever quite say why. She did not gamble wildly, or drink, or have lovers that anyone could name. She simply noticed things, and said them, in a voice so pleasant that one did not realise until the next morning that one had been dissected.

"A spinel," repeated Sir Hector.

"From Badakhshan, I should think. The mines up the Oxus, above Faizabad. They've been sending these stones down to India for a thousand years, and for most of that time everyone called them rubies, which is understandable. The Black Prince's Ruby in the Imperial State Crown is a spinel too, Sir Hector, so you're in the best of company. It's only in the last century or so that people have been able to tell the difference. A ruby is a little harder. The colour's a little different, if you hold it to the light — a touch of blue in a ruby, I always think; a spinel is more honest about being red."

Sir Hector had gone a colour of his own, which a jeweller might have described as *pigeon's blood*.

"My dear Lady Vane. I bought that stone from a dealer in the Kandahar bazaar who had it from the treasury of the Durrani kings."

"I'm sure you did. And I'm sure he believed it, too, at the price." She smiled at him with real kindness. "It's a beautiful thing, whatever it is. I shouldn't mind owning it myself."

It was then that Sir Hector made the mistake, and he made it, as such men always do, out of a desire to recover his dignity in front of the young man from the Foreign Office.

"You'd find that difficult," he said. "Nothing leaves this room unless I carry it out myself."

"Oh, I don't know," said Lady Vane.

"Fifty pounds says you couldn't. Not tonight. Not in front of witnesses."

"A hundred," said Lady Vane, "and the witnesses may choose themselves. Shall we say Mrs Fenwick, Colonel Dacre, and" — she looked at the young man — "Mr Pryor? He seems to have very good eyes."

---

Sir Hector, to be fair to him, did not cheat. He did not lock the stone away. He left it in the open box on the table by the window, where everyone could see it, and he made a little speech about it, and then — because it was his habit, and because nothing would have made him change his habits for a woman — he rang for dessert to be served in the study, among the treasures, as it always was.

It came in on a silver stand: grapes, a pineapple, and, in the middle, the pride of Sir Hector's glasshouse in Surrey, a pomegranate the size of a cricket ball, its skin the colour of an old saddle with a flush of rose where the sun had caught it. Sir Hector grew them from a cutting he had brought home from Herat in a biscuit tin, and he always explained this, at length.

"May I?" said Lady Vane, while he was explaining it, and took the fruit and the little silver knife together before anyone could object.

She was very good with a knife. She scored the skin in a ring around the crown, and then down the sides along the ridges, the way the women do in Persian kitchens, and broke the fruit open with her thumbs into two rough halves. The inside of a pomegranate is one of the more surprising sights that nature offers to a dinner table: not flesh at all, but a honeycomb of tight, glassy, blood-red seeds, each in its little sac of juice, packed into chambers and divided by pale, creamy-white walls as thin as tissue paper. The seeds catch the lamplight like — well, like rubies, as every Persian poet has observed, generally at too great a length.

"Do look," said Lady Vane, to the room, and the room looked.

What the room did not see — because the room, very naturally, was looking at the fruit — was the hand that had, a moment earlier, rested for no more than a second on the table beside the ebony box; or the small, deft movement with which a red stone wet with crimson juice was pressed deep into one of the inner chambers, between two white membranes, among the seeds it so closely resembled. A spinel, after all, is more honest about being red. It lay there in the glistening jelly like one more seed, a little larger than the rest.

"I never eat them," said Lady Vane, "they get into one's teeth. But Bertie adores them." She turned to the footman. "Would you be very kind, Harris, and take this half out to my car? My chauffeur knows. Bertie will be beside himself."

Harris, who was used to the habits of the rich, took the half-pomegranate on a plate and carried it out of the study, past the sergeant on the hard chair, who looked at Harris's hands and saw fruit, down the stairs, and out into Chester Square, where a grey Daimler stood at the kerb with its engine idling and, on the back seat, a small brown capuchin monkey in a red waistcoat sat waiting with its hands folded, like a bishop.

---

The stone was missed eleven minutes later, which Mrs Fenwick, who kept the time, said afterwards was a very respectable interval.

There was a scene, naturally. The sergeant was summoned and swore, quite correctly, that nothing had passed him but a footman with a plate. The ebony box was examined, the carpet was searched, and Colonel Dacre, who had been on the North-West Frontier as a subaltern and had never quite got over it, suggested that the women be searched too, and was told by Mrs Fenwick that he might search her personally if he wished to be shot.

Lady Vane sat through it all with her hands in her lap, and when it was over, and Sir Hector had sunk into a chair with the air of a man watching his own funeral, she rose and said she really must go.

"The stone," said Sir Hector hoarsely. "Where is it?"

"In my car, I imagine," said Lady Vane. "Unless Bertie has eaten it. He's a very thorough monkey."

---

It came back the next morning, by hand, in a small box from Asprey's, with Lady Vane's card and a note asking that her hundred pounds be paid, at Sir Hector's convenience, to the Zoological Society, for the improvement of the monkey house — which was, as Mr Pryor pointed out, a good deal more elegant than collecting it.

The stone had been washed. On the card she had written only one word, in a large clear hand, and Sir Hector, who kept the card for the rest of his life and showed it to nobody, could never decide whether it was an apology or a correction.

*Spinel.*
