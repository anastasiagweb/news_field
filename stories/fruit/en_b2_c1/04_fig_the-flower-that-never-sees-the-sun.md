# The Flower That Never Sees the Sun

The late Master of St Botolph's, Cambridge, had been a mathematician, which explained his will, and a practical joker, which explained everything else. He had left the bulk of his considerable fortune — the house in Grantchester, the shares in a brewery, the very good Turner watercolour of Ely in a storm — to *that Fellow of the College who shall bring before the Governing Body, at its first meeting of the Michaelmas Term following my death, a flower from the College garden on which no ray of English sun has ever fallen*. The money, failing such a Fellow, was to go to the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds, an organisation the Master had despised for forty years on account of its views about his cat.

"He meant it as a puzzle," said the Senior Tutor, when the will was read. "He was always setting puzzles."

"He meant it as a trap," said Professor Edmund Lyle, who was the College's botanist, and had been the Master's pupil, and had therefore spent the next nine months thinking about very little else. "He knew I'd try. He wanted to watch."

He did try. Nobody at St Botolph's ever disputed that Lyle tried.

---

The difficulty, as Lyle explained to anyone who would listen at dinner, was not in principle insurmountable. A flower needs light, generally speaking, to open; but a flower does not need light to *form*. The flowers of a tulip or a hyacinth are already folded up inside the bulb, a perfect miniature, months before the leaves show; that is what a bulb *is* — a flower waiting in its overcoat. Dig the bulb before it sprouts; take it straight down, in a black bag, to a black cellar; force it in the dark, with warmth and water, and the flower will rise from a bulb that has never in its life been touched by daylight.

"The bulb was touched by daylight last spring," said Dr Philip Cray, who was a historian of law and sat opposite Lyle at dinner, and had done so, with diminishing pleasure on both sides, for twenty-two years.

"The *bulb*," said Lyle, "is not the flower. The flower is a new thing. It did not exist last spring. Read the will, Cray. *A flower on which no ray has fallen.* Not a plant. Not a bulb. A flower."

"I have read the will," said Cray mildly. "I've read it rather more often than you, I suspect. I'm a lawyer."

"You're a historian of law."

"Which is a lawyer who has the decency to be dead," said Cray, and helped himself to the cheese.

---

Lyle's cellar was beneath the old buttery, and it was, by every account, a masterpiece of applied botany. He had blacked the grating with tar paper and laid three thicknesses of baize over the door. He had chosen hyacinths, after much thought, as the most willing to perform in the dark, and he had lifted the bulbs himself from the Fellows' Garden one February night with a lantern hooded down to a red pin of light, and carried them below in a hatbox. For warmth there was a paraffin stove; for water, a can that he filled by touch. He went down every evening after dinner and sat with them, in the perfect blackness, like a man at the bedside of a child.

They grew. That was the cruelty of it. They grew magnificently — upward, desperately upward, long pale stems reaching for a light that never came, until they leaned against the cellar wall like exhausted soldiers. And at the top of each stem, the flowers formed, and were small, and were a ghastly, bloodless white-green, and would not open. They waited. They had been designed by a million years of evolution to wait for the sun, and Lyle, who of all men knew it best, could not persuade them otherwise.

In the end he did what he had sworn not to do. He brought in a lamp — a weak electric bulb, behind a blue filter, which was not, he argued to himself, the *sun*, and certainly not the *English* sun — and in a fortnight the hyacinths had coloured, faintly, to the shade of a bruise, and opened, more or less.

"It's not sunlight," he said at dinner, a little feverishly. "It is not sunlight in any sense the testator could possibly have intended."

"I'm sure you're right," said Cray.

---

The Governing Body met in the Combination Room on the second Thursday of October, at half past two, under the portraits of eleven Masters and a painting of the College's founder that nobody had ever liked. There was a long table covered with green baize, a silver inkstand that no one had used since 1911, and, today, an unusually full attendance.

Lyle came in last. He had carried the hyacinth over from the buttery in a pot, wrapped in black cloth, and he set it on the baize and unwrapped it with the gravity of a man laying a wreath. It was very pale and rather crooked, and it smelled, very faintly, of paraffin.

The Senior Tutor said that it was a remarkable achievement. Several Fellows agreed. The Bursar, who was thinking about the Turner, said nothing.

"One small point," said Cray.

He did not stand. He did not need to.

"The meeting was called for half past two. The buttery is across Old Court. It is a bright afternoon — an unusually bright afternoon, for October. I happened to be at my window." He paused, courteously. "You crossed the court at twenty-five past, Lyle. The cloth slipped. I'm afraid the sun was on that flower for at least nine seconds before you noticed."

There was a silence in which several people heard the Combination Room clock for the first time in years.

"I grant you the lamp," said Cray, more gently. "A lamp in a cellar is not the English sun, and I should argue so myself, if I were acting for you. But Old Court is a different matter. Old Court has been receiving the English sun since 1350, whenever there was any to receive."

---

Lyle sat down. It was, people agreed afterwards, the only moment in twenty-two years at which anyone had felt sorry for him.

Then Cray took from his pocket a small green fig.

He had picked it that morning from the old tree in Chapel Court — the great sprawling fig against the south wall, older than the Chapel itself, which every Fellow walked past four times a day and none of them ever looked at. It was hard and green, the size of a walnut, with a tiny closed eye at its blunt end. He laid it on the baize, took out a pocket knife, and cut it cleanly in half, from stalk to eye.

"May I present," he said, "a flower from the College garden."

The Fellows leaned in. Most of them had eaten figs. None of them, it appeared, had ever looked at one.

"It's not a fruit at all, you see. Or not in the way one supposes. Lyle will correct me. It's a hollow stem, turned in on itself like a purse. And all over the inside of the purse — there, you can see them, those little pale threads, packed in tight like the pile of a carpet — are flowers. Hundreds of them. Tiny, perfect flowers that bloom inside the fig, in the dark, with the skin all round them and the little eye at the end sealed up with scales. They are born, they open, they set seed, if they're lucky, and they never see the sky. The botanists have a word for it."

"*Syconium*," said Lyle, from somewhere very far away.

"Thank you. A syconium. Every flower inside it has spent its entire existence in Chapel Court, in the College garden, in the darkest place there is. Until about forty seconds ago." Cray looked at the cut face of the fig, glistening faintly under the electric light of the Combination Room, which was on, because it was October and the Combination Room faces north. "And as you'll observe, it is now a quarter to three, and the sun is on the other side of the building."

---

The Governing Body, which is composed largely of lawyers and mathematicians and therefore enjoys nothing so much as a definition, took the matter under advisement and, in a meeting that lasted until nearly seven, found in Cray's favour by nineteen votes to four. The RSPB was not informed.

Cray spent the money with characteristic restraint. He gave the house in Grantchester to the College as a home for graduate students, sold the brewery shares, and kept the Turner, which he hung in his own rooms, where it can still be seen by anyone willing to listen to his explanation of the will.

The fig tree in Chapel Court has a small brass plaque now. It was Lyle who paid for it. It says only: *Ficus carica. It flowers in secret.*

Most people think that this is botany.
