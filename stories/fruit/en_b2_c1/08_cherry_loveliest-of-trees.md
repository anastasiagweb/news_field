# Loveliest of Trees

Book collectors, like fishermen, are divided into those who want to catch something and those who want to be seen catching it, and Julian Ferrers had always flattered himself that he belonged to the first kind. He had been buying books for thirty years, mostly in the back rooms of shops in Hay-on-Wye and Cecil Court, and he had a small, perfect, unfashionable collection of English poetry between 1890 and 1914 that he showed to almost nobody. It was a matter of some private irritation to him, therefore, that the most interesting book he ever bought came from an online auction, at four o'clock in the morning, after a bad dinner.

It was a first edition of *A Shropshire Lad* — Kegan Paul, 1896, one of the five hundred copies that Housman had paid to have printed and that had sold so slowly the publisher was glad to see the back of them. The binding was rubbed, the spine faded, the price modest. The description mentioned, in passing, *some loose MS leaves and pressed flowers laid in*. Julian bid without much hope and woke up to find he had won it, chiefly because the only other bidder, a dealer named Royce whom he had disliked for twenty years, had apparently gone to bed.

When the parcel came, he opened it at his desk, and the book fell open, as old books do, at the place where something had been kept. It was the second poem. *Loveliest of trees, the cherry now / Is hung with bloom along the bough.* And pressed flat across the page, brown now and thin as tissue, were two small clusters of cherry blossom, each a pair of flowers on joined stalks, laid side by side like two hands.

---

The manuscript leaves were six pages torn from a diary, written in pencil in a small, upright, rather beautiful hand. There was no name, no year. There were a great many entries about weather and walking. And there were three entries, on the last two pages, that Julian read so often in the following week that he could recite them.

*12th April. Up the Edge from Much Wenlock in a wind. The wild cherries out at last, the whole hanging wood dark and leafless and the cherries standing white in it like brides at a funeral. From the stile at the top one sees three, in a long bend, and no more. Those three are ours.*

*13th April. To the summer-house again with E. From its door the three cherries stand one behind another up the slope, so that you see only one, and the stile is due north, over the trees. The letters are safe. E. says no one will ever find them, and I said, Not unless they come in April.*

*14th April. E. read me his new poem, and I read him mine. We are neither of us as good as A.E.H., but we are rather better company.*

There was a cutting from a newspaper, too, folded in four, dated 1922: a notice of the death of a young man called Edmund Vayle, a poet of whom Julian had never heard, and of whom — after three days in the London Library and two at the Cambridge University Library — he could find almost nothing at all. A slim book of verse, privately printed, in 1913. A few letters in a box in the archive of Trinity College, Cambridge, where Housman had taught, written to a friend whose name had been carefully cut out of every page.

---

Royce rang him on the Thursday.

"You've got the Housman," he said. "The one with the flowers. I'll give you three times what you paid."

"Why?"

"Sentiment."

Julian had known Royce for twenty years and had never seen him exhibit sentiment for anything except a profit margin. "No," he said, and Royce rang off, and Julian sat for a long time looking at the receiver, and then went upstairs and packed a bag.

Royce, he learned later, had bought the other part of the diary at the same sale, under another name — fourteen pages, the earlier part of the same spring, with a rough sketch-map of Wenlock Edge and a little drawing of the summer-house: a square stone thing with a pointed roof, half lost in trees. He had gone to Shropshire the very next day, in the first week of March, and spent a fortnight walking up and down the Edge with his map, in the rain, and found nothing. The Edge is a long, steep, wooded ridge running for miles above the plain. It is full of old quarries, old paths, and old ruins, and every one of them is buried in trees.

Julian went in April. He had not been especially clever; he had simply read the entries more slowly.

---

Wild cherry is not much of a tree for most of the year. In summer it is merely green, among other green things; in winter it is grey and bare, among other grey, bare things; and a man looking for it on a wooded hillside in March would see nothing to tell it from the ash and oak and hazel crowding around it. But for perhaps ten days in April, before the woods come into leaf, the wild cherry flowers, and then it is unmistakable — a tall white cloud standing up out of the dark, leafless trees, visible for miles, so white that it seems to give off light. Housman had seen it. *Wearing white for Eastertide.* So had the diarist. *Three, in a long bend, and no more.*

He found the stile on the second day, at the top of the old path from Much Wenlock. He stood on its step in a cold wind, with the whole of the hanging wood falling away below him, brown and grey and purple, still wintry; and in it, in a long bend down the slope, as if someone had placed them there on purpose, stood three white trees.

The rest was simple. He was not a surveyor, but he had a compass, and the diary, and a lifetime's habit of patience. From the stile he took a bearing due south, down into the wood. Then he walked down to the three trees and sighted along them, from the highest to the lowest, until they stood one behind another and became a single white tree, and he followed that line down the slope with his compass in his hand. On the second evening, with the light going and his boots full of mud, he found the place where the two lines met. It was a hollow on the slope, thick with brambles and holly, and in the middle of it, so overgrown that he had walked past it twice that afternoon, stood four walls of grey stone and the stump of a pointed roof.

The summer-house was nearly a ruin. Ivy had come through the window and a young ash had come through the floor. But the stone seat along the back wall was intact, and under it, when Julian lay on his back in the leaves with a torch, he found a stone that was not quite like the others, and behind the stone, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin that had once held biscuits, a bundle of letters, tied with a faded tape.

---

He did not read them there. He found, rather to his own surprise, that he did not want to. He sat on the stone seat for a long time, in the dark, with the tin on his knees, listening to the wind in the wood, and thinking of two young men sitting in the same place a century before, reading each other their poems and agreeing, very cheerfully, that neither of them was as good as A.E.H.

The letters are at Trinity now, with the others, where scholars may read them and argue about them as scholars do. Julian gave them, and the diary, and the book with its two pressed flowers; and he sold the rest of his collection soon afterwards, without very much regret.

Royce wrote an article about the discovery for a trade magazine. It was quite a good article. It was very well researched, and gave a full and accurate account of the diary, the map, and the summer-house, and it mentioned Julian's name only once.

It did not mention the cherries. Royce, after all, had gone in March.
