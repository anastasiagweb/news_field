# Little Jack Horner's Pie

There is a particular hour on Christmas evening in an English country house — after the presents and before the quarrels, when the fire is at its best and the guests are at their worst — at which conversation turns, by some law of nature, to nursery rhymes. At Hartcombe Court, in Somerset, on the Christmas in question, it turned to Little Jack Horner a little after nine, and it was Dr Toby Lark who turned it.

"Pure Victorian invention," he said, stretching his legs towards the fire. "Every guidebook in Somerset tells it. Thomas Horner, steward to the last Abbot of Glastonbury, sent up to London in 1539 with a Christmas pie for King Henry, to soften the old tyrant's heart — and baked inside the pie, the title deeds to a dozen of the abbey's manors. On the road, the story goes, Horner lifts the crust, pulls out the deeds to Mells for himself — *put in his thumb and pulled out a plum* — and the Horners have lived at Mells ever since. Charming. Total nonsense. The family has always said they bought the place, fair and square, and I must say I believe them."

Dr Lark was a historian who appeared on television, and was staying at Hartcombe because he was engaged to the eldest daughter of the house and had not yet been told that she was thinking better of it. He was clever, confident, and wrong about one thing in three, which on television is considered an excellent average.

"The family do say so," agreed Miss Margaret Eames, from the corner. "And they may very well be right. But I'm afraid you're wrong about the plum."

---

Miss Eames was the archivist at Hartcombe. She was sixty-eight, wore cardigans of great antiquity, and had been cataloguing the muniment room above the stables for eleven years, at a rate that the family described as *thorough* and Dr Lark, privately, as *geological*. She rarely spoke at dinner. When she did, Sir Giles Hartcombe had learned to put down his glass.

"In Tudor English," she said, "a *plum* didn't usually mean a plum. Not the fruit off the tree. It meant a raisin — or any sort of dried fruit, really: currants, prunes, figs, dates, whatever came in from the south in barrels. That's why there are no plums in a plum pudding, and never have been. If Jack Horner pulled out a plum, he pulled out a raisin. It's a joke about a man who expected one thing from a pie and found another." She paused. "I've always rather liked it."

"Delightful," said Dr Lark. "Though it doesn't make the story true."

"No," said Miss Eames. "That's not what makes it true."

---

She went up to the muniment room and came back with a book, and the family made room for it on the library table under the lamp — a squat, greasy, leather-bound volume, its pages brown at the edges, smelling faintly of smoke and something older than smoke. It was the kitchen book of Hartcombe: a household manuscript of recipes, receipts and remedies, written in half a dozen Tudor hands between about 1530 and 1580, and used, as such books were, until it fell to pieces.

"Hartcombe was a manor of the abbey until 1539," said Miss Eames, "like Mells. When the abbey fell, its servants scattered. One of them — the cook, I think — came here and stayed, and brought some of his recipes with him. This is his hand." She turned the pages carefully to a double spread near the front. "And this is a pie."

It was a drawing, in faded brown ink, of a great standing pie seen from above — the kind of grand, high-walled pastry case that Tudor cooks raised by hand to stand on its own on a feast-day table, as a castle stands. The case was oblong, with a rounded end, and its inside was divided by pastry walls into twelve compartments, six on each side of a central path, each labelled in a tiny, cramped, careful script. *Reysons of ye sonne. Corance. Prunes. Dates. Fygges.* The fruits went round in order, with a note of the weight of each, and a line across the top of the page that read, in Latin, *For the King's Christmas*.

"Twelve compartments," said Sir Giles slowly. "Twelve manors."

"So the guidebooks would say. But look at the shape of it." Miss Eames laid a finger on the rounded end. "That isn't a pie shape. Nobody raises a pie like that; it would fall down in the oven. That's an apse. This isn't a drawing of a pie at all, Sir Giles. It's a plan. With a pie drawn on top of it, for anyone who happened to be looking."

"A plan of what?" said Dr Lark.

"Your chapel," said Miss Eames to Sir Giles. "Twelve ledger stones in the floor of the nave, six on each side of the aisle, and the apse at the east end. I've walked over them every Sunday for eleven years. And every compartment in the pie has a fruit in it, except one." Her finger moved to the fourth compartment on the north side. Its label was not a fruit. It was a single word.

*Voyde.*

"Empty," said Miss Eames. "No plum."

---

They went out to the chapel at a quarter to eleven, in coats over their evening clothes, with two torches and a crowbar from the stables, and the fire left to burn itself out behind them. It was a small chapel, cold as a cave, older than the house, with white walls and a smell of damp hymn books, and the twelve ledger stones lay in the floor of the nave exactly where Miss Eames had said, their inscriptions worn almost smooth by five centuries of boots.

The fourth stone on the north side bore no name at all.

It took Sir Giles, his son and the under-gardener, who had been fetched from his cottage and did not seem to think it at all unusual, nearly an hour to lift it. Beneath it there was no grave — only a shallow cavity in the rubble, lined with slate; and in the cavity a box of lead, no bigger than a family Bible, its seams sealed with solder that had gone white with age.

Inside the lead was oiled cloth. And inside the cloth was a single folded sheet of parchment, with a seal of brown wax hanging from it on a strip of hide, on which, when Sir Giles held his torch to it with a hand that was not entirely steady, they could make out the figure of the Virgin, enthroned, and part of the name of the Abbey of Glastonbury.

Nobody spoke. Outside, very far away, a church clock was striking midnight.

"Well," said Dr Lark at last, a little hoarsely. "Well. It seems one plum was left in the pie after all."

"A raisin," said Miss Eames gently. "Do try to remember."

---

The parchment went to the record office in Taunton the following week, and from there, after a good deal of correspondence, to a conservator in Wells, where it remains. It has not yet been unfolded. The wax is brittle and the parchment is fragile, and the people in Wells — who have waited, as they point out, nearly five hundred years — are in no hurry to wait a little longer.

Dr Lark went on television in February to talk about the discovery, and talked about it very well. The engagement did not survive the spring. Sir Giles has put a small brass plate on the fourth stone in the chapel floor, and Miss Eames has gone back to the muniment room, where she is now on page forty of the kitchen book, at the recipe for a goose.

She says there is no reason to believe that the goose is a plan of anything. She says this, Sir Giles notices, without quite looking up.
