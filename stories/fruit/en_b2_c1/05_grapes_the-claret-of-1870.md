# The Claret of 1870

Josiah Penhallow, who made a fortune in Cornish tin and spent the second half of his life trying to become an Oxford man, left his old college two things in 1926: a sum of money sufficient to rebuild the north range, and three dozen bottles of claret of the 1870 vintage, with instructions that one bottle should be opened every few years, *in a spirit of seriousness*, until they were gone. The north range was rebuilt in 1931 and has been leaking ever since. The claret, on the other hand, had been treated with perfect seriousness for a hundred years, and on the evening of the centenary there was one bottle left.

It lay in a wire cradle on a trestle table in the undercroft, beneath a vault of fourteenth-century stone, between two candles. Around the table, in black tie, stood the Warden, six Fellows, two guests, and Mr Edwin Pell, who had looked after the college cellar for forty-one years and was the only person present who did not appear to be enjoying himself.

One of the two guests was the reason.

---

Hugo Marchbank wrote about wine for a Sunday newspaper, and had written about it so successfully, for so long, that he had come to believe that wine existed largely in order to be written about by him. He was handsome, in a florid way, and had a famous palate, a famous column, and a famous dislike of what he called *the old guard* — by which he meant anyone who had known about wine before he did. He had been invited because the Warden thought the centenary deserved a little publicity. By the time the soup was cleared, the Warden had begun to suspect that it was going to get some.

"I hope," said Marchbank, standing over the bottle in the candlelight with his hands behind his back, "that nobody will be too disappointed. But I have to say, as a professional, that I should be astonished if this is what it says it is."

There was a small, chilly silence, of the kind that stone vaults are good at.

"Go on," said the Warden, who had no choice.

"The 1870 Médoc," said Marchbank, enjoying himself, "is one of the great legends of the trade. One of the last great vintages before phylloxera — before that wretched little louse came over from America and ate the roots of every vine in Bordeaux. Wine made from the old, ungrafted vines, the wine our great-great-grandfathers drank. And because it is a legend, it is the most faked wine in the world. There must be ten times more 1870 in the cellars of rich men today than was ever made in 1870." He smiled. "The method is perfectly simple. You take an old bottle — an *empty* old bottle, they turn up at every sale — and you fill it with something young and decent, nicely filtered so it looks clean. You recork it, you re-wax it, and you sell it to a man from Cornwall who wants to be a gentleman."

Mr Pell did not move. The candles moved slightly, as if someone had sighed.

"Mr Penhallow," said Pell, "bought those bottles from the château in 1888, sir, through Barton's in Bordeaux. The receipt is in the Bursary."

"Receipts," said Marchbank comfortably, "are the easiest thing in the world to fake. Easier than labels. Look, I don't blame anyone. These things happen. The college has been sold a pup, and a very long-lived pup at that. I'll write it up kindly."

"Would you care," said Mr Pell, "to put money on it, sir?"

---

It is a strange thing to watch a quiet man become, quite suddenly, the most important person in a room. Nobody had heard Edwin Pell raise his voice in forty-one years, and he did not raise it now; but every head in the undercroft turned towards him.

"Name it," said Marchbank, delighted.

"If it's what you say," said Pell, "I'll resign tomorrow, and you can put that in your paper. If it's what Mr Penhallow said it was, you'll write that it is. In the same paper. On the same page."

"Done," said Marchbank.

---

Mr Pell drew the cork himself, slowly, with an old-fashioned screw and a steady hand; it came out whole, black and swollen and soft as cake, and he laid it on a white plate. He decanted the wine over a candle in a single long pour, as cellarmen did a hundred years ago, watching the shoulder of the bottle until the first dark cloud of sediment crept into the neck, and then he stopped, and set the bottle down.

The wine in the decanter was the colour of old brick, with a pale amber rim. Marchbank looked at it, swirled it, sniffed it, and smiled the smile of a man who has been proved right and intends to be generous about it.

But Pell was not looking at the decanter. He had tipped the last inch of the bottle, sediment and all, into a shallow white saucer, and was bending over it with a jeweller's loupe screwed into one eye.

"A filtered wine," he said, without looking up, "throws a fine deposit. Like coffee grounds, sir, if you take my meaning. Very fine and very even. A young wine poured into an old bottle hasn't had time to throw much of anything at all. This" — he moved the saucer a little nearer the candle — "is not like that. This is the old way. They pressed the whole bunch in those days, stalks and all, and never filtered a drop. You get skins in it. Bits of stalk. Pips." He held out the loupe. "And these."

The Warden bent over the saucer, and then the Fellows, one by one, and finally — because it would have looked very odd if he had refused — Marchbank.

In the black crust of sediment, among the fragments of skin and the dark specks of pip, lay something very small and very pale: a tiny brownish cap, no bigger than a grain of rice, shaped like a minute bishop's mitre.

"What on earth is it?" said the Warden.

"Calyptra, sir," said Pell. "The flower cap. A vine flower hasn't got petals the way a rose has — the petals are all joined up at the top in a little hood, and when the flower opens, the hood comes off whole and falls. Most years they drop to the ground, and you never see them again. But in a cold, wet flowering — and the Médoc has had a good many of those — some of them stick. They catch on the little green grapes and stay there all summer. And if you press whole bunches the old way, and don't filter, they go into the vat with everything else and end up in the bottom of the bottle." He sat back on his heels. "You can't put that in with a funnel, sir. Nobody making a fake knows it's there to put."

---

Marchbank was a professional, and he did what professionals do when they are losing: he looked for another argument.

"The cork could have been changed," he said.

"Look at it, sir," said Pell, and Marchbank looked at the soft, black, branded cork on the plate, the château's name still just legible along its side, and said nothing.

"The bottle," he said.

Pell turned the empty bottle in the candlelight. It was a heavy, uneven, greenish-black thing, slightly lopsided, with a deep punt and a seam of bubbles in the glass near the base where a man's breath had gone into it a hundred and fifty-six years ago. Around its neck, under the dust, was a capsule of soft grey lead, torn now where the screw had gone through.

"Hand-blown, sir. Lead capsule, original to the bottle — you can see the crimp. They stopped using lead long ago. You'd have to find an empty bottle, a cork that fitted it, a capsule to match, and a wine pressed with its stalks and never filtered, a hundred and fifty-six years ago. I'm sure it could be done." He paused. "But nobody would trouble to do it for a college in Oxford. We don't pay enough."

---

Marchbank kept his word, which was to his credit, and wrote the column, which was a good one — rather better, people said, than most of his columns, because for once he had something to describe that he had not expected. He called Pell *the last of the great cellarmen* and the wine *an ancient and noble survivor*, and he did not mention the wager.

The wine itself, it must be said, was not very good. It was thin and faded and tasted principally of time, and the Fellows drank it with the solemn faces of men attending the funeral of somebody they had never met. Only Mr Pell, who took the last glass down to the cellar to drink alone, seemed to think it worth the wait.

"It was a hundred and fifty-six years old," he said to the Warden, the next morning, when asked. "It had nothing left to prove."

He meant the wine. The Warden was fairly sure he meant the wine.
