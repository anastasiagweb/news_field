# Portrait of a Boy with a Halved Fruit

The Long Gallery at Haverleigh is a hundred and forty feet of oak floor, north light, and ancestors, and like most English long galleries it was built for walking in when it rained, which in the North Riding is always. The Cliffords who built it had been walking up and down it, in one sense or another, for four hundred years, and most of them were still there on the walls, looking down with the expression of people who have been asked to wait.

The exception was the boy.

He hung between two windows, where the light was best, in a frame that had cost more than the cottage of the man who carved it. He was perhaps fourteen, dark-eyed, in a black doublet with a falling collar, and he held towards the viewer, on the flat of his left palm, half an orange cut clean across. The flesh of the fruit was red — not orange, not pink, but the deep, wet crimson of a blood orange in February — and it was this, more than the boy, that drew the eye and held it. The family called him the Caravaggio. The family's guidebook, revised in 1971, called him *Portrait of a Boy with a Halved Fruit, Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio, c. 1605*, and said that he had been brought from Rome by Sir Thomas Clifford, who had certainly been in Rome, and had certainly brought back a great deal.

Dr Helena Marsh had been invited to Haverleigh to confirm this, and was beginning to suspect she had been invited for no other reason.

"You'll find it all perfectly straightforward," said Gerald Clifford, who was not yet Lord Haverleigh but had hopes of becoming so before Christmas. He was a tall, pleasant, expensive-looking man who said *perfectly* the way other people say *please*. "There's a hearing at the Committee for Privileges in the spring. My cousin's people are being tiresome about the line of descent. The picture rather settles it, you see. The boy is Thomas's son, our direct ancestor. A portrait by Caravaggio is not the sort of thing one commissions for a by-blow."

"No," said Helena. "It isn't."

She was looking at the orange.

---

It is one of the small pleasures of an art historian's life that fruit does not lie. Sitters lie; painters lie; inscriptions, labels, guidebooks and grandchildren lie with real enthusiasm. But a quince is a quince, and a lemon in a Dutch still life is the lemon its painter saw, with its peel spiralling down from the table's edge because he had watched a peel do exactly that. Fruit has a history of its own, and it keeps very good records.

The blood orange is a latecomer to European eyes. Sweet oranges had reached Italy, by way of Portuguese ships, not long before the boy was supposedly painted; red-fleshed ones appear in no description that Helena had ever been able to find before the 1640s, when a Jesuit called Ferrari, writing a learned and beautifully illustrated book about citrus, described an orange whose pulp was the colour of wine as something rare and worth remarking. Forty years after Caravaggio's supposed brush had laid it down so casually on a boy's palm.

"That's hardly conclusive," said Gerald, when she told him, with the faint impatience of a man who has been warned that experts like to be difficult. "Someone must have grown one before some priest wrote it down."

"Very likely," said Helena. "It isn't conclusive. It's interesting. It means I'd like to look more closely."

"How closely?"

"Through it," said Helena.

---

The radiograph came back from the Courtauld in March, a week before the hearing. Helena took the plates up to Yorkshire herself, because she did not trust the post and because she wanted, if she was honest, to see Gerald's face.

They laid them out on the billiard table in the gun room, under the green-shaded lamp, and for a while nobody said anything. An X-ray of an old painting is a strange object: a ghost that has not been told it is dead. It shows the bones of a picture rather than its skin — the heavy lead-white passages glowing pale, the thin glazes vanishing altogether — and very often it shows the painter's first thoughts, the hand moved, the hat removed, the dog painted out.

On Helena's plates, the boy was there, much as he had always been; slightly younger about the mouth, perhaps. The doublet was there. The hand was there.

The orange was different.

Beneath the crimson there was a fruit of the ordinary kind, painted thickly in a pale, lead-rich flesh that showed white on the film, segment by careful segment, with the pith picked out around the rim. And across the cut face of it, from upper right to lower left, ran a narrow dark band — a diagonal stripe left deliberately empty of white, as clean as if it had been ruled.

"What on earth is that?" said Gerald.

Helena had expected the question, and she had spent the train journey choosing how gently to answer it. In the end she decided that gentleness would be a kind of insult.

"It's a baton sinister," she said. "Or something very like one. In English heraldry, a narrow band running that way across a coat of arms is the mark of illegitimate descent. And a halved orange, in your family's case, isn't merely a fruit. The Clifford bastards, as far back as the herald's visitation of 1612, bore *an orange slipped and halved, proper*, with exactly that mark across it. Somebody painted the boy holding his own coat of arms, Mr Clifford. It was a joke, I think. A rather tender one. A father's joke."

"And the red?"

"The red came later. A hundred and fifty years later, at least, on the evidence of the pigment, which is a type of vermilion and lake that nobody was mixing in Rome in 1605. Somebody in the eighteenth century — when your branch of the family first began to say it was the senior one — sent for a restorer and asked him to make the fruit a little more interesting. A blood orange has no pith to speak of and no pale flesh, so the stripe went under the crimson and was never seen again. And once nobody could read the arms, the boy could be anyone's son."

---

Gerald Clifford was not a stupid man, merely a hopeful one, and he took it, Helena thought, rather well. He stood for a long time by the billiard table with one hand flat on its green cloth, looking at the pale ghost of the fruit beneath the red.

"It's not a Caravaggio, either, I suppose," he said at last.

"No. It's very good — Roman, I'd say, certainly of that date, painted by someone who had stood very close to Caravaggio and watched how he did the hands. But no."

"Then it isn't worth anything."

"It's worth a great deal," said Helena. "It simply isn't worth a peerage."

The hearing, in the event, was short. The Committee for Privileges, which has seen everything and is surprised by nothing, received the radiographs with the polite interest of a body that has been lied to by better families than the Cliffords, and the title went to the cousin, who lives in Hampshire and is said to have no interest in pictures at all.

---

The boy is still in the Long Gallery at Haverleigh, between the two windows, where the light is best. Gerald, who kept the house and the debts, considered having the eighteenth-century paint removed and the original fruit restored, stripe and all; he went so far as to obtain an estimate. In the end he did not do it.

"He's been holding that orange for two hundred years," he told Helena, the last time she visited. "It seems unkind to take it away from him now."

And it is true that the red is beautiful — far more beautiful than the honest yellow underneath. That, she reflected on the train home, is the trouble with most improvements to the truth. They are almost always better painted.
