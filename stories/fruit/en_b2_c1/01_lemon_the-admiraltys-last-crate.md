# The Admiralty's Last Crate

There are two kinds of historian at an Oxford high table: those who have been to the archive and those who have read the book of someone who has, and the second kind always talks more. Professor Ambrose Quaile belonged, by temperament and by long practice, to the second; Dr Simon Harrow, who had spent the Long Vacation in a cold reading room in Portsmouth with a magnifying glass and a cardigan, belonged to the first, and had therefore said almost nothing all evening.

"The *Corisande*," Quaile was explaining to the Dean, over the remains of a rather tired partridge, "is the perfect parable of naval incompetence. A frigate of thirty-two guns, lost off Cape Passero in the autumn of 1804, in weather that a Thames barge would have survived. Overloaded, of course. Lemons for Nelson. The fleet was terrified of scurvy, and someone at Messina simply kept filling the hold until she sat like a duck."

"Lemons," said the Dean, who liked a story to have fruit in it.

"Lemons, and lemon juice in casks. The Navy drank it by the gallon. Admirable for the teeth; fatal, it seems, for the ship." Quaile smiled at his own sentence, as he usually did.

Harrow put down his fork. "She wasn't overloaded with lemons," he said.

It was said quietly, but high tables are built for acoustics, and the conversation on either side of him stopped in the way that conversation stops when a junior fellow contradicts a senior one: not in shock, exactly, but in anticipation.

"My dear Harrow," said Quaile. "The manifest survives. I have quoted it."

"You have quoted the manifest," Harrow agreed. "I have read the log."

---

He explained it over the port, because the Dean insisted, and because Quaile, who could not bear to leave a room in which he might be discussed, stayed to listen.

The manifest, signed by the agent victualler at Messina, declared forty casks of lemon juice and a quantity of fresh fruit in crates. The master of the *Corisande*, a careful Scot named Inglis, had entered in his log, as masters did, the ship's draught fore and aft on leaving harbour. He had also, being careful, worked out what that draught ought to have been, from his stores, his water, his guns, and the weight of the cargo as declared. Lemon juice is a little heavier than water and a great deal lighter than anything that matters. Inglis's arithmetic, laid out in a neat column in the margin, came to one figure. The sea, which is never bribed, came to another. The ship sat four inches deeper by the stern than her cargo could explain.

"Four inches," said Quaile, "is a sailor's thumb."

"On a frigate," said Harrow, "four inches is several tons. Inglis thought so too. He wrote *query* beside it, and underlined it, and then — this is the interesting part — someone else has drawn a line through his query in a different ink. The same ink as the signature of a Mr Ffolliott, who travelled as a passenger and is described in the muster book, with great economy, as *a gentleman on the King's business*."

"A spy," said the Dean, delighted.

"A paymaster," said Harrow. "Which in Naples in 1804 came to the same thing."

He reminded them, since Quaile would not, how things stood. Naples was a kingdom balanced on a knife. Its queen loathed Bonaparte; several of its ministers loved money more than they loathed anyone. French troops sat in Apulia, French agents sat in every coffee house, and British gold — discreet, regular, and in quantity — was what kept certain ministers remembering which side their bread was buttered on. The money could not travel openly. French privateers stopped and searched what they could catch, and Messina was full of eyes.

"So you hide it," said Harrow, "in the one cargo that nobody wants to examine closely."

"Lemons," said Quaile, with less conviction than before.

"Lemon juice. Think what a search looks like. A French officer comes aboard. He is tired, he is suspicious, and he has a great many ships to look at. He opens a cask. He sees juice. He dips a ladle, perhaps; he sniffs it; if he is very conscientious he tastes it, and his whole face folds up like a letter. Nobody, having tasted Sicilian lemon juice at full strength, puts his arm into it up to the shoulder. The acid bites every scratch on your hand. It ruins your cuff. It does what a sentry does, and it does it without being paid."

"And underneath?"

"Underneath — at the bottom, because gold is nineteen times heavier than water and has no intention of floating — jars. Earthenware, sealed and waxed inside and out, so the acid couldn't get at the seals. Packed down into the bottom third of a few chosen casks, with honest juice above. The cask weighs what a cask of juice ought to weigh to a man who lifts one corner. It does not weigh what it ought to weigh to a ship."

---

There was a pause, of the respectful kind. Quaile used it, as Harrow had known he would.

"A pretty story," he said. "Inventive. One might almost publish it. But you have a ship at the bottom of the sea, a passenger with a funny name, and a crossed-out word. You do not have a single jar. Evidence, my dear Harrow, is a thing one can put on the table."

Harrow had been waiting for that sentence since August. He found that, now it had come, he did not much enjoy it; he liked Quaile less than he pitied him, and pity is a poor seasoning for victory.

"The *Corisande*," he said, "made three runs from Messina that year. She sank on the third. On the second she landed her casks at Valletta, and the ship's surgeon — a Mr Edward Lacey, who later came home, married money, and left this college the sum that still pays for the Lacey Lectures — took one empty vessel ashore as a keepsake. He thought it handsome. He mentions it in a letter to his sister. *A curious Sicilian jar, waxed within, which the purser says came out of the juice and is no longer wanted.*"

"And this jar," said the Dean slowly, "is—"

"On the windowsill of your study, Mr Dean. It has held your predecessors' daffodils since about 1810."

---

They went up together, the three of them, with a candle, because the electric light in the Dean's study had failed in 1987 and nobody had wished to disturb the wiring. The jar stood where it had always stood: squat, reddish, the size of a large man's two fists, with a narrow neck and a thin brown skin of wax inside its mouth that a century of water had softened but not removed. The daffodils in it were plastic. The Dean had the grace to look embarrassed.

Harrow lifted it and turned it to the candle. On the shoulder, under the dust, were two small marks cut into the clay before it was fired: a crown, and a number.

"Seventeen," he said. "Inglis lists the casks by number in the log. Cask seventeen is one of the six beside which Mr Ffolliott drew his line."

Quaile looked at the jar for a long time. It is to his credit, and Harrow afterwards said so, that he did not argue. He did something more difficult. He held out his hand for it, and when it was given to him he weighed it, empty, in his palm, as a man weighs a thing he has been told about all his life and has never troubled to pick up.

"So she wasn't overloaded with lemons," he said at last.

"No," said Harrow. "She was overloaded with ministers."

---

The college, which loves a scandal so long as it is two hundred years old, did very well out of it. There was an article, then a lecture — the Lacey Lecture, naturally, which Harrow was invited to give and Quaile was invited to introduce, and did, with a generosity that surprised everyone, including Quaile. The *Corisande*'s wreck was located the following summer by a team from Malta, and somewhere beneath the sand off Cape Passero, they believe, the rest of cask seventeen's companions are still keeping their secret, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

The jar is in a glass case now, in the college library, with a small typed card. The Dean, who missed it, bought a replacement for his windowsill from a shop in the Covered Market — a cheerful yellow thing, Italian, with lemons painted on it.

It holds, he says, nothing at all. He has learned to be careful about what he puts in jars.
