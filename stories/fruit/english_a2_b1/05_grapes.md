# The Scissors of St. James's

Lot 87 was a pair of silver grape shears, made in London in about 1815, and the estimate was eight hundred to twelve hundred pounds. Clara Moss knew this because she had written the description herself, at the kitchen table, eating her lunch.

Clara was twenty-four and the most junior person at the auction house in St. James's. She wrote descriptions, carried boxes and took the photographs for the catalogue. The grape shears had been easy. They were pretty little things, with handles like vine leaves and a clever extra blade on one side — a clamp. When you cut a bunch of grapes from the stalk, the clamp caught the stalk and held it, so the grapes did not fall onto the tablecloth. A very early example. Elegant. Useful. Twelve hundred pounds at most.

For the photograph, she had used the grapes from her lunch box. The shears looked so much better holding a real bunch.

The shears had been sent in by Mrs Edith Pell, a widow of eighty from Bath, who had written in pencil that they had "belonged to my husband's family for ever" and that she "needed a new boiler."

On the day of the sale, the room was half empty. Then Lot 87 came up, and two hands went up at the same time.

One belonged to Mr Conrad Vance, an American collector who wore a bow tie and always sat in the front row. The other belonged to Lord Ashby, who sat at the back with his eyes half closed, as if he was bored. Two thousand. Four thousand. Eight. The auctioneer's voice rose a little with every bid. Twelve thousand pounds — ten times the estimate — and Lord Ashby stopped. Mr Vance smiled and wrote something in a small notebook.

"Why?" said Clara afterwards, to nobody. Nobody knew.

She went down to the basement, where the old books lived, and began to look.

It took her four evenings. On the fourth, in a dusty printed book of letters and papers from 1815, she found a list — an inventory — of the travelling things of the Duke of Wellington. His beds, his boots, his maps, his silver. And a dressing case of dark wood, "with a false bottom, opened by the small key." Under it, in the Duke's own words, a note to his servant:

*Turn the key only so far as the grape shears open when they hold the stalk. No further, or the lock will break and the bottom is closed for ever.*

Clara sat back.

The clamp. Of course. When the shears closed on a stalk, the clamp caught it a moment before the blades met. At that moment the blades stood open at one small, exact angle. Not more. Not less. The Duke had used his own grape shears as a secret ruler, and he had trusted that nobody would ever think of it.

Somebody had thought of it. Two people, in fact. And one of them had paid twelve thousand pounds for a ruler.

But the shears were only the ruler. Where was the box?

She looked again at Mrs Pell's pencil letter. *My husband's family for ever.* She looked in the old books for the name Pell. And there, in the same inventory, at the bottom of the page, was the name of the Duke's personal servant in 1815: Samuel Pell.

Clara took the train to Bath on Saturday.

Mrs Pell lived in a small flat that was very cold, because of the boiler. She made tea, and Clara told her everything. Mrs Pell listened with her head on one side, like a bird.

"A dark wooden box," she said slowly, "with a little brass lock?"

She went into the bedroom and came back with it. It was full of knitting wool and needles.

"My sewing box," she said. "It's always been my sewing box. The key goes round and round and then it sticks. My husband said never to force it. I never did."

They took the wool out. Clara opened her catalogue at Lot 87 and laid it on the table. There was her photograph: the silver shears holding a bunch of supermarket grapes, the clamp tight on the stalk, the blades open at exactly the right angle. You did not need the shears. You did not need twelve thousand pounds. You needed a picture and a protractor, and Clara had a protractor in her bag, left over from school.

She turned the little key very slowly, watching the angle. There was a soft click. The bottom of the box lifted like a sigh.

Inside lay a packet of letters tied with faded red ribbon, in the Duke's handwriting, never opened, never printed, never seen.

The letters were sold at the auction house the following spring. Clara wrote the description. It was the longest one she had ever written.

Mr Conrad Vance sat in the front row. Lord Ashby sat at the back with his eyes half closed. They bid against each other for a long, long time.

Mrs Pell bought a new boiler. And a new sewing box, with no lock at all.
