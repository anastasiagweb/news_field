# The Marmalade of Vicarage Lane

In the village of Little Ashcombe, in the Cotswolds, February is the month of marmalade. Snowdrops come up in the churchyard, the bitter Seville oranges arrive from Spain, and every kitchen on Vicarage Lane smells of sugar and peel. On the last Saturday of the month the whole village carries its jars to the village hall, the judges taste them with little silver spoons, somebody wins a gold medal, and everybody else goes home and says the judging was unfair.

For ten years, the somebody had been Mrs Margaret Fortescue.

Mrs Fortescue was the widow of a colonel, and she never let anyone forget it. She lived in the biggest house on the lane, behind a high wall, with a small glass orangery at the back. "My marmalade," she told everyone, "is made only from Seville oranges from my own tree. Nobody else in England can say that." Her jars had handwritten labels in purple ink. Her ten gold medals hung in a row in her front window, where the postman could see them.

Mr Arthur Penrose, the village chemist, had never entered the competition. He was a small, quiet man of sixty who had lived above his shop for thirty years. He knew which of his neighbours took pills for their hearts, which took pills for their nerves, and which bought hair dye and pretended they didn't. He knew a great deal and said very little. This year, for the first time, the vicar had asked him to be a judge.

So on the last Saturday in February, Mr Penrose stood in front of forty-two jars of marmalade with a silver spoon in his hand.

Mrs Fortescue's jar was number seventeen. He knew it was hers, because she was standing three metres away, watching him the way a cat watches a bird. He held the jar up to the window. The marmalade was beautiful — clear, golden, full of thin, perfect strips of peel. And there, in the middle, was a fine red line. A thread of ruby colour, like a vein in marble.

Mr Penrose looked at it for a long time.

He knew his oranges. A Seville orange is orange inside — always. Red comes from one place only: the blood orange of Sicily, which carries a colouring in its flesh that makes it look as if someone has cut a finger on it. And that red colouring has a little secret. In acid, it stays red. But in something alkaline — ordinary baking soda, for example — it turns blue, and then green.

Mr Penrose gave jar seventeen a polite nine out of ten and said nothing.

That evening, in the back room of his shop, he stirred a spoonful of the marmalade into a glass of warm water. Then he added baking soda, a little at a time. The water fizzed and hissed. And slowly, like a sky before a storm, the pink water turned grey, then a dirty blue-green.

He did the same with a jar of real Seville marmalade from his own cupboard. It fizzed too, but it stayed exactly the colour of weak tea.

On Monday he went to the grocer's to buy some tea he did not need.

"Funny thing," said Mr Bell, the grocer, as he weighed it. "Mrs Fortescue's gardener was in last week. I said, 'Doesn't she grow her own oranges?' He laughed so much he had to sit down. That tree's never had an orange on it in twenty years, he says. It's a lemon, anyway."

"Is it?" said Mr Penrose.

"The only one who buys oranges from me in winter," said Mr Bell, "is old Mrs Dunn, over in Upper Ashcombe. I let her have the blood oranges cheap — the soft ones, the ones nobody wants. Ten years she's been buying them. Makes marmalade, I think. Never enters it. Says she can't afford the bus."

Mrs Dunn lived in a cottage with a green door and a broken gate. She had been a cook in a big house before her husband died, and her kitchen smelled of oranges. On a shelf by the stove stood a row of jars with plain white labels.

"A lady buys them," said Mrs Dunn, when Mr Penrose asked. "Every February. Twelve jars. Ten pounds for the lot, which is very kind of her. She says she gives them to the poor." She looked at him and blinked. "Why? Are they good?"

"Mrs Dunn," said Mr Penrose, "they have won ten gold medals."

Mrs Dunn sat down very suddenly on a kitchen chair.

Mr Penrose never told the village. He was not that kind of man. He wrote one short letter to Mrs Fortescue, and he did not sign it. The next week the ten gold medals disappeared from her front window, and she told everybody that she was "retiring from competition, to give the younger ladies a chance."

The following February, the vicar drove to Upper Ashcombe and brought Mrs Dunn to the village hall in his car. She carried one jar with a plain white label.

She won, of course.

And when Mr Penrose held her jar up to the window, there it was — that thin red line, like a secret that had finally decided to tell the truth.
