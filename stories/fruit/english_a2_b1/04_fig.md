# The Wall at Tarring

I was twenty, and the youngest reporter on the local paper, when I learned that a tree can give evidence in a court of law.

It was a small case in a small room — the sort of case the senior men always gave to me. A company called Harmon Homes wanted to knock down an old garden wall in West Tarring and build eight houses on the land behind it. The land was a fig garden. The people of Tarring said it was the oldest fig garden in England, and that the first tree had been planted there in the twelfth century. Nobody could prove that, of course. Legends never come with receipts.

The law was simple. If the wall was old enough, it was protected, and nobody could touch it. If it was built in Victorian times, Mr Harmon could knock it down on Monday morning.

Mr Harmon had a very good lawyer, a tall man with silver hair and a voice like warm honey. He held up a map.

"Your Honour, this map of 1868 shows no wall here. None. And here" — another paper — "is a builder's bill from 1874, for 'one garden wall, flint and brick, at Tarring.' The wall is Victorian. It is younger than the railway. It is not history, Your Honour. It is simply in the way."

He sat down, looking like a man who has already ordered his lunch.

The people of Tarring had no lawyer. They had Miss Ruth Abbott.

Miss Abbott was a retired botany teacher of about seventy, in a brown cardigan and walking boots. She had taught half the people in that courtroom, including, I discovered later, the judge's wife. She carried a wooden box, and she put it on the table in front of the judge as carefully as if it held eggs.

"Your Honour," she said, "I'm not a lawyer. I would like to show you a root."

The silver-haired lawyer smiled.

She opened the box. Inside was a round slice of wood, about as wide as a saucer, sawn clean and smooth, pale, with fine dark lines in it.

"Three weeks ago," said Miss Abbott, "Mr Harmon's workmen came into the garden early on a Sunday, without permission, and began to cut away the roots of the oldest fig tree, where it grows against the wall. To make the wall easier to knock down, I suppose. The neighbours stopped them. But they had already cut through one root. This root. It was growing through the wall. Not over it, not beside it — through it, between the stones."

"Miss Abbott," said the judge, "where is this going?"

"A tree keeps a diary, Your Honour. Every year it grows one new ring of wood. Count the rings, and you know the age. Roots do the same, more or less, though they are harder to read. I have spent three weeks with a microscope and a very bright lamp." She took a paper from her cardigan pocket. "This piece was cut from inside the wall. It has two hundred and eleven rings."

Nobody spoke.

"A root cannot grow through a wall that isn't there yet," said Miss Abbott. "This root went into those stones more than two hundred years ago. Long before 1868. I'm sure Mr Harmon's builder repaired the top of the wall in 1874 and sent somebody a bill for it. But the bottom of the wall was already there. The tree was already holding it."

The silver-haired lawyer stood up. Then he sat down again. It was the only time in fifty years I have seen a lawyer with nothing to say.

"There is one more thing," said Miss Abbott. "It isn't evidence, exactly. It is just — interesting."

She took a small envelope out of the box and shook something into her hand. It was a coin, very thin, smaller than a modern penny, dark, with a rough, uneven edge.

"When the workmen cut the root, it was wrapped round one of the old flints at the bottom of the wall. Holding it like a fist. This was between the root and the stone. The British Museum looked at it last Thursday." She put it on the table, under the judge's nose. "It's a silver penny of King Henry the Second. From about the year 1180."

The judge looked at the coin for a long time. Then he looked at Mr Harmon. Then, for some reason, he looked at me, as if he wanted to be sure the newspaper was writing this down.

"The wall stays," he said.

I wrote it down. It was my first front page.

The wall is still there. I go and look at it sometimes, on summer evenings, when the figs are soft and purple and the wasps are drunk on them. Mr Harmon built his houses somewhere else in the end, beside a main road. I am told they have very nice walls.

And inside the old flints at Tarring the roots go on growing, slowly, one ring a year, keeping count — the way something there was already keeping count in 1180, when a man, perhaps in a brown robe, with a basket of young trees on his arm, dropped a penny in the earth and never came back to look for it.
