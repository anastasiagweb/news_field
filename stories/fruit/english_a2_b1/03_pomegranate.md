# The Charterhouse Ink

The document arrived at the library in a cardboard box from a supermarket, which was the first thing Sarah Linden did not like about it.

The second thing was Mr Gideon Crane.

He came with it, in a long camel coat, and stood in the old library in Oxford as if he had bought it. "Fifteenth century," he said, and put the box on her desk. "Found it in the attic last month, behind a chimney. A gift of land to the monks of the Charterhouse. Perfectly genuine. I just need somebody with letters after her name to say so."

Sarah was twenty-nine, and she had the letters, but only just. She was the youngest palaeographer in the department — a reader of old handwriting — and she spent her days in a small room under the roof with manuscripts five hundred years old. She liked them much more than most living people.

She opened the box. Inside, wrapped in a tea towel, was a single piece of parchment, brown and soft, smelling faintly of dust and animals. In a neat, dark hand, a knight named Sir Walter de Combe gave "the meadow called Long Mead, by the river" to the monks of the Charterhouse, for the good of his soul, in the year 1462.

Sarah knew Long Mead. Everybody in Oxfordshire knew Long Mead. It was the big green field beside the river at Combe, where the village held its summer fair and children learned to swim. For three hundred years everybody had believed it belonged to the village. But if this document was real, it had belonged to the monks. And when King Henry VIII closed the monasteries, he had given all the Charterhouse lands at Combe to a man called Thomas Crane.

"Four hundred houses," said Mr Crane happily. "Lovely spot."

He left his card. On it was his company's sign, a little purple heron. "My great-grandfather's design," he said. "He made the family fortune in Manchester. Colours, you know. Dyes for cloth. Clever man."

Sarah did all the right things first. The skin was calfskin, and it was truly old. The handwriting was correct for the 1460s: the long tails, the little hooks, the short forms of the Latin words. Even the ink looked right — brown-black, the colour of strong coffee. Medieval monks made ink like that from the thick skins of pomegranates, boiled for hours, then mixed with green vitriol, a kind of iron salt. That ink bit into the skin and stayed there for ever.

"Has it been tested?" asked Professor Ambrose, when she showed him. He was eighty-two and nearly blind, and he still came into the library every day. He had taught her everything.

"Mr Crane says his own laboratory tested it. Pomegranate and iron. The real medieval recipe."

"Did he," said the professor. "How very helpful of him." He gave her his old brass magnifying glass. "Remember what I told you in your first week, Sarah? Ink goes into the skin. Dirt sits on top of the skin. Never the other way round."

She took the glass up to her room under the roof and turned on the strongest lamp.

It took her two hours to see it. After that, she could not stop seeing it.

Long ago the parchment had been folded into eight, and along the folds the skin had cracked, the way old leather cracks. The cracks were full of dirt — centuries of dust and candle smoke, pressed deep into the skin. And where the words crossed the cracks, the ink did not stop. It ran down into them, the way water runs into a dry riverbed. In two places, under the glass, she could see the brown-black ink lying on top of the grey dirt, as clean as new paint on an old wall.

Somebody had written on this parchment after it was old. After it was folded, cracked and dirty. Somebody had written on it this year.

Sarah sent a tiny scraping of ink, smaller than a grain of salt, to the university laboratory. The answer came back nine days later. Pomegranate, yes. Iron, yes. And something else: a purple chemical dye, added to make the weak brown ink darker and older-looking. It was called an aniline dye. Nobody in the world had made one before 1856, when an eighteen-year-old student in London discovered it by accident.

She read the report twice. Then she took Mr Crane's card out of her drawer and looked at the little purple heron.

Within twenty years of 1856, half the dye works in Manchester were making aniline purples. One of the biggest — she found it in the library catalogue in ten minutes — had belonged to a man called Josiah Crane. His recipe books, a local history said, were "still kept by the family."

Mr Crane, it seemed, had found more than one old thing in his attic.

The village of Combe held its summer fair on Long Mead in July, as usual. Sarah went. She ate a burnt sausage and watched the children jump into the river, screaming.

She thought about Mr Crane in her office, in his camel coat. *Colours, you know. Clever man.*

He had been right. His great-grandfather was a very clever man. He had made a colour that lasted for ever.

That was exactly the problem.
