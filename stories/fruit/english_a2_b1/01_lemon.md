# The Glasshouse of Lady Evelyn

Lady Evelyn Trewin died in March, at the age of ninety-one, and her nephew Rupert arrived with a spade before the funeral flowers were dead.

The house stood on a cliff in Cornwall, and at the end of the garden, almost hanging over the sea, there was a glasshouse — Victorian, iron and glass, full of strange plants that Lady Evelyn had collected all her life. In winter the wind came off the Atlantic and screamed at the glass. Inside, it was always warm, and it always smelled of lemons.

Everybody in the village knew about the Trewin diamonds. Lady Evelyn's grandfather had brought them back from Africa: a little bag of small, clear stones. She had worn them at her wedding, and never again. "They are somewhere safe," she used to say, and then she laughed, which annoyed people.

The will was read in the library on a wet Tuesday. Rupert got the house, the land, the furniture, the paintings and "everything inside the walls of the house." He smiled at that. Then the solicitor turned the page.

"To my niece, Jenny Pascoe, who has looked after my plants for six years and has never once asked me for anything, I leave my lemon tree in the blue pot, and everything that grows on it."

Rupert laughed out loud. Jenny said nothing. She was twenty-six, she had earth under her fingernails even at the reading of a will, and she lived in one room above the village shop.

Rupert began to dig the next morning.

He dug up the rose garden. He lifted the floorboards. He took the backs off the paintings and the bottoms out of the drawers. He paid two men from Penzance to empty the old well. For three weeks the house sounded like a building site, and every evening Rupert sat in the kitchen, dirty and angry, and drank Lady Evelyn's whisky.

Jenny came every day to water her tree, because it was too big to move. It was a Ponderosa lemon, older than she was, and it grew the biggest lemons she had ever seen — fat, rough, yellow fruit as large as a baby's head, so heavy that the branches needed sticks to hold them up.

She remembered a morning three summers ago. She had come into the glasshouse early and found the old lady standing on a chair with a knife in her hand and string in her mouth.

"Get down, you'll fall!"

"I am grafting," Lady Evelyn had said through the string. "Go away and come back in an hour."

When Jenny came back, there was a tight bandage of string round one of the thickest branches, and the old lady was sitting in the wicker chair with her eyes closed, looking very pleased with herself.

"Trees are better than banks, Jenny," she had said. "A tree heals. Remember that."

The string had come off the next spring. Now there was only a lump on the branch, round and rough, like a knot in old wood. And from that branch hung the biggest lemon on the tree. The branch bent under it like a fishing rod.

At the end of the third week Rupert came into the glasshouse. The men from Penzance had found nothing in the well except a bicycle and some bottles. He looked at the tree, and then at Jenny.

"She was a crazy old woman," he said. "She left me a house with nothing in it. And I think you know where they are."

"I don't," said Jenny, which was true.

"Everything inside the walls of the house," said Rupert. "This glasshouse is joined to the house. So it's inside the walls. So it's mine." He looked at the huge lemon. "All of it."

"The tree is mine. And everything that grows on it."

"Is it?" said Rupert. He took the great lemon in both hands, as if it were somebody's neck, and pulled.

The lemon did not come off. Rupert pulled again, with all his weight, and there was a loud crack — not the soft sound of fruit, but the sharp sound of wood. The branch broke at the lump, exactly where the string had been, and Rupert stood there with a lemon and half a metre of branch in his hands.

He was so angry that he threw them both on the stone floor.

The lemon burst. Juice and yellow flesh went everywhere — over his shoes, over the tiles, over Jenny's boots. The branch hit the floor and split open along the old lump, like a walnut. And out of the broken wood, rolling slowly through the lemon juice, came small, bright stones. Ten. Twenty. More. They rolled across the wet floor and stopped against the toes of Jenny's boots, shining like drops of water in the sun.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Below the glass, the sea hit the bottom of the cliff, again and again.

Jenny bent down and picked up one of the stones. It was sticky with juice. Three years ago, she understood now, the old lady had cut into the living branch, pushed the stones inside, and tied it up tightly. And the tree, like every tree, had healed. Year after year it had grown new wood over the wound and closed the secret inside — and then, right on top of it, like a flag, it had grown its biggest lemon.

"It wasn't inside the walls," said Jenny. "It was inside the tree."

She wiped the stone on her jeans and put it in her pocket.

Then she went to get the dustpan, because Lady Evelyn had never allowed a mess in her glasshouse.
