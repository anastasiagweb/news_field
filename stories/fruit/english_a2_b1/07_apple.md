# The Lost Orchard of Devon

I met Owen Tapp in a pub on the edge of Dartmoor, on a wet night in October, and he showed me an apple that rang like a bell.

He was a retired postman, about seventy, with a red face and a canvas bag that he never put down. For thirty years he had walked the moor delivering letters, and he had looked over every garden wall in every village. Now he looked for lost apples. There are hundreds of them in Devon — old varieties that grew in one valley, or one farm, and then disappeared when the old people died and the orchards were cut down. Owen found them. Newspapers called him "the Apple Detective." He hated that.

"There's one I never found," he told me. "Not in forty years."

It was called Coombe's Seedling, after Dr Ezra Coombe, a Victorian doctor who loved plants more than patients. He had grown it in the 1870s, and he had written about it in his notebook: *a small yellow apple with a very open heart. When ripe, the seeds come loose inside and make a noise like a little bell when the fruit is shaken.* Then Dr Coombe died, his garden became a car park, and Coombe's Seedling was gone.

But that was only half the story. The other half was the will.

Dr Coombe had owned the rights to a small tin mine on the moor, Wheal Grace, which had closed long ago. In his will he wrote: *My papers for Wheal Grace I leave to my family, and they lie beneath the Bell.* Nobody ever found them. His family dug under the bells of every church for ten miles. The mine was forgotten. The family grew poor. And now, a hundred and forty years later, a big company wanted to open Wheal Grace again, because the old rocks held a metal that modern telephones need. The company had been trying for years to discover who owned the rights. So far, nobody.

"Under the Bell," said Owen. "Everybody thought it meant a church."

He had spent the summer talking to old people, which is what an apple detective mostly does. And in a cottage near Widecombe, an old lady called Mrs Hannaford, who was ninety-four, had laughed at him.

"Coombe's Seedling? Never heard of it," she said. "But my granny had a tree she called the Pixie Bell. Little yellow apples. You shook them by your ear and they rang. Granny said the pixies lived inside. There was one in the old chapel yard at Holne Moor, I think. Granny's granny planted it. A doctor gave her the tree, she said."

"Her granny's name?" asked Owen.

"Coombe. Mary Coombe. She was the doctor's daughter."

The chapel at Holne Moor had been empty for eighty years. Its roof had fallen in. Its little churchyard was full of brambles as high as a man. Owen went there in October with gloves and a saw, and he cut his way through for two days.

On the second evening, in the far corner, behind a broken gravestone, he found an apple tree.

It was very old and very bent, more like a crooked old man than a tree. Half of it was dead. But on the living half hung twenty or thirty small yellow apples. Owen picked one, held it next to his ear and shook it gently.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

"And here it is," he said to me in the pub, and he took it out of his canvas bag and put it in my hand.

I shook it. It rang. A tiny, dry, cheerful sound, like a bell on a cat's collar, a long way off. The heart of the apple was so open that the ripe seeds sat loose inside it, like peas in a box. Two farmers at the bar turned round to listen. One of them laughed and said his grandfather had a word for apples like that, but he could not remember it.

"You found the apple," I said. "Well done."

"I found more than the apple," said Owen.

Under the Bell, Dr Coombe had written. Not under *a* bell. Under *the* Bell — the Pixie Bell, which his daughter had planted in the chapel yard. Owen had brought a spade the next day. The roots of the old tree were thick and twisted, like fingers holding something. Between them, sixty centimetres down, was a tin box, black with rust. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the papers for Wheal Grace. Dry. Clear. Signed.

"Who do they belong to?" I asked.

"His family," said Owen. "The will says so."

"And who is his family?"

Owen finished his beer. He smiled for the first time that evening.

"A lady of ninety-four," he said, "in a cottage near Widecombe, who has never had any money in her life. I took her the box this morning. The company's lawyers are going to see her next week." He stood up and picked up his bag. "She says she'll make them a cup of tea. And she says they can't come in unless they listen to her apples first."
