# The Cherry Amber Necklace

Leonard Ash had been testing beads in hot water for forty years, and he knew every smell they could make.

He worked in a small room at the back of an auction house in Brighton, near the sea. Rings, brooches, pearls and old watches came across his desk all day, and he told the auction house what was real and what was not. He was sixty-eight, wore a cardigan with holes in the elbows, and did not like being hurried.

On Thursday a young woman named Rosie Turner brought in a necklace.

"It was my great-grandmother's," she said. "From the 1920s. Granny always called it her cherry amber. I'm sorry to sell it, but my rent's gone up again."

Leonard laid it on the black velvet on his desk. Thirty round beads, dark red, almost black — the colour of cherries in July, the colour of old wine with a light behind it. The middle bead was a little bigger than the others.

"Cherry amber" is not really amber. Real amber is old tree resin, millions of years old. Cherry amber is something cleverer and newer: a hard plastic, made from chemicals in factories between the two world wars. Women loved it. It was cheaper than amber and brighter, and today collectors pay good money for it. But there are many copies. So Leonard tested it the old way.

He rubbed the first bead hard with his thumb and smelled it. Then he dropped it, on its string, into a cup of very hot water and smelled again. A sharp, chemical smell came up — like old hospitals, like the red soap his mother used to wash the floor with. Carbolic. That was the right smell. That was the real 1920s plastic.

He went along the necklace, bead by bead. Carbolic. Carbolic. Carbolic.

Then he came to the bead in the middle.

It smelled of nothing. Or almost nothing. Something soft and faintly sweet, like a garden in summer, like a wooden chair that has stood in the sun.

Leonard frowned. He put one drop of warm water on the bead with the end of a spoon and waited a minute. When he touched it, the surface felt sticky under his finger. Very slightly. As if the bead were beginning to remember that it had once been soft.

He sat back in his chair.

He knew that feeling. As a boy he had grown up in Kent, among the cherry orchards, and in summer the old trees wept a thick, golden-red gum from cracks in their bark. He and his brother used to pick it off and chew it. It was hard in the sun and sticky in the rain. Cherry gum. Tree gum. Not plastic at all.

Somebody, a hundred years ago, had taken a lump of gum from a cherry tree, shaped it into a bead, covered it with a thin coat of varnish so that the air could not touch it, and hidden it in the middle of a plastic necklace where it looked exactly like its neighbours. And then the necklace had lain for a century in a dry box, in a dry drawer, waiting.

Leonard switched on his lamp, the strong one, and held the bead up to it.

The plastic beads stayed dark. But the gum bead glowed — a deep, warm red, like a fire seen through a window. And in the very centre of it something small and flat shone back at him. A tiny plate of gold, no bigger than a fingernail.

He took his strongest glass and looked for a long time.

There were letters on the plate, very small and very neat. Two initials: *R.A.* And under them, a name and a number: a bank in the City of London, and *Box 114.*

"Miss Turner," said Leonard, "what was your great-grandmother's name?"

"Ruth," said Rosie. "Ruth Ashdown, before she married. I'm named after her — Rosie's really Ruth. Why?"

Leonard did not answer at once. He was thinking about a young woman in 1925, with a necklace and a secret, and a cherry tree somewhere in an orchard. Perhaps someone had loved her. Perhaps someone had been afraid for her. People do not hide gold plates inside cherry gum for no reason.

"I don't think you should sell this," he said, and gave her the necklace back.

The next morning Rosie telephoned the bank. She was put on hold for twenty minutes, with music. Then a polite man came on the line and spoke for a long time.

Box 114 existed. It had never been opened. It had been rented in May 1925 by a Mr H. Ashdown, for his daughter, Miss Ruth Ashdown — later Mrs Turner — and her children, and their children. The rent had been paid in full, in advance.

For one hundred years.

"Which ends," said the polite man, after a little cough, "on Friday."
