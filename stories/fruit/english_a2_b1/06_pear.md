# The Black Pear of Worcester

Detective Sergeant Meg Harrow had eaten many things in her life, but she had never eaten a Worcester Black Pear, because nobody can.

"You can't bite them," her grandmother used to say. "Not in October. You could break a window with one. Eight hours in red wine and they're still hard. And they don't float, either. I dropped one in the duck pond when I was a girl, and it went straight to the bottom, like your grandad's false teeth."

Meg remembered this on the Tuesday morning, standing in a cold auction room in Worcester, looking at an empty glass case.

Until Monday night, the case had held a wax pear. It was a Victorian model, made in 1860 for a gardening society — a perfect copy of a Worcester Black, the city's own fruit, the one on the city's coat of arms. Brown-red skin, little grey spots, a short dark stalk. It looked so real that visitors had to be stopped from picking it up.

It was also hollow. Inside it, rolled up very small, was a piece of paper: a list of a lost family collection of painted miniatures — tiny portraits, each no bigger than an egg — with a note of where they had been hidden in 1860. An X-ray had found the paper last month. The wax pear was to be sold on Wednesday. Now it was gone.

Only six people had held keys to the room. Five of them had been at a dinner all evening. The sixth was a painter called Julian Rook.

Mr Rook was a copyist. He painted copies of old pictures for rich people who wanted something on the wall that looked like a famous painting but cost less than a car. The auction house had hired him to paint the wax pear for the front of the catalogue. He had spent three days alone with it.

His studio was above a shoe shop near the cathedral. It smelled of paint and coffee and, very strongly, of pears.

"Of course I've got pears," said Mr Rook, when he saw Meg looking. He was a handsome man of forty in a paint-covered shirt. "I'm painting pears. Still life." He waved at a big canvas on the easel, half finished, and at a wide basket on the table beside it, full to the top with brown-red fruit. "Worcester Blacks, all of them. I bought them at the market on Saturday. Have one."

Meg looked at the basket. There were perhaps forty pears in it. Brown-red skins. Little grey spots. Short dark stalks. She looked at them for a long time, and they looked back at her.

"Go on," said Mr Rook, smiling. He held out a kitchen knife. "Cut any one you like."

She could cut one. She could cut ten. If she cut the wrong ten, she would look very stupid, and the wax pear would leave Worcester the next time she turned her back.

She picked up a pear and weighed it in her hand. It was hard — really hard, as hard as a stone — and heavy, much heavier than it looked.

"Mr Rook," she said, "have you got a bucket?"

He stopped smiling. Only for a second, but she saw it.

There was a big white sink in the corner, where he washed his brushes. Meg put in the plug and turned on both taps. When the sink was full, she picked up the basket and, very gently, poured all the pears into the water.

They went in with a sound like heavy rain.

Most of them went straight to the bottom and sat there, dark and still. A few came up slowly and stayed just under the surface, only their stalks showing, like the noses of tired swimmers. That was what a Worcester Black did in October. Grandmother was right. Hard as a stone, and almost as heavy.

And one pear did not go down at all.

It bounced up at once, like a cork. It sat high on the water, much too high, and then it rolled over and lay on its side, with its stalk pointing at the ceiling, as if it was lying on a beach, enjoying the sun. Inside it, Meg knew, there was a small room full of air, and a rolled-up piece of paper.

"Well," said Mr Rook, after a long silence. "That's not fair."

"No," said Meg. "It's physics."

She lifted the wax pear out with two fingers and dried it carefully on a clean tea towel.

The paper inside it was opened at the police station, with two people from the auction house watching. The list of miniatures was there, in faded brown ink, and under it a short note in the same hand: *Put away safe, in the old pear store, under the third shelf.*

The old pear store, it turned out, had been knocked down in 1962. A supermarket stood there now.

Meg did not tell her grandmother about the miniatures. She did take her one of Mr Rook's real pears, the next time she visited. Her grandmother turned it over in her hands and laughed.

"October?" she said. "Put it in the fruit bowl, love. Maybe by Christmas."

It is still in the fruit bowl. It is still hard.
