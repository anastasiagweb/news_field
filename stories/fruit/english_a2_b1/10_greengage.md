# The Green Gage of Suffolk

I was at Hengrave Hall on the evening the emerald disappeared, and I am sorry to say that I was one of the people the police searched.

It was a warm evening at the end of August, and the old house in Suffolk was full of people in their best clothes, drinking wine for a children's hospital. The family who once lived at Hengrave, the Gages, have a story that one of them brought the first greengage trees to England in the 1720s — the round plums that stayed green and tasted of honey, and that still carry the family's name. So the evening had a greengage theme. There were greengage tarts, greengage cocktails, and a lot of green dresses.

And there was the emerald.

It belonged to a rich American lady called Mrs Delphine Marlow, who had given the hospital a great deal of money. It was a raw emerald from Colombia, uncut, not polished, about the size of a large grape, and very green. It sat on a little velvet cushion in the Great Hall under a glass cover. "Like a greengage," said Mrs Marlow, who liked the joke. "But you can't eat it."

At nine o'clock there were speeches. Everyone turned to look at the speakers. When they turned back, the glass cover was lifted and the cushion was empty.

The police arrived in eleven minutes. The doors were locked. For two hours, a hundred and twenty people were searched — pockets, handbags, shoes, the insides of hats. Nothing. The police searched the Great Hall, the gardens, the toilets and the flower pots. Nothing.

At the back of the hall, in the passage to the kitchen, stood the baskets of fruit for the village fair the next day: apples, pears, blackberries, and one big basket of greengages, round and bright green, picked that afternoon. A young policeman looked into it.

"Not ripe yet, are they?" he said, and walked on.

I remembered something then, though I did not think much about it. Earlier in the evening, Mrs Marlow's nephew, a charming young man called Toby, with very white teeth, had stood beside me by that basket. "Greengages, are they?" he said. "They look as if they need another month. Sour as lemons. I'll buy the whole basket at the fair tomorrow — my mother makes chutney. Nobody else will want them."

And there was Mrs Pike.

Mrs Agnes Pike was the housekeeper. She was seventy-nine and had worked at Hengrave for fifty-one years, and she did not like policemen in her kitchen at eleven o'clock at night. She came out into the passage, picked up the basket of greengages, carried it to the kitchen table, and sat down with a knife and a large bowl.

"If I'm to be kept here till midnight," she said, "I shall do something useful. These are for my jam."

"They're not ripe, are they?" said the young policeman again.

Mrs Pike looked at him the way she probably looked at a dirty plate.

"Young man," she said, "a greengage is green when it is ripe. That is the whole point of it. Look." She held one up to the light. "See the little gold freckles? Feel how soft it is? That is honey inside, not lemon. An unripe greengage is hard and dull. A ripe one stays green and goes soft and sweet. Everybody in Suffolk knows that." She looked out of the door at the hall full of rich guests. "Well. Everybody from Suffolk."

And she began to cut.

She worked quickly, the way people work who have done one thing for fifty years. Cut the plum round the middle. Twist the two halves. Take out the stone with the point of the knife. Drop the stone in a white saucer. Halves in the bowl. *Click* went each stone into the saucer. *Click. Click. Click.*

I watched her, because I had nothing else to do. Many of us were watching her by then. There is something comforting about an old woman making jam while the world goes mad around her.

About halfway down the basket, she picked up a plum that was softer than the rest, with a small cut in the side, as if a bird had pecked it. She cut it round the middle and twisted it open.

Inside were two stones.

One was brown and wrinkled, like every other plum stone in the world. The other was green — deep, clear, cold green — and when she dropped it into the white saucer, it did not go *click*. It went *clack*, heavier and harder, like a coin on a table.

The kitchen was very quiet.

Mrs Pike picked up the green stone, wiped the plum juice off it with her apron, and held it up to the light.

"Somebody," she said, "thought these plums were sour. Somebody thought nobody would touch them till tomorrow. And somebody," she said, without turning her head, "told me this afternoon that he'd buy the whole basket for his mother's chutney."

Every head in the kitchen turned towards Toby Marlow. His white teeth were not smiling any more.

The police took him away at half past twelve.

Mrs Pike made the jam the next morning. She says it is the best she has made in fifty years. She calls it Emerald Jam, and it is not for sale.
