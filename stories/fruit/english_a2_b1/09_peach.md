# The Heated Wall at Chatsworth

The wall was two hundred and fifty years old, and it was hollow, and Hannah Brough had been inside it for three weeks.

Not all of her, of course. Her arms, her torch, her little camera on a long cable. Hannah was a heating engineer from Sheffield, thirty-four years old, with a van that said *Brough & Sons — Boilers, Chimneys, Flues* on the side. There were no sons. There was Hannah, and there had been her father, and his father before him. "We've been lighting fires for other people for two hundred years," her father liked to say. The great house in Derbyshire had called Brough & Sons for its boilers for as long as anyone could remember.

This job was different. This was a peach wall.

In the eighteenth century, rich men in the cold north of England wanted peaches. Peaches like sun, and Derbyshire does not have much of it. So the gardeners built a long brick wall facing south, and they made it hollow. Inside, between the bricks, ran flat horizontal passages, one above another, like the shelves of a bookcase. At the bottom of the wall were small fireplaces. On frosty nights a boy lit the fires, and the hot smoke travelled slowly along the passages inside the wall, and the bricks grew warm, and the peach trees tied flat against the bricks felt warm too, and did not die. In the morning the smoke came out of little chimneys on the top.

Now the wall was being repaired, and Hannah's job was to clean and check every passage.

They were all the same. Every one was black inside, thick with two hundred years of soot. She came out every evening looking like a coal miner.

All except one.

It was the fourth passage from the bottom, near the east end, and at first she did not notice that it was strange. Then her camera went in, and the picture on her little screen was red. Red brick. Clean red brick, as if it had been built yesterday. No soot at all. No smoke had ever travelled through this passage. Not once.

At the end of it, a metre inside, there was an iron plate, a damper, closing the passage like a door. On the damper, somebody had scratched four numbers with the point of a knife.

*1812.*

Hannah sat back on her heels on the wet grass and thought.

A passage that had never been used. Closed in 1812. With fires burning above it and below it every winter for a hundred years after that. The passage between them would always be warm — never hot, because no fire went through it, but never cold either, and never wet. Dry and warm. For two hundred years.

It was, she realised, the best cupboard in Derbyshire.

She told the estate manager, and the estate manager told the house, and the next morning four people stood in the garden and watched her take out three bricks and slowly, slowly pull open the old iron damper.

Behind it lay a long tube made of tin, with a lid. Inside the tube, rolled up and tied with string, was a thick bundle of paper.

They opened it in the library, on a big table, wearing white gloves.

There were forty watercolour paintings of peaches. Every peach was painted from life, the size of a real one: the soft red side and the yellow side, the fine hairs on the skin, a leaf, a cut half showing the stone. Under each painting was a name in brown ink — the name of the variety, the date it was picked, and how many came from each tree. Some of the names nobody in the room had ever heard of. These were peaches that had disappeared from England long ago. Nobody had ever seen these paintings. Nobody had known that they existed.

At the bottom of the bundle was a single folded sheet. It was a will.

*I, Thomas Weller, Head Gardener, being ill and not like to see the spring, leave these my paintings, which are the work of my life, and the book of how to grow these trees, to the boy who keeps the fires, who has kept my wall warm through every frost these six winters and never once let it go cold. He cannot read yet. I pray somebody will read this to him.*

Everybody was quiet. Thomas Weller. The library had his name in an old account book, the estate manager said later — head gardener before the famous ones, before anyone wrote books about the gardens. Nobody had remembered him at all.

"Who was the boy?" somebody asked.

The estate manager found it that afternoon, in the same old account book, on a page for the winter of 1812. A list of wages. *For the lighting of the peach wall fires: W. Brough, aged eleven. Four pence a night.*

Hannah read it twice. Then she took off the white gloves, very carefully, and put them on the table.

The paintings went to a museum in the end, and the book of how to grow the trees went with them, because that was the right thing. But the museum printed the name of the boy beside the name of the gardener, and there were two old peaches, from Weller's list, that a nursery managed to grow again from a forgotten tree in a vicarage garden in Bakewell.

Hannah planted both of them against the wall.

Then she went back to work. It was October, the forecast said frost, and somebody had to keep the wall warm.
