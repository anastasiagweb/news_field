# The Smyrna Crate

The crate arrived at the British Museum on a Tuesday in November 1910, addressed in a hand that Edward Lyle had not seen for seven months. It came up from the goods entrance on a porter's trolley, smelling of tar and the sea and, very faintly, of honey. Stencilled on the side were the words *FIGUES DE SMYRNE — PREMIÈRE QUALITÉ*. Painted underneath, in black, rather badly: *Mr E. Lyle, Dept. of Greek and Roman Antiquities. Personal.*

Lyle stood looking at it for some time after the porter had gone.

The handwriting was Arthur Penrose's. Arthur, who had shared his rooms at Cambridge. Arthur, who had gone east in 1907 with a small grant, a large moustache, and the conviction that a Hellenistic city lay under a hill near the Gediz River that every other archaeologist had walked past. Arthur, whose last letter had come in April, from a village whose name Lyle could not pronounce, and who had not been heard of since. The Foreign Office had made enquiries. The Ottoman authorities had made enquiries. A cousin had begun, quietly, to speak of him in the past tense.

Lyle opened the crate himself, with a chisel borrowed from the workshop.

Inside were figs. Dried Smyrna figs, the kind sold at Christmas in round wooden boxes, but here packed loose and deep, pressed flat into a solid brown mass, sticky and dusted with white sugar where they had dried. On top lay a card. On it, in Arthur's hand, only five words:

*Eat them slowly, old man.*

---

He did not eat them. He sat in his office with the crate on the floor and the gas fire hissing, and he thought about Arthur, who had never in his life made a joke without a reason.

He took out one fig and weighed it in his hand. Then he took out another from the same layer and weighed that. The first was heavier. Not by much. But he had spent twenty years handling small objects — coins, gems, lamps, the little clay figures that Greek families had put in their graves — and his hands knew weight the way a musician's ear knows pitch.

He pressed the heavy fig gently with his thumb. Under the soft, sticky skin, under the seeds, there was something hard.

He could have cut it open. He did not, and later he was never sure whether that was because he was a careful scholar, or because he was afraid of what he would find.

Instead, the next morning, he did something that nobody in his department had done before. He took the crate in a cab to a hospital in Bloomsbury, where a young doctor he knew slightly had a Röntgen apparatus — a machine that most of the Museum's Trustees still regarded as somewhere between a toy and a fairground trick. Lyle did not ask the Keeper's permission. He told himself that it was because the Keeper was in Paris.

The X-ray room was small and dark and smelled of hot dust. The doctor laid the figs out on the plate in rows, fifty at a time, and the machine buzzed and crackled, and a green light flickered in a glass tube. Afterwards they stood together in the red light of the darkroom, watching the glass plates come up slowly in the developing tray.

The figs appeared as soft grey shadows, like clouds. Most of them were only that.

Twenty of them were not. Inside each of the twenty, sharp and white, was a shape. Small ovals, some engraved: Lyle could see, even in the wet plate, the outline of a head in profile, a running horse, a winged figure. Clay seals — terracotta gems, pressed from moulds two thousand years ago. And, in six of the figs, thin white curves, broken and folded, with tiny repeated leaf-shapes along their edges.

"What are those?" said the doctor.

"Gold," said Lyle. His voice sounded strange to him. "A diadem. A wreath for the head. Hellenistic, I think. Probably for a burial."

It was a clever packing. He had to admire it. The dense, heavy paste of pressed figs was a perfect cushion; it held each object still, protected it from shock, kept out air and water. And no customs officer in Smyrna, no guard at the port, no bandit on the road from the hills, would ever break open a fig to look for treasure. Figs were what that country sent to the world. Figs were invisible.

---

He sat in his office that night with the twenty figs on a white cloth in front of him, still unopened, beside one ordinary fig he had kept for comparison, and the plates leaning against the lamp.

He knew the law, as every man in his department knew it, though they did not often say it aloud. Since 1884, and more strictly since 1906, the Ottoman government had forbidden the export of antiquities from its territory. Everything found in the ground belonged to the Empire, and to its museum in Constantinople. Things still left Turkey, of course — in diplomatic bags, in the luggage of travellers, in crates of figs. And when they arrived in London, the Museum generally did not ask how.

He knew what the Keeper would say when he came back from Paris. *An important acquisition. A gift from a lost colleague. Penrose would have wished it.* The objects would go into a case in the Hellenistic room with a small card, and the newspapers would call Arthur a hero, and nobody in Constantinople would ever be told.

And Lyle knew something else, which he did not want to know. Arthur had sent the crate to him. Not to the Keeper, not to the Museum. *Personal.* Arthur had trusted that his oldest friend would understand what to do. The difficulty was that Lyle did not know what Arthur had meant him to do. Keep them safe, until Arthur came back? Give them to the Museum, as a last gift? Or had the joke on the card been the whole message: *slowly*, carefully, think about it, old man, before you do the easy thing?

He thought about the hill near the Gediz, which he had never seen. He thought about a village woman, perhaps, who had sat at a table pushing small clay faces into figs with her thumb, for money or for Arthur's sake, and who might now be asked questions by soldiers. He thought about the gold leaves, which had been made for someone's head when they were put into the dark.

At two in the morning he took a sheet of the Museum's paper and began a letter, in French, to the Director of the Imperial Museum in Constantinople. He wrote it three times. The third version was very short.

Then he put the twenty figs into a tin box, one by one, and locked it, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket, where he could feel it against his ribs.

The ordinary fig was left on the cloth, soft and sweet and empty. He looked at it for a long time. Then he ate it, slowly, as he had been told, and sat in the dark with the taste of honey and seeds in his mouth, waiting for the morning and for news that did not come.
