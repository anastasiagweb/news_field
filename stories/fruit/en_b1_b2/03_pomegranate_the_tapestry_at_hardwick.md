# The Tapestry at Hardwick

In February the cold at Hardwick did not come through the windows. It came up through the stone. Margaret Owen had worked in the house for twenty-two winters, and she knew the exact moment, around half past three, when the light in the great windows turned from white to the colour of weak tea and the floor of the Long Gallery began to give back the cold it had been keeping since morning. She wore two pairs of socks, fingerless gloves, and her late mother's grey cardigan. She was the keeper of textiles, and the textiles did not like heating.

The five tapestries lay on padded tables at the north end of the gallery, under a white cotton cover that she folded back one section at a time. They were older than the house. Bess of Hardwick, who had built the place in the 1590s with four husbands' money and her own iron will, had bought them already old, from someone else's collection, the way she bought everything: carefully, cheaply, and in bulk. They were Flemish, early sixteenth century, woven in wool and silk. Each one showed a garden — rabbits, carnations, a small dog — and, in the middle, a single split pomegranate.

The pomegranate had been the emblem of Catherine of Aragon. It was all over the things that had once been hers: on her bindings, her plates, the sleeves of her ladies. Margaret had always assumed that the tapestries had been made for Catherine's household, and had wandered, after her fall, from one great house to another, as such things did, until Bess bought them.

She had not expected them to tell her anything else.

The job was ordinary: a condition survey before the tapestries went away for cleaning. She was mapping the old repairs. Over five hundred years every tapestry collects them — patches, darns, places where a later hand has rewoven a worn area in new wool. You find them by looking at the colour, and then at the back.

It was on the third tapestry that she noticed the seeds.

The pomegranate was shown cut open, as it always was, its skin pulled back to show the fruit inside. Inside, in neat rows, were the seeds: small rounded shapes of red. She had seen them a thousand times. But today, because she was looking for repairs, she was looking at the red itself. And the red of the seeds was not the red of the rest.

The skin of the fruit was woven in a deep, slightly brown crimson — kermes, she was almost sure, the expensive insect dye of the early Tudor workshops. The seeds were brighter. A clean, warm red, a little orange. Madder. And when she lifted the edge and turned her torch on the back, the seed area was rewoven: new warps, a different spin to the wool, a tighter, more domestic hand. Not a Flemish workshop. A woman with a needle, in a cold room, sometime later.

Someone had taken out the old seeds and put in new ones.

She checked the other four. The same. In each pomegranate, the seeds were madder, not kermes, and the back showed the same careful, later hand.

Then, because she was tired and her pencil was in her hand, she counted them.

Four. Seven. Ten. Thirteen. Sixteen.

She sat very still. Nature does not count in threes. No weaver in Flanders, filling a fruit with seeds, would make five fruits that rose by exactly three each time. This was not a pattern of the eye. It was a pattern of the mind.

---

She did not tell anyone that evening. She told herself it was because there was nothing yet to tell. That was partly true.

The next morning, before the house opened, she walked the Long Gallery with a notebook. It was the longest room she knew, almost the length of the house, its walls lined with oak panelling below and tapestries above, its windows so tall that on bright days the room seemed to have no walls at all. There were two great fireplaces on the inner side. She had walked past them perhaps ten thousand times.

She tried the numbers against the windows first, then against the portraits. Nothing. Then she began to count the oak panels along the inner wall, from the north end, where the tapestries had first hung in Bess's inventory of 1601.

Four panels. A pilaster. Seven. A doorway. Ten. And there the first fireplace began, its stone surround cut into the wood.

She went on. Thirteen panels. Sixteen.

The sixteenth panel was the narrow one on the left side of the second fireplace, half hidden by the stone. It was darker than its neighbours, as if it had been oiled by more hands. She knelt and pressed it, gently, the way she would test the strength of an old silk. It did not move. She pressed the lower corner. Something inside the wall clicked, like a tongue against teeth.

---

The niche was the size of a bread oven, lined with brick. Inside, wrapped in a piece of linen that had once been white, was a small book.

Margaret did not touch it. She photographed it, sent one message, and waited, cold to the bone, for an hour, until the conservator from the regional team arrived with gloves and a box.

When they unwrapped it on the table, under the soft lamps, it was a book of hours, perhaps ninety pages of vellum, very worn at the corners where fingers had held it during prayers. On the first page, among painted flowers, was a split pomegranate. Under it, in faded ink, in a Spanish hand: *Katherina*.

"Hers," said the conservator, very quietly. "It could actually be hers."

It would take a year of study to say so properly. But Margaret did not need a year to understand what the madder seeds meant. Somewhere in Bess's household, in the 1590s, a woman who could sew had known what had travelled to Hardwick, folded inside these tapestries, from the old queen's house. A forbidden book, from a forbidden faith, in a Protestant house run by a woman who was frightened of nothing except the Crown. And that woman with the needle had hidden it in the wall, and then hidden the way to it in the one place where nobody would look: in the fruit, in plain sight, in red.

There was going to be a great deal of attention. Newspapers. Television. The director had already telephoned twice. Margaret was due to retire in the summer, and she knew, as clearly as she knew the temperature of the stone, that this would be what she was remembered for: not twenty-two years of patient darning, but a cupboard behind a fireplace.

She found that she minded, a little. Then she found that she did not mind at all.

That evening, when the others had gone, she went back to the north end of the gallery. The light was the colour of weak tea. She folded the white cotton cover back from the fifth tapestry, and looked for a long time at the sixteen red seeds that a woman had put in, one by one, four hundred years ago, without signing her name.

Then she folded the cover over them again, carefully, the way you tuck in a child.
