# The Pip That Lied

Court 26 of the Rolls Building was a modern room pretending to be an old one: pale wood, green leather, no windows, and a clock on the wall that made no sound at all. Dr Frances Alder had given evidence in courts for fifteen years, about fallen trees and disputed hedges. She had never before been asked to give evidence about a man's memory.

The case was a claim in the Chancery Division, which hears the quarrels of families over money and land. The late Lord Hartwell had died two years earlier at the age of ninety-one, leaving a house in Herefordshire, an estate, and four volumes of handwritten diaries, which his nephew, Mr Simon Hartwell, now wished to publish as memoirs. The diaries also contained, in the last volume, a long and rather moving passage in which the old lord promised the house to his nephew. The will said something different. The will left the house to a cousin.

If the diaries were genuine, the nephew had a case. If they were not, he had nothing.

Counsel for the cousin, a tall woman from the Temple with a voice like cold water, had asked Frances to read one page.

---

It was the entry for October 1985. *Brought the guests out to the tree after lunch. Ten years since I carried that pip home from Virginia in my waistcoat pocket — the pip of the Newtown Pippin, the apple Jefferson grew, and there it is under the library window, and there they are on the branches, exactly the same apples, gold and green with that russet at the top. I gave one to each guest. Old Coles says he never saw a tree so true.*

Old Coles was in court. Mr Albert Coles, eighty-two, the estate gardener for fifty years, in a brown suit that had been pressed for the occasion, with large red hands folded on the rail of the witness box. He had told the court, the day before, that he remembered it perfectly. He had stood beside his lordship in the spring of 1975 when the pip was planted, in a pot first and then under the library window. He had watered it himself. He had seen it grow from seed. And ten years later he had picked the apples, and they were Newtown Pippins, the real thing, as good as any in America.

He had said it simply, and the judge had written it down, and Frances, sitting at the back, had felt sorry for him in a way she could not yet explain.

Now she stood in the witness box herself. She had brought nothing with her but a folder of photographs and a single apple, which lay on a white napkin on the ledge in front of her.

"Dr Alder," said counsel, "can an apple tree grown from a pip produce the same apples as its parent?"

"No."

"Never?"

"Almost never. I would say never, in practice." She turned to the judge, because judges like to be spoken to. "My Lord, apples are what botanists call heterozygous. It means that each apple tree carries two very different sets of instructions inside it — one from each parent — and when it makes a seed, those instructions are mixed again, like shuffling two packs of cards together. Every pip in every apple is a new individual. Plant the pips from one Newtown Pippin, and you will get fifty different trees, and almost all of them will give you small, hard, sour apples. Crab apples, more or less. Wild apples."

"Then how does anyone ever grow a Newtown Pippin?"

"By grafting. Every Newtown Pippin tree in the world has been made by cutting a small piece of wood from an existing Newtown Pippin tree and joining it onto the root of another tree. The variety travels by wood, not by seed. It has done for centuries. It is the same with every named apple you can buy."

There was a small silence. The nephew's barrister, a pleasant round man, made a note.

"Did you examine the tree under the library window?"

"I did, in September, with the permission of the court."

She opened her folder and held up the first photograph.

"This is the base of the trunk. You can see here, about twenty centimetres above the soil, a swelling — a ring, slightly thicker than the trunk above and below it. That is a graft union. It is the scar where a piece of Newtown Pippin wood was joined onto a root. A tree grown from a pip does not have one. It cannot have one. Nobody grafts a seedling onto itself."

"Could you tell what root it had been joined to?"

"Yes. Below the union the bark is a different colour and texture. The tree is short, about two and a half metres, and it has a wire support beside it, because the roots are weak and shallow. Those are the habits of a dwarfing rootstock. I took a small sample of the root, with permission, and the laboratory at the RHS confirmed it. It is M9." She paused. "M9 was selected at the fruit research station at East Malling, in Kent, early in the last century. It is the standard dwarfing root used by commercial nurseries in this country. In 1975, if you went into any garden centre in Herefordshire and bought a young Newtown Pippin in a pot, it would very probably have been on M9."

"So the tree under the library window—"

"Was bought. From a nursery. As a grafted tree. It was never a pip."

---

She did not look at Mr Coles. She looked at the apple on the napkin, which she had picked from the tree on her second visit, ten days before: gold and green, with a rough russet patch around the stalk, a real Newtown Pippin, sweet and sharp. It was a perfectly honest apple. It had simply been grown in the way all apples are grown, and not in the way the diary said.

The rest of the case moved quickly after that. Once the court knew that one detail in the diary was impossible, it began to look at others. Ink was tested. A date was checked. The handwriting expert, who had been uncertain before, became less uncertain.

On the last day, in the corridor outside Court 26, Frances found Albert Coles sitting alone on a bench, his hands on his knees.

"I didn't mean harm," he said, without looking up. "I raised that boy, you know. Simon. His mother was ill. He was in my potting shed every day of his life till he was eleven. And his lordship — his lordship did talk about a pip. Once. At dinner. Said he'd like to grow one. So when Simon asked me, I thought, well, perhaps he did." He turned his hat round in his red hands. "I bought that tree myself, you know. Nineteen seventy-six. From the nursery on the Ross road. Eleven pounds. I'd forgotten that, till you said about the garden centre."

Frances sat down next to him. She did not say anything for a while. Then she asked him how the tree was keeping, and he told her: well, a bit of canker on the south side, and it wanted a good prune in the winter, and somebody should look after it, whoever got the house.

When she got back to her office above the Lindley Library that evening, she took the apple out of her bag and put it on the windowsill. It sat there in the yellow light from Vincent Square, gold and green and russet, keeping its secret, which was that it had never been a secret at all.
