# Peach Melba at the Savoy

The archive of the Savoy was below the river, or felt as if it was. Dr Lena Ward came down to it each morning by a service lift that smelled of floor polish and hot bread, along a corridor of white tiles where the kitchen porters passed her with trolleys of silver, and through a door marked *Private*, into a long, low, cool room full of boxes. It was always the same temperature. Somewhere above her head, three floors up, people were eating breakfast beside the Thames, and down here the menus of a hundred and thirty years lay in grey cardboard, waiting.

She was a historian of opera and of food, which her colleagues found faintly comic. The two things belonged together. In the 1890s a great singer and a great dinner were the same kind of event: expensive, temporary, and talked about for weeks.

She was looking for Nellie Melba.

Everyone knew the story, or a version of it. The Australian soprano, singing Wagner at Covent Garden, had stayed at the Savoy, where the chef was Auguste Escoffier. Escoffier, who admired her, had made a dessert in her honour: fresh peaches, on vanilla ice cream, served in a swan carved from ice, because of the swan in *Lohengrin*. Later the raspberry purée was added, and the swan was lost, and the dish went round the world on hotel menus and in tins.

Lena was not interested in the version everyone knew. She was interested in the kitchen.

---

The order book for 1895 was a heavy volume bound in green cloth, its pages ruled in red, filled with the brown handwriting of a clerk who had recorded every item that went up from the kitchens for private dining. She had been working through it for a week, at one page an hour, copying quantities into a spreadsheet: so many pounds of butter, so many dozen eggs, so many bottles of this or that.

On the page for the eleventh of July she found a private dinner for eight in the River Room. The guests were not named; only the host's initial, *Mr H.*, and a note: *By order of M. Escoffier. Swan. Pêches.*

Below it, the clerk had listed the ingredients for the dessert. And the numbers were wrong.

Not wrong in the sense of a mistake. Wrong in the sense that no kitchen would order them. Peaches, 18. Vanilla pods, 1. Cream, 13 pints. Sugar, 15 lb. Eggs, 18. Ice for carving, 1 block. Sugar again, 19 lb. Cream again, 20 pints.

Lena had looked at enough orders to know their rhythm. Pastry kitchens order in round numbers, in dozens and half-dozens; they do not order sugar twice for the same dish, or eighteen eggs and eighteen peaches. Then, almost as a joke, because the peaches came first and the peaches were the point, she wrote the alphabet across the top of her notebook and counted.

Eighteen: R. One: A. Thirteen: M. Fifteen: O. Eighteen: R. One: A. Nineteen: S. Twenty: T.

*R A M O R A S T.*

She said it aloud, quietly, to the boxes. Ramorast. It sounded like a name, and it sounded like nobody. There was no Ramorast in the hotel's registers, and none among the famous guests of that summer. Whoever he was, a clerk in a hot kitchen had been told to put him into the book, and had hidden him in the only place a pastry clerk could: in sugar and cream and fruit.

It meant nothing to her. She wrote it down, and went to lunch, and did not eat.

---

It took her a month, and help from a friend in the archive of the Foreign Office, to understand the rest.

There were other pages. In the Savoy's letter books there was a note from a certain *H.* — a junior diplomat, the letters suggested, attached to an embassy in London — thanking the hotel for its *great discretion on the evening of the 11th*. In the diplomatic papers there was a report, dated the twelfth of July, that a sealed dispatch had been received *by private hand* from an agent who could not, at that time, be seen entering an embassy. And in the archive of the Savoy's own engineers, among the bills for repairing the ice-carving room, there was an order for *a small hollow mould, gold-lined, watertight*, signed by the chef.

The swan had not been solid. Inside the ice, packed in the cold beneath the peaches and the vanilla ice cream, there had been a sealed gold case, small enough to close in a hand. The dish was carried in to eight guests with great ceremony. And sometime between the soup and the coffee, as the swan slowly melted on its silver tray, someone at that table had reached into the cold water with a dessert spoon and taken something out.

It was a lovely piece of theatre. Lena was sure that Escoffier, who loved theatre, had enjoyed it.

But it was the name in the numbers that kept her awake. She found it, finally, in a programme from Covent Garden in 1893, two years before the dinner, in small type at the bottom of a cast list: *Mr A. Ramorast, répétiteur.* A rehearsal pianist. The man who sat at the piano in the empty theatre and played the orchestra's part while the singers learned their notes.

In the gossip pages of that year she found the rest, or as much of it as she would ever find. In the spring of 1893 the soprano had been ill, and frightened, and her season had begun badly. There had been talk that her contract would not be renewed. And then something had changed. She had sung *Lohengrin* in a new way, it was said — more simply, more slowly, with a breath in a different place in the second act. Nobody knew who had taught her that. She never said.

Lena thought she understood, then, why his name was in the order book. The dish carried the singer's name; the swan carried the diplomat's secret; and somebody at that dinner — the singer herself, Lena was almost sure, who could not thank a rehearsal pianist in public in a room full of important men — had asked the chef for one more name to be written down that night, where it would last.

Ramorast died in 1897. He had no grave that Lena could find.

---

On her last afternoon in the archive, the hotel's head of communications came down to see her: a pleasant young man in a very good suit, who had read her notes. He said that the hotel was of course delighted. He said that there might perhaps be a small problem with the diplomatic part, which was a long time ago but which involved a country that was still, technically, rather sensitive. He wondered whether the story could be told without it. Simply the dessert. Simply the singer. Simply the pianist.

"The pianist is the only part that matters," Lena said.

"Well, then," he said, smiling, and she could not tell whether he had agreed with her or not.

When he had gone she sat for a while with the green order book open in front of her at the eleventh of July. Eighteen peaches. A man who had played the piano in an empty theatre, for a frightened girl, and who had been paid back, two years later, with eight letters in a kitchen book where nobody would ever look.

The archive was very quiet. She switched off the reading lamp, and in the grey light from the corridor the columns of the clerk's brown numbers faded, one by one, into the page.
