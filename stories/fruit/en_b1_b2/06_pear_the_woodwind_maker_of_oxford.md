# The Woodwind Maker of Oxford

The workshop was in a narrow street behind the covered market, up a flight of stairs that smelled of linseed oil and other people's coffee. Peter Lamb had worked there for twenty-six years. It was a long, low room under the roof, with a window at each end, so that in the morning the light came from the east across the benches and in the afternoon it came from the west, warmer, and showed every scratch. On the walls hung the tools of a trade that had hardly changed in three hundred years: reamers, long and thin as knitting needles; gouges; rasps; little brass gauges for measuring the inside of a bore. In the drawers underneath were the instruments themselves, in pieces, waiting.

Clara Weiss arrived on a wet Thursday in March, with two cases under her coat.

She was a recorder player of about forty, well known in the small world of early music, with short grey hair and the habit, which Peter liked, of holding a case by the handle and the bottom at the same time, as if it were a baby. She put both cases on his bench and opened them.

"I bought this one in Amsterdam, four years ago," she said. "And this one at a house sale in Edinburgh, in January. They're both alto recorders. Both eighteenth century. Both cracked. I want them playable. If they can be."

Peter looked at them for a long moment without touching.

They were a pair, or they looked like one. Each was in three joints — head, middle, foot — turned with the same slightly heavy rings at the joints, the same swelling at the head. Both were stained dark, almost black, in the way that makers of the time had stained fruitwood to look like ebony. And both, where the stain had worn away under the fingers, showed the colour of the wood beneath: a warm, pale, pinkish brown, like the inside of a biscuit.

"Pearwood," he said.

"Not boxwood?"

"Most of them were box, yes. Box is harder. But pear was cheaper, and it turns beautifully, and if you stain it, nobody in a candle-lit room can tell the difference." He picked up the Amsterdam head joint and turned it in the light. "Pear trees were everywhere. A maker could buy a whole trunk from an orchard when it stopped giving fruit."

---

He took them apart that afternoon. It was slow work. Old joints swell and stick over two centuries. He warmed each one gently in his hands, worked it, waited, worked it again. By evening the six pieces lay side by side on a white cloth.

The next morning he began on the cracks. To repair a crack in old wood you must first understand the wood: which way the grain runs, where the growth rings are, where it is weak. He put the Amsterdam foot joint under the microscope.

Pear is a quiet wood. Its rings are faint and close together; it does not shout like oak. But under magnification they were there: fine darker lines curving round the end of the tube, and among them, the tiny marks of old knots, small brown dots where a twig had once grown out from the branch. He sketched the end grain on a card, as he always did, ring by ring, knot by knot. There was a small knot at about two o'clock, another very close beside it, and a ring that ran strangely thick for a short distance, as if the tree had had one good summer.

Then he put the Edinburgh foot joint under the microscope, and stopped breathing for a moment.

The same two knots. The same thick ring. But reversed, as in a mirror.

He went back and forth for an hour. Head joints, middle joints, feet. Each time it was the same. The pattern in one instrument continued into the other, the way the pattern on a cut log continues on both faces of the cut. Somewhere, a long time ago, a maker had taken one piece of pearwood, split it down the middle, and turned two recorders from the two halves.

They were not just a pair. They were one tree.

---

Clara came back on Saturday. He showed her the cards, side by side, and she understood immediately; musicians know about mirrors.

"The same tree," she said. "Can you tell when it was cut?"

"Not from the rings. Pear doesn't work like oak — there's no reference for it. But look at this." He turned the head joint and showed her, just below the window, a small stamp burned into the wood: a crown, a letter S, and a name, half worn away. "That's a London maker. And this was inside the Edinburgh case, under the lining."

It was a paper, folded small, brown at the edges. A bill, in a tidy hand. *To a pair of Concert Flutes, Pear, stained, with Engraved Bells. £2 2s. 0d. Rec'd with thanks. June 1740.*

Clara read it twice. "Flutes," she said softly. "That's what the English called recorders, then." Peter watched her face, which was quiet in a way he did not quite understand.

"There's something else," he said. "The engraving."

Round the bell of each foot joint, under the dark stain, ran a narrow band of engraved lines. On one instrument they looked like decoration: five straight lines, with small marks between them. On the other, the same. He had not thought much of it until he laid them on the cloth with the mirrored grain matching, bell to bell, the way the tree had grown.

Then the lines met. Five lines, running unbroken from one bell onto the other. A stave. And on the stave, small and neat, a clef and eight bars of notes, in two voices, and at the end, three letters cut very small into the wood: *G.F.H.*

Neither of them spoke for some time. Outside, a bicycle bell rang in the street.

"Handel was in London in 1740," Clara said at last. Her voice was careful. "He wrote for recorders. He wrote duets. But I don't know these bars. I've played everything there is, and I don't know them."

"It could be a copy of something lost. Or a joke. Or a maker's fancy." Peter heard himself say it, as a professional must. "It will need people who know more than I do."

"Yes." She touched the Edinburgh foot joint with one finger. "This one belonged to my teacher. In Edinburgh. I didn't tell you. He died in November, and his sister sold the house, and I bought it back at the sale, because I couldn't bear for anyone else to have it." She did not look up. "He spent forty years looking for its other half. He used to say it was out there somewhere, waiting. He thought I was being sentimental when I bought the Amsterdam one. He said it couldn't possibly be."

Peter said nothing. There is nothing useful to say to that.

"If this is real," she said, "they'll want them in a museum. Behind glass."

"Probably."

"They're instruments. They were made to be played. Together." She looked at him then, and he saw what she was asking, and also that she would not ask it aloud.

He mended them over the next six weeks, carefully, with the old methods and a little hide glue, as well as he had ever mended anything. On the last afternoon, in the warm western light, he put them together, head to middle to foot, and laid them in their two cases side by side.

Then he closed the lids, one and then the other, and listened to the small sound the old brass clasps made, both the same.
