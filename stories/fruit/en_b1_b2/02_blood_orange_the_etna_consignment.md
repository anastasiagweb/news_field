# The Etna Consignment

The fog came up the river at Wapping a little after three, as it did most afternoons that February, and by four o'clock Thomas Reed could no longer see the far side of the warehouse. The lamps along the gallery made yellow circles in the air. Beneath them, between columns of black iron, the stock of half the Mediterranean was stacked in rows: sacks of almonds, barrels of Marsala, bales of cork, and, against the river wall, the ruined cargo of the steamship *Santa Agata*, eleven days out of Catania.

Reed had been a marine insurance investigator for nineteen years. He worked for an office in Leadenhall Street that insured ships, and the things inside them, and the people who swore those things had been lost. In nineteen years he had learned that every cargo is a story, and that the job is not to believe the story or to disbelieve it, but to check it, one line at a time.

The story of the *Santa Agata* was simple. She had loaded at Catania: four hundred crates of oranges and eight cases of antique Sicilian lace, very fine, the property of a Mr Calloway of Bond Street. Off Cape Finisterre she had met heavy weather. Seawater had come into the forward hold. The oranges had survived, as oranges do. The lace had not. Eight cases of old lace, valued at nine thousand pounds, were now a grey, salty paste. Mr Calloway's claim lay on Reed's desk in Leadenhall Street, typed on good paper.

The lace was in front of him now, in its opened cases. It was certainly ruined. It was also, he thought, rather ordinary lace for nine thousand pounds, but that was not something he could prove by looking unhappy at it.

"You want the oranges as well, sir?" said the foreman, a large, patient man named Dobbs, who had worked on that wharf since before the war.

"I want the oranges."

They went down into the cold at the river end, where the crates stood six high. Each crate was stencilled *TAROCCO — CATANIA — S. AGATA* in black. Dobbs opened the first with a short iron bar. Inside, the fruit lay in neat layers, each orange wrapped in a square of thin paper. Reed took one out, unwrapped it, and cut it in half with his pocket knife.

The flesh was streaked with red. Not the full dark red of a Moro, but the true *Tarocco* colour: gold with veins and flames of ruby running through it, like a sunset seen through cloud. The juice ran over his fingers and smelled of winter.

He had grown up in a house where oranges arrived once a year, at Christmas, and he had never lost his respect for them.

"Another," he said. "From the back."

Dobbs opened a crate from the second stack. Reed cut. Gold. Pale gold, all the way through, without a single thread of red.

He cut six more from that stack. All pale. He went back to the first stack and cut four more. All red.

He stood for a while with the knife in one hand and a pale half-orange in the other.

The red in a blood orange is not a matter of the tree. It is a matter of the nights. The colour — the chemists called it anthocyanin — gathers in the flesh only when warm days are followed by cold nights, again and again, through the last weeks before picking. On the slopes of Etna, where the air falls cold from the snow at sunset, the oranges blush. Put the same trees in a warm coastal valley, where the nights stay soft, and they give you fine, sweet, pale fruit that nobody would call blood oranges at all.

And Reed knew — because the fruit importers had been talking about it at every lunch in the City for a month — that this winter on Etna had been hard. Frost on the lower groves. Snow down to the villages. Every Catanian *Tarocco* sold at Covent Garden that season had been as red inside as a cut finger.

"Dobbs," he said, "what does the paper say?"

The foreman held one of the wrappers up to the lamp. The red-fleshed oranges had been wrapped in thin paper printed with a blue cartwheel and the words *Agrumi di Sicilia*. The pale ones were wrapped in paper of the same weight, almost the same colour. But the printing was different. A palm tree. And under it, in small French letters: *Bône*.

Bône was in Algeria. A warm port. A warm valley behind it, full of orange trees.

---

It took Reed two more days and a great many telephone calls to put the rest together. The bills of lading said that the *Santa Agata* had loaded at Catania on the ninth of January. The Lloyd's agent in Catania, when he finally answered his telephone, said that she had not been in Catania since November. The ship's log had a neat entry for the ninth — *Wind NW, moderate, loading cargo* — in handwriting that was neater than every other page in the book. The coal account, which nobody ever bothered to forge, showed that she had bought coal in Bône on the eighth.

It was not difficult, in the end, to see the shape of it. Somebody had bought fifty crates of genuine Sicilian oranges on the market in Bône, which were easy to buy in any Mediterranean port, and stacked them at the front, where an inspector would look. Behind them, three hundred and fifty crates of local fruit, with the stencils painted on in Algeria. And eight cases of cheap lace, which would never be real Sicilian lace in a London court, because they would be destroyed first by water poured carefully into the forward hold.

Mr Calloway was a dealer who owed money in three countries. That was not a surprise.

The surprise was the first mate.

His name was Harold Pike. Reed met him in the office in Leadenhall Street on a wet Thursday. Pike was forty, thin, with very clean hands and a wedding ring that he turned while he talked. He had written the false entry in the log. He did not deny it. He said, very politely, that the captain had asked him to, and that the captain had been good to him for twelve years.

"He's sixty-three, Mr Reed. The owners were going to put him ashore in the spring. No pension. Mr Calloway said there'd be something for him. For his wife."

"And for you?"

Pike looked at his hands. "My boy's in the sanatorium at Midhurst. His chest." He said it simply, as a fact, like a position on a chart. "They don't keep you for nothing."

Reed wrote it down, because it was his job to write things down. He also wrote down the coal account, the wrapping paper, the palm tree, the name *Bône*. He knew which of those things the court would care about.

When Pike had gone, Reed sat for a long time at his desk. The fog had come in from the river again and the window was white. On the blotter in front of him lay one of the pale oranges from the back of the stack. He had brought it with him as evidence, wrapped in its paper with the little palm tree, and had not yet put it in an envelope.

It was a good orange. Sweet, and perfectly honest about where it had grown. It had simply never felt a cold night.

He did not eat it. He sat and looked at it until the lamps came on in the street below.
