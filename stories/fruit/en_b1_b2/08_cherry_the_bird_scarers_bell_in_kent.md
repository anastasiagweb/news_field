# The Bird-Scarer's Bell in Kent

The tower stood in the exact middle of the orchard, and from the top of it, on a clear evening in late June, Anna Marsh could see the whole of the old Gage estate laid out below her like a map: the farmhouse with its tall chimneys, the oast house with its white cowl, the church on its low hill a mile to the east, and all around, in long green rows, the cherry trees.

They were *Napoleon Bigarreau*, most of them — the old Kentish cherry, pale yellow with a red blush, sweet and firm, the kind that nobody grew any more because the trees were too tall to pick without ladders. Some of them were sixty feet high. In the hot weeks of early summer the fruit hung in them like lamps.

The tower was why Anna was there. She was an architect who worked on old buildings, the kind that are too small for anyone to notice and too old for anyone to replace: barns, dovecotes, granaries, toll houses. The cherry watcher's tower was a square wooden platform on four oak legs, with a roof like a hat and a ladder up the middle, built — the estate papers said — in 1868. For a hundred years, a boy had sat up here every day from dawn to dusk while the cherries ripened, pulling ropes.

The ropes still ran out from the platform to the trees. There were twelve of them, thick old hemp lines, replaced many times, which went out over the orchard to the tops of the twelve oldest trees. At the end of each line, high in the canopy, hung a brass bell. When the boy pulled a rope, the bell rang, and the starlings rose shrieking from the branches and went off to steal someone else's fruit.

"The trees have been replaced twice since my great-grandfather's time," Mr Gage had told her, on the first morning. He was nearly ninety and walked with two sticks. "But the lines never changed. My father was very particular. Same twelve places. Same twelve bells. You plant the new tree in the old hole, he used to say, and the bell goes back where it was."

---

She began by measuring. That was always how she began. A building tells you most of its story through its measurements, if you are patient: where the builder was careful, where he hurried, where he changed his mind.

She measured the platform, the legs, the roof. Then, because the ropes were part of the structure, she measured the directions of the ropes with a compass and a surveyor's level, and plotted them that evening on her laptop in the farmhouse kitchen.

They were not random.

She had expected them to go simply to the twelve tallest trees, at whatever angles the trees happened to stand. Instead they spread out from the tower in a strange, precise fan. When she checked them against the sky, using the program she used for planning the light in new rooms, she found that the line to the north-east pointed exactly at the place on the horizon where the sun rose on Midsummer Day. The line to the south-east pointed at the midwinter sunrise. Others marked the equinoxes, the rising of the moon at its farthest point, the setting of the sun in the west on the shortest day.

Someone in 1868 had used the orchard as a calendar.

And one line was different from all the others. It ran almost due east, towards the church on its hill, and when she checked its angle, it matched the sunrise on the twenty-ninth of June. The feast of St Peter. The church was St Peter's.

At the end of that line stood the tallest tree in the orchard.

---

She climbed it the next morning, with a harness, a rope, and one of the estate men holding the ladder at the bottom. It was a long climb. The leaves closed around her, cool and green, and the starlings complained, and the cherries brushed against her face, warm already from the sun, so ripe that some of them split when she touched them.

The bell hung from a thick branch near the top, on an iron hook. It was the same size and shape as the others, and like the others it was painted a dull bronze-green, flaking a little. She tapped it gently with her pencil, the way she tapped walls to find the hollow places.

It did not sound like brass.

Brass rings with a short, bright, slightly ugly note, like a shop door. This sound was higher, cleaner, and it went on and on, thinning slowly into the air, long after it should have stopped. She tapped again. The same long, pure note.

She took out her penknife and scraped, very carefully, at a place on the inside of the rim where the paint had already lifted. Under the green there was black. Under the black, where her knife touched, a thin bright line of white metal.

Silver.

When they brought it down that afternoon and laid it on a blanket on the kitchen table, it was heavier than any bell of that size should be. Under the paint, around the shoulder, there was engraving — a pattern of leaves, and a pair of crossed keys, the sign of St Peter. On the inside, there were small fixing holes where something had once been fastened. It had never been a bell at all. It was the cup of a reliquary, a church vessel for holding something sacred, sixteenth century, turned upside down and given a clapper.

"Ah," said Mr Gage, when he saw it. He sat down very slowly in his chair.

The story came out over the next week, from the parish records and from a box of papers in the attic. During the Civil War, in the 1640s, when soldiers were breaking the windows and the statues in the churches of Kent, someone at St Peter's had taken the reliquary and hidden it on the Gage land, perhaps in a wall, perhaps in the ground. It had stayed hidden for two hundred years. Then in the 1860s, when the orchard was replanted, Mr Gage's great-grandfather had found it — and, being a man who had read his history and who did not like vicars, he had said nothing. He had painted it green, hung it in the tallest tree, and pointed the line at the church on its own saint's day, where anyone could have seen it, and nobody did.

"My father knew," said Mr Gage. "He told me when I was a boy. I promised I'd never take it down." He looked at the window. "I suppose I thought, if I said nothing, I'd never have to decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether it was ours or theirs." He nodded towards the church. "And whether I minded."

Anna did not answer. She had no answer, and it was not her place. She wrote her report, about the tower, which needed new oak in two of its legs and a new floor. In the last paragraph she wrote, plainly, what she had found in the tree, and where it was now.

On her last evening she climbed the tower once more. The sun was going down behind the farmhouse and the orchard was full of gold light and the noise of birds. The eleven ropes ran out from the platform to their eleven trees. The twelfth hung loose.

A small wind came across the orchard from the east, from the hill, and somewhere among the leaves the eleven brass bells rang together, short and bright and slightly ugly, and the starlings rose.
