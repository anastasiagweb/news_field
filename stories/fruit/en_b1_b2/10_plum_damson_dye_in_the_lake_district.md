# Damson Dye in the Lake District

In the Lyth Valley in April the damson trees flower before they have leaves, so that for two weeks the whole valley looks as if someone has thrown a thin white sheet over the hedges and left it there to dry. Dr Isobel Fenn drove down into it from Kendal on a cold, bright morning, with the heater on and her equipment in two metal cases on the back seat, and thought, as she always did when she came to the Lakes, that the landscape was too beautiful to be entirely honest.

She was a specialist in historic textiles. People brought her old cloth — flags, christening gowns, uniforms, the coats of famous men — and asked her the same question in a hundred different ways. *Is it what we hope it is?* Usually the answer was *no*. Sometimes it was *we cannot know*. Very rarely it was *yes*, and those were the days she remembered.

The farm was at the head of the valley, a long grey house with a slate roof and a yard full of mud and sheepdogs. Jean Dixon met her at the door. She was in her fifties, tired, polite, with the careful voice of someone who had rehearsed what she was going to say.

"It was in my grandmother's chest," she said. "In the back bedroom. We were clearing it to sell. The farm, I mean." She did not explain any further.

The chest stood on the kitchen table. Inside, folded in yellowed paper, was a cloak: a long, heavy travelling cloak of coarse wool, with a deep collar and a single horn button at the neck. The colour was hard to describe. In the light from the window it was a dark plum-grey, almost black, with a faint warm purple where the folds had kept it from the sun.

Beside it was a small notebook in a leather cover, its pages filled with pencil. Isobel opened it with gloved hands. *Walked over from Grasmere by Dunmail. Snow on the tops. W. very silent all day, then talked without stopping from Wythburn to Keswick.* And on the inside of the cover, in ink: *Wm Wordsworth his cloak. Given to J. D. for her kindness.*

"My grandmother always said it was his," said Jean. "The poet's. Daffodils." She gave a short laugh with no pleasure in it. "There's a dealer in London who says, if it's true, it could be worth more than the farm."

---

Isobel spent two days with the cloak in the cold back room of the farmhouse, with a lamp, a magnifier, and the small kit she used for taking fibre samples.

The wool was local. She was almost sure of that from the first hour. It was the coarse, springy wool of the fell sheep, hand-spun, the thread uneven, woven on a narrow loom. The dye took longer. She took three tiny samples from inside the seams, where nothing would show, and sent them by courier to the laboratory in York. On the second evening the results came back by email, and she sat at Jean's kitchen table and read them twice.

Anthocyanins. Tannins. Aluminium.

Damson. Damson juice, boiled with alum to fix the colour to the wool. The alum turned the red of the fruit into that deep, cool purple-grey, the colour of the fells under rain. It was an old country recipe, from a valley where every hedge was full of damsons and nobody had money for indigo from India or cochineal from Mexico. The fruit was free. The colour was the valley's own.

"So it's local," said Jean. "It's from here."

"Almost certainly. I'd say the cloth was dyed somewhere in this valley, or very near it, probably in the first half of the nineteenth century."

"Then it's his."

Isobel put the paper down. This was always the moment. She had learned, over twenty years, that the kindest thing was also the hardest: to say exactly what she knew, and no more.

"It means the cloak was made here," she said. "It doesn't mean Wordsworth wore it. A hundred farmers in this valley might have had a cloak exactly like this one. The writing in the notebook could be his — or a visitor's — or your great-great-grandmother's, telling a story. I can tell you what the cloth is. I can't tell you whose shoulders it was on."

Jean looked at the window for a long time.

"The dealer said you'd say that," she said finally. "He said the experts always say that."

"He's right," said Isobel. "We do."

---

It was on the third morning, packing up, that she found the rest.

She was folding the cloak for the last time, and she turned the collar up to look once more at the inside of it, under the raking light of her lamp held low against the cloth. The collar was thicker than the rest of the cloak, lined with a second layer of the same wool, and the dye there was heavier, almost black, as if it had been dipped twice. In the flat light of the window it was simply dark.

In the low, side light of the lamp, it was not.

Across the inner surface of the collar, small and close, ran lines of stitching in dark silk — almost exactly the colour of the dyed wool, so that they could not be seen at all, only felt as a faint roughness under the fingertip, or seen as tiny shadows when the light came from the side. Isobel moved the lamp slowly along the collar, the way she had been taught, and the shadows became a shape.

A line of hills. A stream, marked with a wavy line. A small square, and next to it, a cross. And letters, so small she needed the magnifier: *Fold by the Pass. Third stone from the door. Low.*

"Jean," she said. "Come and look at this."

---

They went up on the Saturday, in borrowed boots, with Jean's son, who knew the fells. The pass was three hours above the farm, past the last wall, past the last tree, into a wide brown place where the wind came over the tops and pushed at them like a hand. The fold was where the map said it would be: a ruined shepherd's hut of grey stones, without a roof, with a sheep pen beside it.

Third stone from the door. Low. It came out easily. Behind it, in a dry space in the wall, was a small tin box, very rusty, tied with string.

Inside there were no poems. There were letters — six of them, folded small — written in pencil, in a woman's hand, to a man named John, who had gone to work in the mills at Kendal and had not come back. *I have dyed the cloth with the damsons from the top hedge as you liked*, said one. *It will keep you dry on the road. Come home over the pass and I will know you by it.*

Jean read them all, sitting on a stone in the wind. Isobel did not interrupt her.

"Not Wordsworth, then," said Jean at last.

"No," said Isobel. "I don't think so."

Jean folded the letters back into the tin, carefully, along their old lines. Below them the valley lay in the spring light, white with damson flower from one end to the other. She sat and looked at it for a long time, holding the box in her lap, and did not say what she was going to do about the farm.
