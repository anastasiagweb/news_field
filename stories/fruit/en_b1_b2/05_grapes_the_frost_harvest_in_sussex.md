# The Frost Harvest in Sussex

The frost came down on the last night of October, exactly as Ruth Ashdown had known it would. At two o'clock she had stood at her bedroom window in her husband's old jumper and watched the white spread slowly down the hill in the moonlight, like milk poured over a tilted plate.

Cold air is heavy. It slides. On the chalk of the South Downs it pours off the top of the slope after dark and gathers at the bottom, in the hollow by the stream, where the oldest fields of the estate had always been the last to ripen and the first to freeze.

The lower field was the one that mattered this year. It held two rows of the new clone — a Pinot Noir selected, after nine years of work, to ripen in the short English season. Ruth had planned to pick it at first light, before the frost could do much harm. Two hundred vines. Nine years.

At six, when she walked down with her torch and her secateurs, the press house door was open.

---

Inside, the cold smelled of crushed fruit and wet concrete. The old basket press stood in the middle of the floor where it always stood, but its steel had been washed and was still wet. The hose had not been coiled. In the stone trough below the press, where the juice ran out into the collecting tank, there was a thin layer of pomace — skins, stems, broken berries — that nobody had cleaned away. And on the shelf by the door, where the laboratory journal for the new clone had sat for nine years, there was a clean rectangle in the dust.

She stood very still in the doorway. She did not need to think about who had keys.

Daniel Pryce arrived at nine, in his good jacket, carrying coffee for both of them, as he did on harvest days. He had been her consultant for twelve years, and her husband's friend for longer. When Tom died, three winters ago, it was Daniel who had come every weekend to help with the pruning. It was Daniel who had found the investors, this summer: a group from London who wanted to buy into the estate and plant forty more hectares of the new clone, if the figures were good.

"Ruth. I heard about the frost. I'm so sorry."

"Someone pressed fruit here last night," she said. "And the clone journal's gone."

He put the coffee down on the bench. His face showed only concern, which was the expression she had seen on it most often in twelve years.

"Gone? I took it home on Friday. I was writing up the figures for London. I have them here." He opened his bag and took out a printed sheet. "Look. The clone reached twenty-one Brix on the lower field on Thursday. Twenty-one, Ruth, in England, in a cold year. Acid falling nicely. That's ripe fruit. That's the whole case."

She looked at the sheet. Brix is a measure of the sugar in grape juice; you read it with a refractometer, a little tube with a prism inside that bends the light. The new clone, on Thursday, when she had tested it herself, had reached seventeen and a half. She had written it down. It was in the journal.

"I didn't record twenty-one," she said.

"Your sample must have been from the shaded end. I tested again in the afternoon."

He said it kindly, as you would to someone who was tired. She had been tired for three years.

"And the investors' laboratory wanted juice as well as figures," Daniel went on. "That was me last night, I'm afraid — I should have rung you. I picked a few bunches at dusk, before the frost came down, and pressed them. The courier collects the sample at ten. I tested it at home: twenty-four Brix. Even better than the field."

Ruth looked at him for a moment. Then she went to the cupboard behind the door and took out her own refractometer, the one that had been Tom's.

---

She knelt by the stone trough. The pomace there was cold and dark, and in the low places of the stone, under the skins, a little juice had gathered and not drained away. She dipped the end of a glass rod into it and put a drop on the prism. She closed the cover, held the tube up to the light from the doorway, and looked through it.

The line between light and dark fell at twenty-four.

She did not say anything. She took a second drop, from the other end of the trough, and a third. Twenty-four. Twenty-four and a half. Then she took a small sample in a jar, carried it to the bench, and tested the acid with the titration kit, slowly, drop by drop, watching the colour change.

The acid was very high. Higher than she had ever seen in that field.

She put the jar down.

"Daniel," she said, "do you know what happens when you press frozen grapes?"

He did not answer.

"The water in the berries turns to ice first. The sugar and the acid don't freeze so easily. So when you press, the ice stays behind in the skins, in the press, and what runs out is everything else — concentrated. Sweeter. And sharper." She held up the jar. "Ripeness raises the sugar and brings the acid down. Frost raises both. Your sample for London says twenty-four. My juice in this trough says twenty-four. And the acid is through the roof. That isn't ripe fruit. That's seventeen-and-a-half fruit that froze last night at the bottom of the hill and was pressed in the dark before it could thaw."

The press house was very quiet.

"You didn't pick at dusk. You came down at three in the morning," Ruth said. "You picked the coldest rows. You pressed them so you'd have a sweet sample for the laboratory. Then you wrote twenty-one on your sheet, with the acid falling, because that's what ripe fruit is supposed to look like. You knew the frost would make the juice sweet. You forgot it would make it sour as well."

For a long time Daniel looked at the old press, still wet from his washing.

"They would have walked away," he said at last. "Without the figures. You'd have sold to the people from Brighton by Christmas. They'd have pulled up Tom's vines and put in a car park for weddings." His voice was quite steady. "I couldn't watch that, Ruth. Not after everything. I was going to make it true later. In a warm year the clone might actually —"

"I know," she said. And she did know, which was the worst part. She knew he had done it for her, and for Tom, and for the vines, and also for the fee the investors had promised him, and for his name on the planting plan for forty hectares. All of it at once. People are like that. Grapes are simpler.

"Bring the journal back this afternoon," she said. "I'll telephone London myself."

When he had gone, she stayed in the press house. The sun was coming over the top of the Downs now, and the frost on the lower field was beginning to go, from white to grey to dark wet green.

In the corner of the trough, missed by the press, lay a single whole berry, black and dusty-blue. She picked it up. It was still hard with ice. She held it in her open palm and watched it soften slowly in the warmth of her hand, until it was only a grape again.
