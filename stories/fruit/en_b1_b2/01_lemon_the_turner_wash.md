# The Turner Wash

At ten o'clock on the night before the sale, the conservation studio at the top of the building in Mayfair was the only room still lit. Below it, five floors of carpet and polished wood waited in the dark for the morning, for the dealers and the telephones and the quiet men from the museums. Up here there were only the long white tables, the smell of wheat-starch paste and old paper, and the radiator ticking as it cooled.

Julian Asher had worked on paper for thirty-one years. He knew that most of the secrets in a watercolour were not hidden at all. They were simply waiting for someone to look slowly.

Lot 47 lay on the table in front of him, out of its frame. *Circle of Joseph Mallord William Turner. Margate, the Sea from a Window, c. 1840. Watercolour on wove paper.* Estimate: eight to twelve thousand pounds. It was a small sheet, a little larger than a letter. A pale grey sky dissolved into a paler grey sea; in the corner, a window frame, just a few strokes of brown; and in the middle, a single smear of yellow where the sun was trying to come through. It was beautiful in the way that a breath on cold glass is beautiful. It was also, according to the catalogue, by nobody in particular.

The catalogue entry had been written by Marcus Hale, the head of the department, who had gone home at six with his coat over his arm and a hand on Julian's shoulder. *Just the condition report, Julian. Nothing heroic.*

The condition report was finished. What kept Julian at the table was the file.

It had come with the painting from the seller, an old woman in Hampshire whose husband had died in the spring. Inside were insurance letters, a photograph from 1962, and three pages of notes in her husband's small, careful handwriting. Most of it was about the frame. But on the last page, almost at the bottom, he had written: *My grandfather always said there was a dedication on the margin, written in lemon. He would never let anyone warm it.*

Under the line, in soft pencil, someone had made a small tick.

Julian knew that tick. He saw it every day on the edges of catalogue drafts. Marcus ticked things when he had read them.

He sat back. Lemon juice was the oldest invisible ink in the world; children used it, and so did spies. You wrote with it, it dried to nothing, and when you held the paper near a flame the acid made the fibres burn a little faster than the rest, and the words came up brown. It was a lovely trick. On a possible Turner, twelve hours before a sale, it was unthinkable. Heat would mark the sheet for ever. You could not un-brown paper.

But acid changes paper in other ways too. It attacks the fibres slowly, quietly, over a hundred and eighty years, and paper that has been touched by it does not answer light in the same way as paper that has not.

Julian switched off the overhead lights. The studio became a dark box with the city glowing faintly orange at the windows. He took the ultraviolet lamp from its drawer, put on the yellow glasses, and turned it on. The lamp hummed. A cold violet light fell across the sheet.

Under ultraviolet, old paper glows. This sheet glowed a soft, milky blue, like skimmed milk in a glass. The watercolour went dark and flat. The little window frame became a black shape. Julian moved the lamp slowly along the lower margin, then the left, then the top, the way his first teacher had taught him: never quickly, never hoping, just looking.

Along the right margin, the blue was not quite even.

He held the lamp still. There, in the glow, were darker lines. They were faint, brownish, broken in places, like the marks a wet thread leaves on a tablecloth. But they had a rhythm, and the rhythm was handwriting. A capital letter with a long, impatient tail. A small word. A line under it.

He moved closer and read it in pieces, the way you read a name on a gravestone.

*For Mrs B. — with thanks for the window. W.T.*

Julian turned off the lamp and sat in the dark for a while.

He knew, as everyone in his profession knew, that in his last years Turner had stayed in Margate in the house of a widow who kept lodgings, a woman with a view of the sea. He knew that the old man had painted from that window again and again. And he knew what a dedication in Turner's own hand would do to an attribution. *Circle of* would become nothing. Just the name. Eight thousand pounds would become three hundred thousand, perhaps more, by lunchtime tomorrow.

He also knew, now, that Marcus had read the same line in the same notes and had written *Circle of*.

There were simple explanations. Marcus might have thought the story was family nonsense; most family stories are. He might have meant to come back to it. But Julian had worked beside Marcus for eleven years, and he had seen the name of one particular dealer on the list of people invited to view Lot 47 privately, on Tuesday, before the public view. A dealer who was also Marcus's oldest friend. A dealer who bought things that were catalogued too cheaply, and sold them again, a year later, in New York, correctly.

Julian thought about the old woman in Hampshire. He had spoken to her once, on the telephone, about insurance. She had said, *My husband loved that little sea. I can't look at it now.* She needed the money for a nursing home. She had not said that, but he had heard it.

He thought, too, about himself. He was fifty-six. Marcus had promised him the senior post when Marcus retired next year. Marcus had said it in the pub, warmly, twice.

At midnight he switched the lamp on again, set the camera on the copy stand, and photographed the margin under ultraviolet, eleven times, with a scale bar and the lot number in each frame. He wrote a short note in plain language: what he had read in the file, what he had done, what he had not done. *No heat applied. Examined under UV only.* He printed the best photograph and clipped it to the note.

Then he hesitated over the last line. He could write *For the attention of Marcus Hale.* That would be correct. It would also give the whole thing back to Marcus, to decide in the morning, quietly, before anyone else arrived.

He wrote instead: *Copy to the Chairman. Copy to the seller.*

He read it twice. It did not sound brave. It sounded like a condition report, which was what it was.

He put the watercolour back into its folder and laid the folder in the plan chest, in drawer seven, where it belonged. He placed the note on Marcus's desk, squarely, in the middle, where nobody could miss it or say they had.

Outside, a taxi went down Bond Street with its light on, looking for someone. The radiator had stopped ticking. The studio smelled, as it always did at this hour, of paste and dust and cold.

Julian took off the yellow glasses, folded them, and put them in his top pocket. Then he reached for the ultraviolet lamp. For a second its violet light lay across the empty table, showing every small fibre and scratch on the white surface, everything that had ever been done there.

He switched it off.
