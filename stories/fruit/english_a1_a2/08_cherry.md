# The Scent of Bitter Almonds

The library was warm and dark. Only two lamps were on, and the fire. Mr Ashworth sat in his big leather chair beside it, with his head back and his mouth a little open. On the small table at his elbow stood a glass. A little dark red liquid was still in the bottom.

On a gold chain across his waistcoat hung a small key.

Miss Hearne sat on the other side of the fire with a book of old plant drawings on her knees. She was seventy-eight. She had been a botanist at Kew for forty years. She did not hear well any more, but she saw everything.

Philip came in from the hall. Mr Ashworth's nephew. Thirty, handsome, with soft hands. He walked across to his uncle's chair.

"Uncle Gerald?"

No answer.

Philip bent over the chair. He bent lower. He picked up the glass and smelled it. Then he stood up very fast.

"Almonds," he said. "Bitter almonds. Oh God. That's cyanide. Somebody's poisoned him."

Miss Hearne closed her book.

"Get a doctor," said Philip. His voice was loud now, very loud. "Get everybody. The telephone in the hall. Dr Lyle in the village, somebody take the car. Everybody out. Go. Now—"

"Philip," said Miss Hearne.

"Didn't you hear me? He's been poisoned!"

"He has been drinking Cherry Bounce," said Miss Hearne. "Sit down."

She stood up, slowly, and came across the carpet. She took the glass from his hand. She held it under her nose.

"Yes," she said. "Almonds. Of course almonds. Do you know how Cherry Bounce is made, Philip? No. You don't. It's an old recipe. Seventeen hundreds. Your uncle's grandmother made it, and her grandmother. You take black cherries from Kent, in July. You put them in a jar with sugar and brandy. And you break some of the stones with a hammer, the hard stones from the middle of the cherries, and you put them in the jar too. Then you wait. Months."

She turned the glass in the lamplight. The liquid was the colour of a dark ruby.

"Inside every cherry stone there is a little seed. It smells of bitter almonds. The almond tree and the cherry tree are cousins, you see. Same family. Same smell. The smell goes into the brandy. That's why people love it." She put the glass back on the little table. "Your uncle drinks Cherry Bounce every Sunday night. This library has smelled of almonds every Sunday for thirty years."

Philip looked at his uncle.

Mr Ashworth breathed in, slowly. Breathed out, with a small noise. In. Out.

"He's asleep," said Miss Hearne. "That's all."

"He—" Philip stopped. "He doesn't wake up."

"No. He doesn't." She looked at Philip for the first time. Really looked, the way she looked at a leaf under a glass. "And that is interesting, isn't it? Your uncle sleeps in his chair every Sunday. Ten minutes, usually. Then a door closes, or the fire moves, and he wakes up and says, 'I wasn't asleep.' Tonight you shouted in his ear. You shouted very loud. He didn't move."

Philip said nothing.

"And here is another interesting thing," said Miss Hearne. "You came in. You went to him. You didn't shake him. You didn't touch his face. You didn't say, 'Uncle, are you ill?' You picked up the glass and you said, 'Poison.' And then you said, 'Everybody out.'"

She looked down at the gold chain on the old man's waistcoat. At the small key.

"The key to his desk," she said. "He never takes it off. And his new will is in that desk. I know, because I signed it on Tuesday. And you know, because you were listening at the door."

The fire moved. A log turned over. Mr Ashworth did not wake.

"Something in his coffee, I expect," said Miss Hearne. "Something to make him sleep very deeply for an hour. Nothing to hurt him. You're not a killer, Philip. You're not brave enough. You only needed ten minutes alone in this room."

Philip's face was white. "You can't prove—"

"No," she said. "Probably not."

She picked up the glass of Cherry Bounce. She looked at him over the top of it.

Then she drank it, slowly, to the bottom.
