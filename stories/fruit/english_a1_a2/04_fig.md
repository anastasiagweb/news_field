# The White Drop on the Glove

The morning room was full of sun. On the table: a pot of tea, a jug of milk, a plate of toast that nobody had eaten.

Mr Julian Ferrars sat in the chair by the window. He was ready to leave. Brown travelling coat. His car was outside on the drive. On his hands, a pair of grey gloves. Suede, soft as the skin of a young deer.

"I really must go by ten, Inspector," he said. "I've told you everything. I was in the drawing room until eleven. Then I went up to bed. I didn't go outside. Nobody went outside. It was raining."

"It stopped at half past eleven," said Inspector Wood.

"Did it? I was asleep."

Inspector Wood sat down opposite him. He was a big, slow man with careful hands. He had been in the house since seven o'clock. He had seen the library on the first floor, with the window open and the glass case empty. A gold snuffbox, French, with a picture of a lady on the lid. Small enough for a pocket. Worth more than the roof of this house.

He had talked to six guests. All six said the same thing. Drawing room. Bed. Nobody went outside.

Mr Ferrars was the seventh.

"Nice gloves," said the inspector.

"Thank you. Jermyn Street."

"There's a mark on the left one."

Mr Ferrars looked down. On the back of the left glove, near the wrist, there was a spot. Small. White. Like a drop of cream.

"Milk," he said. "From breakfast. I'm a clumsy man with a jug." He smiled. "May I go now?"

"May I see?"

Mr Ferrars did not want to. But a man who will not give you his glove looks worse than a man who will. He pulled it off, finger by finger, and gave it to the inspector.

Inspector Wood put the glove on his knee, in the sun from the window. He looked at the white spot.

Then he touched it with his finger.

Milk dries in a few minutes. Dry milk is a thin, pale powder. You touch it, and it falls off. A little white dust.

This was not dust.

The spot was sticky. When the inspector lifted his finger, the spot came up with it. A thin white thread, like a hair made of rubber, between the glove and his skin. It stretched, and stretched, and then broke.

He pressed the suede next to the spot. Soft. He pressed the suede under the spot. Hard. Stiff, like old glue.

"That's not milk," he said.

Mr Ferrars said nothing.

"Do you know the south wall of this house, Mr Ferrars? The old brick wall under the library window?"

"I've never looked at it."

"There's a tree on it. A fig tree. Very old, the gardener says. Older than him. Its branches go all the way up the wall, to the library window. Thick branches. A man could stand on them. A man could climb them, in the dark, after the rain stops."

"I'm sure he could."

"Fig trees are full of milk," said the inspector. "Not real milk. A white juice. Break a leaf, break a little branch, and it comes out. White, like milk. But when it dries, it doesn't turn to powder. It turns to rubber." He held up his finger. A tiny piece of the white thread was still on it. "Like this."

Outside, a car door closed. Somebody laughed on the drive.

"This morning I went out to the south wall," the inspector said. "Halfway up the fig tree there's a broken leaf. Big and green, with five fingers, like a hand. Broken off at the stalk. And on the end of the stalk there is a white drop. Dry now. Rubber."

He lifted the glove to his face and breathed in.

Sun. Old suede. And under it, something else. Green and sweet, like summer, like a garden after rain. The smell of a fig leaf. Nothing else in the world smells like that. Not milk. Not tea. Not toast.

He put the glove down on the table between them, next to the milk jug.

"Your coat, Mr Ferrars," he said. "The inside pocket. Or shall I ask the gardener for his ladder?"

Mr Ferrars looked at the glove for a long time. Then, slowly, he put his right hand, still in its grey glove, inside his coat.
