# The Bruise on the Royal George

At half past seven the dining room was ready. Long table. White cloth. Twelve candles, not yet lit. And in the middle, on a tall silver stand, a pyramid of peaches.

Fourteen peaches. Nine at the bottom, four in the middle, one on top. Royal George, from the hothouse behind the stables. Every one was perfect. Round, gold and red, soft as a baby's cheek.

At the far end of the room stood a writing desk with its lid open. Until twenty minutes ago there had been a letter in the desk. A long white envelope with a red wax seal.

It was not there now.

Henry Lisle stood by the desk with his hands behind his back. Captain Ashby stood by the door with a glass of sherry.

"I've asked everybody," said Henry. "Mrs Marlow. Mr Fenn. You. Everybody says the same thing. Nobody came into this room. Nobody went near the desk."

"Then perhaps a servant."

"My servants have been with me for twenty years."

Captain Ashby drank some sherry. He was a tall man, fifty, with a red face and a gold ring on the little finger of his right hand. A signet ring, with a flat square top, cut with a small bird.

"Henry. Old man. I've been in the drawing room since seven. Ask Mrs Marlow."

"Mrs Marlow says you went out for a cigar at ten past seven. On the terrace."

"I did."

"The terrace door is there." Henry pointed. Beside the desk, at the end of the dining room, there was a glass door. "From the drawing room to the terrace, the short way is through this room. Past the table. Past the desk."

"I went round by the hall."

"Did you."

Henry walked to the table. He took a pair of white cotton gloves from his pocket and put them on. Slowly. One finger, then the next.

"Do you know about hothouse peaches, Ashby?" he said. "No. Why would you. My gardener picks them in these gloves. He carries them in these gloves. He puts them on the table in these gloves. Nobody touches a Royal George with a bare hand. Not ever."

"Why on earth not?"

"Because the skin is so thin. A ripe peach is a bruise waiting to happen. You touch it, not hard, just touch it, and at first you see nothing. Nothing at all. But inside, under the skin, the flesh is hurt. And slowly, slowly, it goes brown. Ten minutes. Twenty. Then the mark comes up through the skin, like a photograph in a dish of water."

He looked at the pyramid.

"I built this myself at seven o'clock. In gloves. Every peach perfect. At ten past seven I went upstairs to dress. When I came down at half past, the letter was gone. And the peach on the top looked perfect."

Captain Ashby said nothing.

"It's twenty-five to eight now," said Henry. "Come and look."

The captain did not move. Then he did. He came to the table and looked at the top of the pyramid.

On the top peach there were two marks.

One was round and soft. Brown, a little darker than the skin around it, as big as the end of a finger. The mark of a fingertip. A sleeve touches the stand, the top peach begins to fall, a quick hand catches it and puts it back.

And next to it, a second mark. Smaller. Darker. With sharp, straight edges. Not soft like a finger. Hard. Square. As if something flat and metal had pressed into the skin.

Henry did not touch the peach. He turned to the captain.

"Your hand, Ashby."

"Don't be ridiculous."

"Your hand. Please."

Slowly, Captain Ashby lifted his right hand. Henry held it by the wrist, very gently, in his white cotton glove. He brought it close to the peach. Not touching. Just close. The tip of the little finger. The flat gold ring, lower down the same finger.

The space between the two marks on the peach was the space between the end of the captain's little finger and his ring.

Exactly.

Mrs Marlow wore her rings on her third finger. Mr Fenn wore no ring at all. Henry knew. He had looked at all their hands at dinner the night before, without thinking, the way a host does.

"The letter, Ashby," he said.

Outside on the terrace, in the dark, a bird called once.

Captain Ashby put down his glass of sherry. He did it very carefully, as if the glass, too, could bruise.
