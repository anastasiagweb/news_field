# The Silver Knife at Table Four

Rain on the windows. Tuesday. Eight o'clock.

Arthur stood by the door of the dining room, as he stood every night. He had worked at the club for forty-one years. He knew every table, every fork.

The man at table four was new.

Grey suit. Wet umbrella. He sat with his back to the wall.

Arthur went to him.

"Good evening, sir."

"Good evening. Tea, please. Indian. And a lemon."

"Slices of lemon, sir?"

"No. One lemon. Whole. Not cut."

Arthur waited. In forty-one years, nobody had asked for a whole lemon.

"And a knife," the man said. "Not steel. One of the old silver fruit knives. From the club's Georgian set. The ones with the shell on the handle."

Arthur did not move for one second. Then he said, "Of course, sir."

He went to the silver room behind the kitchen. The Georgian set lived in a long wooden box with green cloth inside. The silver was dark now, almost black. Nobody used it. The treasurer, Mr Pryce, said it was too valuable to use.

Arthur took one fruit knife and laid it on a white napkin on a small silver plate. Then a lemon from the kitchen. Yellow and heavy.

He carried them back through the dining room. Tea. Lemon. Knife.

"Thank you," said the man.

Arthur stood two steps back, as a waiter stands. The man did not ask him to go.

The man picked up the knife. Dark grey, with black along the edge.

Then he cut the lemon in half.

The smell came up at once. Clean. Sharp.

The man took one half of the lemon. He pressed the cut side against the blade. Slowly, he pulled it from the handle to the point. Once. Twice. Three times.

Juice ran down the blade onto the napkin. The napkin went grey.

Arthur watched the knife.

The black was going. Under the lemon, the blade was coming clean. In the middle it was white. Bright white, like the moon. That was silver. Arthur knew silver.

But the edge was not white.

Along the edge, and at the point, the colour was different. Yellow. A dull, cheap yellow, like an old brass key.

The man touched the point with his finger. Then he touched the finger to his tongue.

"Metal," he said quietly. "Coins. Real silver doesn't taste like that."

He put the knife down on the napkin.

"A fruit knife is sharpened every few years," he said. "For a hundred years, somebody sharpened this one. If it was solid silver, the edge would be silver too. But this is cheap metal with a coat of silver on top. Very thin. The sharpening took the coat off the edge. The lemon took off the black. Now you can see."

Arthur said nothing.

"Who has the key to the silver room?" the man asked.

"I do, sir. And the treasurer."

"Mr Pryce."

"Yes, sir."

"When did the set last leave the club?"

"Two years ago. Mr Pryce sent it away to be cleaned. It came back after three months." He stopped. "It came back dark, sir. It went away to be cleaned, and it came back dark."

"Nobody looks closely at black silver," the man said. He took a small card from his pocket and put it on the table. A name, and below the name: *Lloyd's of London*.

"The club insures the set with us," he said. "Six weeks ago, a Georgian fruit knife with this shell on the handle was sold in Amsterdam. Then another one. Very quietly."

"And these, sir?"

"Victorian copies. Same shell, same shape. Silver on cheap metal. Somebody found them at markets, one by one, over years."

Arthur looked at the knife on the napkin. Forty-one years he had carried these knives. Or the real ones. When had they changed? He had not seen it. The black had hidden it.

"What do you want me to do, sir?" he asked.

"Nothing. No noise. No police in the dining room. That is not how the club does things." The man put the card away. "Is Mr Pryce here tonight?"

Arthur looked. Table nine, by the fire. A thin man, alone, eating fish.

"Table nine, sir."

The man looked at table nine for a long moment. Then he picked up the second half of the lemon and put it on the small silver plate, cut side up.

"Take him this," he said. "With my compliments."

Arthur took the plate. He walked across the room as he had walked for forty-one years. Not fast. Not slow.

At table nine he stopped and put down the lemon.

Mr Pryce looked at it for a long time. Then he put down his fork, very gently, so that it made no sound.
