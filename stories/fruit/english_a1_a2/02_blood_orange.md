# The Crimson Stain at Paddington

Five minutes to midnight. The night train to Cornwall stood at platform one, and the fog stood all around it.

Tom Fletcher sat in a first-class compartment with a leather bag on his knees. The bag was locked. A thin chain went from the handle to his left wrist. He was twenty-six, a courier for the Foreign Office. He did not know what was in the bag. That was the rule.

The door of the compartment opened.

A man came in fast and closed the door behind him. Grey coat, wet with fog. No hat. No luggage. He sat down by the window, opposite Tom, and pressed his hand flat against his coat, over the inside pocket.

He was breathing hard.

Tom looked at the man's hand. Then he looked at the white cuff of the man's shirt.

There was a stain on it. Dark red. Fresh. As Tom watched, it spread slowly into the white cotton.

Blood.

"Are you hurt?" Tom said.

The man did not answer. He looked out of the window. Outside there was only fog and the yellow lamps of the platform.

"Sir. You're bleeding."

"I'm fine."

The red on the cuff was bigger now. A drop ran down onto the man's wrist.

Tom stood up. Above the door was the red handle of the emergency chain. Pull it, and the train would not leave. There would be a guard, a doctor, police.

He reached for it.

"Don't," said the man.

"You need a doctor."

"Sit down. Please. Smell the air."

Tom stopped, his hand up. What a strange thing to say. He looked at the man. Then he did it. He breathed in.

The compartment was warm. The heaters under the seats were on. And in the warm air there was a smell. Not the iron smell of blood. Tom knew that smell; he had broken his nose at school. This was something else. Sweet. Sharp. Bright, like Christmas.

Orange.

The man took his hand away from his coat. Slowly, he put two fingers into the inside pocket and took something out.

It was an orange. Or it had been an orange. Now it was soft and broken, crushed flat, and the juice was dark. Not orange at all, but deep red, the red of wine, almost black at the edges. It ran between his fingers.

"Blood orange," the man said. "Moro. From Sicily. You only get them in winter. January, February." He almost smiled. "Lucky."

Tom sat down.

"Two men followed me from Westminster," the man said. "They were on the platform. In this fog you can see a man, but you can't see his face. You can see a red hand. You can see a red shirt." He turned the broken orange over. "I stopped under a lamp. I pressed my hand to my coat, like this. I squeezed. Hard. Then I bent over, like a man with a knife in him. I let them see my hand."

"And they—"

"They stopped. A man who is bleeding like that is finished. He won't get far. Why run after him?" He looked at the window. "They went back into the fog. Tonight they'll tell somebody I'm dying in a hospital."

Tom looked at the stain on the cuff. Juice. Only juice. In the lamplight, in the fog, nobody could see the difference. Tom couldn't.

The man pushed his thumb into the middle of the orange, into the wet red flesh. He pulled out something small. A metal tube, as long as a finger, as thin as a pencil, closed at both ends. Dry inside. Nothing had gone in.

"Microfilm," he said. "Forty pages."

He held it out to Tom.

"I'm getting off this train," he said, "before it leaves. You are going to Cornwall. There is a man at Penzance who is waiting for this. They told me you would be in this compartment. They didn't tell you about me. That's the rule."

Tom looked at the tube. Then at the chain on his wrist. Then he took the small key from his watch chain and opened the bag, and the man dropped the tube inside.

A whistle, outside, in the fog.

The man stood up. He put the crushed orange back in his pocket. At the door he stopped and looked down at his white cuff, at the dark red stain.

"Good shirt," he said. "Shame."

Then he was gone, and the train began to move.

Tom sat for a long time with the bag on his knees. The compartment still smelled of orange. On the seat opposite there was one small drop of red. He touched it with his finger. Then, because he was twenty-six and nobody was looking, he tasted it.

Sweet.
