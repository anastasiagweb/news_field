# The Five-Pointed Star

Rain hit the windows of the old inn like handfuls of small stones. In the fire, a log broke and fell, and sparks flew up the chimney.

Dinner was finished. On the table there was a white cloth, four silver spoons, four silver forks, a jug of cream and a wooden bowl of apples. Small apples, orange and red, like a sunset.

The judge sat at the end of the table with his hands together. He was eighty-one. For thirty years he had sent people to prison, and he still had the face for it.

"A question," he said.

His two nephews and his niece looked up. Robert, who was forty-five and a banker. Harriet, who was forty and a lawyer. And Kitty, who was twenty-two and did not know yet what she was.

From his waistcoat pocket the judge took an old iron key. He put it on the white cloth. The head of the key was a star with five points.

"The key to my library," he said. "At Combe House. Eleven thousand books. The lock on the door is a star, like this one. When I die, the library goes to one of you. Tonight I am going to decide which one."

Nobody spoke.

"Show me a star," said the judge, "that never rose in the sky. It is hidden here, on this table. You may not leave your chair."

Robert looked at the ceiling. Harriet looked at the window. Then they both looked at the table.

Robert picked up a fork and turned it over. On the back of the handle there were small marks, stamped into the silver. A lion. A letter. A little head. "Hallmarks," he said. "Sometimes there's a star in the hallmarks. A mark for the town." He held it close to his eyes. "Lion. Leopard's head. Letter G..."

Harriet took a spoon. "Some old silversmiths used a star as their mark." She held it near the candle.

The judge watched them. He said nothing.

Kitty did not pick up a spoon. She looked at the wooden bowl.

She took one apple. It was small and hard, orange and red, with a rough brown patch near the top, like a freckle. She held it near her ear and shook it gently.

Something inside went *tick-tick*. A very small rattle.

The judge's eyebrows moved. Only a little.

"Cox's Orange Pippin," Kitty said. "When they're ripe, the seeds come loose inside. You can hear them. Granny taught me."

"Your grandmother," said the judge, "knew a great deal about apples."

Kitty picked up her fruit knife.

Everybody cuts an apple the same way. Down from the top, through the stalk. Two halves. Then four. Then you cut out the hard middle and throw it away.

Kitty did not cut down.

She put the apple on its side, with the stalk pointing at the window. She put the knife to the middle. Not at the top, but at the fat middle part, the equator, like the line round a globe. And she cut across.

The two halves fell open on the white cloth.

In the middle of each half, in the white flesh, there was a star.

Five points. Perfect. Five small rooms in a ring around the centre, and in each room a brown seed, shining. Fine lines went out from the rooms to the edge, like rays of light. That star had been in every apple Robert had ever eaten. He had never seen it. Nobody sees it. Nobody cuts that way.

Robert put down his fork. Harriet put down her spoon.

"A star that never rose in the sky," said Kitty. "Every apple has one. You just cut the other way."

The judge looked at the apple for a long moment. Then he looked at Kitty.

"Thirty years in court," he said. "Every day, people told me what happened. And every day I had to find out what really happened. That was the whole job. Not to look harder. To look from the other side."

He picked up the iron key and leaned across the table. He did not give it to her. He laid it down on the half of the apple nearest to her, so that the five points of the key lay over the five points of the seeds.

They fitted.
