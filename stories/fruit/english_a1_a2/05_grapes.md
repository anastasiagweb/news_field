# The Great Vine at Midnight

At six in the morning the glasshouse was already warm. Water ran down the inside of the glass. Above, under the roof, the Great Vine spread its arms. One old trunk, thick as a man, and from it, branches going out and out, to every corner, under every pane. From the branches hung the grapes. Hundreds of bunches. Black, with a soft grey dust on them, heavy as hands.

Black Hamburg. Planted at Hampton Court in 1768. The biggest vine in the world.

Mrs Ross stood under the middle branch with her head back.

"There," said the night watchman. He pointed. Then he went out, because his night was finished and this was not his business.

Now there were two of them. Mrs Ross, the head gardener. And Owen, who was nineteen, the youngest trainee, who had come in early because she told him to.

Every year, at the end of summer, the gardeners cut the grapes, and the palace shop sold them, and visitors took a piece of history home in a paper bag. Last night, forty bunches lay in boxes in the shop, ready for the morning. Anybody who wanted grapes could take a bunch from a box. Easy. Nobody would notice.

But somebody had come into the glasshouse after dark. Somebody had climbed a ladder to the middle branch, the oldest branch, and cut one bunch.

Only one.

"Why not take from the boxes?" Owen said. "There are forty bunches in the boxes."

"Yes," said Mrs Ross. "Why not?"

She took the ladder from the wall and put it under the branch. She climbed four steps. Owen held the ladder. She looked closely at the place where the bunch had been.

Then she came down and held out her hand. "Your knife."

He gave her his knife. Every trainee had one. She climbed again and cut another bunch, a good one, from the next branch. She came down and held it out to him.

"Look at the top."

He looked. The bunch hung from a short green stalk. As long as her thumb. That was how they cut: close to the grapes, short, clean. She taught them that in the first week.

"Now look up there."

He looked up at the middle branch. Where the stolen bunch had been, the cut was not short. It was far back. Somebody had cut the bunch and also a long piece of green shoot above it. New wood, this year's wood, with three or four leaves on it. As long as a pencil. Longer.

"That's not a bunch of grapes," said Mrs Ross. "That's a cutting."

Owen said nothing.

"A summer cutting. Green wood. Put it in sand, under glass, keep it warm and wet, and in six weeks it has roots. In a year it's a little vine. In ten years, a big one. And it's the same vine. Not a child. Not a cousin. The same plant as this one, from 1768. Nobody sells it. Nobody ever has."

The glasshouse dripped around them.

"A thief who wants grapes cuts like a thief," she said. "Fast. Anywhere. A thief who wants a vine cuts like a gardener. Long, clean, with leaves. This is a very good cut. Somebody taught this person well." She looked at him. "I taught them."

"There are six trainees," Owen said.

"Yes."

"Anybody could—"

"Your sleeve," said Mrs Ross.

He looked down. He was wearing his green wool jumper, because the mornings were cold now, at the end of August. On the right sleeve, near the wrist, there were dark marks. Purple. Almost black. Sticky. The wool there was stuck together in little hard points.

The juice of black grapes. Grapes crushed on a ladder, in the dark, when a heavy bunch swings against an arm.

And in the wool, caught in the stitches, there was a small green thing. A curl. Thin as a wire, turned round and round like a spring. A tendril, the little hand a vine uses to hold on to things. It was holding on to his sleeve.

Mrs Ross did not touch it.

"Where is it?" she said.

Owen looked at the curl on his sleeve. He looked up at the old branch. He looked for a long time.

"In a pot," he said at last. "Under my bed. In a plastic bag. To keep it wet."

"A plastic bag." She closed her eyes for a second. "Good. That's right. That's what I would do."

She gave him back his knife, handle first.

"Bring it here," she said. "Today. And then we'll talk about your future."
