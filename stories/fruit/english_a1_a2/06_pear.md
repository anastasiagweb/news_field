# The Espalier on the South Wall

The wall was old red brick, warm in the October sun, and on it grew three pear trees that did not look like trees.

They were flat. Every branch was tied to a wire. Every branch went where somebody had told it to go. Some went straight up. Some went across, at an angle, and crossed other branches, so the trees made a pattern on the wall, like a fence or a net. Between the leaves hung the last pears of the year. Fat, yellow-green, with a little red on one side. Doyenné du Comice. The best pear in the world, some people say.

"It's a lovely wall," said the new owner. "It's a shame."

His name was Mr Clare. He was forty, from London, and he had bought the house in August. He wanted a swimming pool. The wall was in the way.

"The builders come on Monday," he said.

Mr Hobbs said nothing. He was seventy-four. He had worked in this garden since he was fourteen. He had a small knife in one hand and a pear in the other.

"I know you love these trees," Mr Clare said. "I'm sorry. But they're just trees. We can plant new ones somewhere else."

"A hundred years old," said Mr Hobbs.

"I'm sorry."

Mr Hobbs put the pear in his pocket. He closed his knife.

"Come up to the house," he said. "To the balcony. Before Monday."

"Why?"

"You've never looked at the wall from up there."

They went through the garden gate, and up the stairs, and through the big bedroom, and out onto the stone balcony at the back of the house. From here you could see the whole garden. The paths. The low green hedges. The greenhouse. And at the end, the long south wall, flat in the sun, with the three trees on it.

"Look," said Mr Hobbs.

Mr Clare looked.

From the garden, close, the trees were only branches and leaves and pears. From up here, the branches made shapes.

On the first tree, two branches went across the wall from corner to corner and crossed in the middle. An X.

Next to it, two branches started together at the bottom and went up and out. A V.

Then two straight branches, going up. I. I.

Then a space. On the third tree, one straight branch. I. And next to it, two branches from the bottom, out and up. V.

"Ten," said Mr Clare slowly. "Five. One. One." He stopped. "Seventeen. And four."

"Roman numbers," said Mr Hobbs. "The old way of counting."

"Who did this?"

"Mr Pike. The gardener before me. I was his boy. He was the gardener's boy before that, for old Mr Danby, who had the house in 1920." Mr Hobbs looked at the wall. "You don't make shapes like that in a year. Every winter you cut. Every summer you tie. You keep this branch, not that one. A hundred years. A little every year."

"But why?"

"Old Mr Danby didn't like banks." Mr Hobbs almost smiled. "He had gold. Guineas. Old gold coins. He put them in the wall, behind one brick. A brick with nothing inside. Hollow. Then he told his gardener. Only his gardener. And his gardener grew the number on the wall, so it would never be lost. Not in a book. Not on paper. Paper burns. Paper gets lost. A tree stays where it is."

"Seventeen. Four."

"Row seventeen from the ground. Brick four from the gate."

Mr Clare looked at the wall for a long time.

"And Mr Danby's family? They never—"

"He didn't have a family. Only the house. And the garden." Mr Hobbs turned the closed knife over in his fingers. "Mr Pike told me when I was twenty. 'The gold goes with the house,' he said. 'Tell the next owner. But only if he wants to cut down the trees.'"

"Why only then?"

"Because if he loves the trees, he doesn't need the gold."

Below them, a pear fell from the wall and landed in the grass. They both heard it.

"How much gold?"

Mr Hobbs shook his head. "I never looked. It's not my wall."

Mr Clare took out his phone. He looked at it. Then he put it back in his pocket.

"The builders," he said. "On Monday."

"Yes."

"I'll call them."

Mr Hobbs said nothing. He took the pear from his pocket and opened his knife. He cut one thin slice, white and wet, and held it out to Mr Clare on the blade.
