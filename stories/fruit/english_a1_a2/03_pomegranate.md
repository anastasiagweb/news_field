# Six Seeds for Persephone

The necklace lay on black velvet under a small bright lamp. Eleven gold frames in a row. In ten of them, a red stone, dark as wine. Georgian. Two hundred years old.

Next to it, on the same velvet, lay a pomegranate.

It was a real one. Big, dark red, with a crack down one side. Through the crack you could see the seeds inside. Hundreds of them, shining, packed tight like little red glass beads.

"It's for the colour," Daniel said. "The stones are garnets. Pyropes, from Bohemia. The word *garnet* comes from the Latin word for pomegranate, *granatum*. Because they look like the seeds." He was twenty-four, the assistant, in a good suit that was a little too big for him. He had arranged the tray himself that afternoon. "The guests like it. It's a story."

"I'm sure they do," said Inspector Kemble.

She was not looking at the pomegranate. She was looking at the necklace. Eleven gold frames. Ten red stones.

The third frame from the left was empty.

Twenty minutes ago there had been eleven. Fifteen people in this small back room of a shop in Bath, each with a glass of wine, looking at the necklace before tomorrow's auction. Then the lights went out. One, two, three seconds. Somebody laughed. Somebody said, "Oh!" Then the lights came back, and the owner, Mr Harker, looked down at the tray and went white.

Now the guests were in the front of the shop, with a constable at the door. Nobody had left. Inspector Kemble had made sure of that. Every pocket, every handbag, every glove. Daniel's pockets too. Nothing. No garnet.

Only Daniel was still in the back room with her.

"It's an old building," he said. "The electrics. It happens all the time."

"Does it."

"Well. Sometimes."

She bent over the tray. The stone was small. As big as the nail on her little finger. Easy to hide in a mouth, in a shoe, in the lining of a coat. But they had looked. And a clever thief doesn't keep the stone. A clever thief puts it somewhere and comes back for it later.

Where, in this room?

The table. The velvet. The lamp. The necklace.

The pomegranate.

"Do you know the story of Persephone?" Daniel said. His voice was a little too quick. "The guests asked me. She was a girl, and the king of the dead took her down under the ground. She was hungry. She ate six pomegranate seeds. Only six. And because of those six seeds, she had to stay under the ground for six months every year. That's why we have winter."

"Six seeds," said the inspector.

"Yes."

She moved the lamp a little to the left, so the light fell into the crack of the fruit.

The seeds shone back at her. Hundreds of them. Every one was round and full and wet, and every one held a small, soft point of light, like a drop of water.

Except one.

Deep in the crack, between the round seeds, there was a seed that was not round. Its top was flat. When she moved her head, the flat top caught the lamp. Not a soft point of light, but a sharp white flash, like a tiny window. Then it went dark. When she moved again, it flashed again.

Seeds don't flash. Stones do. A cut stone has flat faces.

And just next to it, one seed was broken. Crushed. It had sent a drop of red juice down the white skin inside the fruit. The drop was still wet. Fresh. As if somebody had pressed hard there with a finger, twenty minutes ago, in the dark.

"Mr Harker said the fruit goes in the bin tonight," Daniel said. "After the guests leave. I can take it out for him, if you like. It's getting old."

Inspector Kemble opened her bag and took out a pair of tweezers.

"You were next to the table when the lights went out," she said. "Mr Harker told me. You were standing just here."

Daniel said nothing. He put his right hand in his pocket.

She put the tweezers into the crack. Carefully, she took hold of the flat seed. It did not come easily. The seeds around it held it tight. Then it came. She lifted it into the light.

A small red stone, cut flat on top, wet with juice.

She laid it on the black velvet, next to the empty gold frame. It lay there and shone.

"Persephone ate six," the inspector said. "You only needed one."
