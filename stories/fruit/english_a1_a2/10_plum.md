# The Fingerprint on the Victoria Plum

The Dean's study was on the first floor, above the old college garden. The window was open. Outside, in the August heat, somebody was cutting grass, and the smell came in.

On the Dean's desk there was a wooden box with its lid up. Inside, on blue velvet, there were thirty small round holes for thirty coins. Twenty-nine had coins in them. Silver and gold. Roman. Greek. Old English kings with long noses.

One hole was empty.

Next to the box stood a white china bowl full of plums. Victoria plums, from the Fellows' Garden, picked that morning. They were dark purple and red, and every one was covered with a soft grey-white dust, as if someone had breathed on them on a cold day.

Mr Gilchrist sat in the chair in front of the desk. He was a coin dealer from London. The Dean had asked him to look at the collection before it went to the museum. He had been alone with the box for half an hour. Then he had left. Then the Dean had counted.

Now he was back, because Inspector Moran had asked him nicely.

He put a pair of thin leather gloves on the desk. Pale yellow. Kid leather, soft as paper.

"I never take these off," he said. "Never. Not when I'm working. Old silver, old gold, they don't like skin. Your fingers are wet and salty. They damage the metal. Every dealer knows that. I wore these gloves the whole time." He opened his hands. "No fingerprints on the box. No fingerprints on the velvet. You won't find one. Because I never took them off."

"You used tweezers," said the inspector.

"Of course. For the small coins."

"Like the one that's missing. A silver penny of King Offa. Very small."

"Very small. Very rare."

"Hard to pick up with tweezers," said Inspector Moran. "In gloves. Thick fingers, thin tweezers. I tried it this morning. I dropped the coin twice."

Mr Gilchrist smiled. "You're not a dealer, Inspector."

"No."

She did not look at him. She looked at the bowl of plums.

"Do you know what this is?" She pointed, not touching, at the grey-white dust on the top plum. "It's called bloom. It's wax. The plum makes it itself, to keep off the rain. It's very thin. Thinner than the dust on a moth. You touch it, and it's gone. Children love to draw in it with a finger."

"How charming."

"The Dean's secretary put these on the desk at half past nine, before you came. She carried the bowl from the kitchen in both hands. She didn't touch the plums. Nobody touched them, until you."

She bent over the bowl, so that the light from the window fell across the top plum.

Most of it was grey-white and soft, like a cloud. But on one side, near the edge of the bowl, there was a place where the bloom was gone. A small oval, the size of a fingertip. There, the skin of the plum was not grey. It was dark purple and shone like glass.

And in the shining place, if you looked close, there were lines. Thin curved lines, round and round, like the rings inside a tree. The lines of a finger, pressed into the wax and printed there, as clear as ink.

Across the lines, from one side to the other, ran one straight, smooth line, where there were no rings at all.

"An old scar," said Inspector Moran. "On the end of somebody's finger. A cut, I think. A long time ago. A knife, maybe. Or broken glass."

Mr Gilchrist did not move.

"You took off one glove," she said. "Just for a minute. To hold the tweezers better, for a very small silver penny. Your other hand went down to the desk. But the desk was full. The box, the papers, the bowl. So your bare finger rested on the edge of the bowl. On the plum. You didn't even feel it. Nobody feels a plum."

Outside, the grass-cutting machine stopped. The room was very quiet.

Mr Gilchrist looked down at his hands. They lay on his knees, the right hand on top of the left.

Slowly, he turned his right hand over and looked at the end of his first finger.
