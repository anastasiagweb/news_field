# Failure Gallery — Lessons from an Earlier Generation

Read before the first audit of any run, and whenever calibrating severity. These are real failures (lightly quoted) from an earlier, weaker generation of a Living History–style collection on Ancient Greece ("Echoes of Hellas", bundles on Minoan Crete, Mycenaean Greece, and the Greek "Dark Age"). The same collection also had real strengths, listed at the end — keep them.

The historical comments below summarise mainstream scholarship as a calibration aid. In a real run, every correction is re-verified against sources per `fact_check_doctrine.md`.

---

## A. One template for thirty stories

Across thirty stories, the same arc recurred: *wake before dawn → palace demands the impossible by a deadline → midday crisis → clever improvisation → authority praises → knowledge passed to the young → "Tomorrow there would be new challenges. But tonight…" → coda about generations.* Word counts across the thirty stories: "generation(s)" 25 times, "secret" 24, "impossible" 17, "the palace wants/expects/demands" 16, "eternal" 13, sentence-initial "Tomorrow" 13, "three days" 11.

- The sneering scribe with a tablet appeared in two stories with almost the same line: "Production is down from last month."
- Endings such as: "The gods lived in the high places, but craftsmanship — true craftsmanship — happened here…"; "This was how it continued — knowledge passed hand to hand…"; "…waiting for archaeologists not yet born to wonder at their massive stones."

**Lesson:** enforce `anti_formula.md` and the variety contract in `story_forms.md` mechanically.

## B. Plots the reader enjoyed — made weaker by contrivance

Intrigue, theft, sabotage, and dangerous journeys were among the most enjoyable stories. They are welcome in the line. What weakened them:

| Story | Contrivance | Plausibility-contract clause broken | A truer version keeps the plot |
|---|---|---|---|
| A treasury keeper discovers stolen silver | A great lord openly confesses to "unauthorised intelligence operations" and "organised crime"; the king spends a morning weighing silver himself and says "Image matters more than reality" | 2 (scale), 3 (motives), 7 (authorities), mentalité | The keeper notices broken sealings on storeroom jars; he traces the theft through the seal impressions and the tablet records; a steward's man is caught by an oath before the gods; the keeper must stay silent about who protected the thief. Small, tense, period means. |
| A chariot builder finds sabotage | A prince visits the workshop at night, lies under the chariot, and philosophises: "Pure breeds are strong but brittle. Mixing creates resilience." | 7, 8, mentalité | The builder must find who damaged the axle before the wheels are counted for the palace records; a tool mark and a debt point to a rival's apprentice; the matter is settled privately to avoid shame for both houses. |
| A merchant's dangerous journey | The route reaches **Alexandria** (founded 331 BCE) in a 13th-century BCE story; the bandit ambush is defused by a bluffing speech naming the conspirator; deals in "forty percent" | Fact (Critical), 8 (speech solves everything), mentalité | A shorter, real route; the danger is the sea and the season; a sacrifice before sailing changes a decision; part of the cargo is lost, part saved; the cost is real. |
| A snake priestess unmasks a thief | The divination snake "smells guilt" — modern lie-detector logic; she also prepares an antidote from venom for bee stings (modern antivenom logic) | 4 (means), fact | Divination works as social pressure: the guilty person, terrified of the goddess, confesses or flees before the rite. The healing uses period remedies. |
| A city falls in the night | A "refugee" lord betrays the city; a well-structured, moving ending | Mostly sound; minor anachronisms (bronze gongs) | Keep the shape; check every object. |

**Lesson:** keep plotted genres; enforce `story_forms.md` → *Plausibility contract*.

## C. Modern minds

- A Bronze Age dye-maker's son proposes managing the shellfish beds sustainably: "Write a proposal… Put in numbers, projections."
- An 8th-century BCE Corinthian farmer proposes proportional taxation, a council of 5+5, and laws carved in stone — and the leader of the ruling family welcomes it.
- An early temple builder: "I believe that the gods, if they exist, probably care more about honest workmanship…"
- A Dark Age potter to his daughter: "The old rules belonged to the old world… That knowledge doesn't care whether you're male or female."
- Aphorisms: "Desperation is the mother of innovation." "Fear makes you run. Respect makes you sing." "Truth is what powerful men say it is."

**Lesson:** `mentalite_doctrine.md` is a hard gate.

## D. Names

- Ordinary people with mythic or famous names: Daedalus (twice, in different books), Ariadne, Minos, Pythia (a title — used three times), Orpheus, Penthesilea, Phemios, Demodokos, Euphronios (a famous later vase painter, given to a Geometric potter), Solon (in a 10th-century BCE colony), Aristoteles (as leader of 8th-century Corinth).
- Names reused across books four hundred years apart: Theron, Hiram, Melissa, Nestor, Alexios.
- Minoans given Classical Greek names although the book's own introduction says they were not Greek speakers.
- A god's name as a man's name: Helios.

**Lesson:** `onomastics.md`, the forbidden list, and the series name registry.

## E. Facts — the most serious failures

**Things that did not exist yet, or not there:**
- Alexandria (founded 331 BCE) in a Late Bronze Age story.
- Metapontion (a Greek colony in Italy founded in the 7th century BCE) as a coastal town of Bronze Age Pylos.
- A hoplite (heavy infantry of the 7th century BCE onward) in Mycenaean Greece.
- Solon (6th century BCE), written law, and councils chosen by lot in a 10th-century BCE settlement — before the Greek alphabet existed.
- A temple at Olympia around 800 BCE (the earliest temple there, the Heraion, is usually dated around 600 BCE).
- A coin ("a copper") as a tip in Bronze Age Crete; coinage begins around the 7th–6th century BCE.
- A lighthouse in a Bronze Age harbour; a papyrus sea chart; navigation by sun angle "to tell latitude".
- A bear on Crete (no bears lived on Holocene Crete).
- Pan worshipped in Thessaly around 1000 BCE (the cult spread from Arcadia much later).

**Wrong technology:**
- A Minoan olive press with large stone crushing wheels turned by a beam — rotary olive mills are much later (Classical/Hellenistic).
- Mycenaean perfume made by "enfleurage" and "distillation" — modern techniques.
- Bronze "hardened" by quenching in water and "morning dew" — steel logic; tin bronze is hardened by hammering, not quenching.
- The black of Geometric pottery as an imported "black iron oxide pigment mixed with olive oil" — it comes from an iron-rich clay slip and a controlled firing.
- A potter's wheel with a "foot pedal" in Minoan Crete.
- A cracked plate repaired by painting gold along the crack — an idea associated with Japanese *kintsugi*, many centuries later.

**Wrong style or period inside one story:**
- Kamares ware (roughly 1900–1700 BCE) painted with the octopus motifs of the later Marine Style (around 1500 BCE).

**Wrong Notes (the worst category):**
- "Chemical analysis of pigments reveals … lapis lazuli from Afghanistan" for Minoan frescoes — Minoan blues were mainly Egyptian blue and minerals such as glaucophane.
- "The technology of olive pressing … remained essentially unchanged from the Bronze Age through the early modern period."
- "The famous 'Treasury of Atreus'" cited as evidence for palace treasuries — it is a tholos tomb, named by later tradition.
- "Scribes appear to have been exclusively male" — the evidence cannot show this.
- "Modern rodeo athletes confirm [the bull-leap] is theoretically possible" — inverts the actual debate.
- A Note presenting an innovation the story invented ("painting the sea from below became a signature of Minoan frescoes") as real art history.

**Lesson:** `fact_check_doctrine.md`, `claim_taxonomy.md`, the NOT-YET list in every dossier, and every Note sentence treated as a claim.

## F. Series-level failures

- The series plan collapsed in its later volumes into telegraphic fragments ("Wine sweeter knowing ending").
- Thematic volumes across periods duplicated chronological ones (Spartan boys, Dionysia, lighthouses, coral divers, weddings).
- Famous people replaced ordinary people as protagonists (Socrates' last day; Cleopatra's feast).
- Promised apparatus (glossaries, maps, timelines) never appeared.
- Mixed British and American spelling within and across books.

**Lesson:** the series pipeline's audits on architecture, uniqueness, and bible completeness.

## G. What worked — keep it

- **"Songs by the Hearth"** — a travelling singer; an old woman says the burnt palace records are gone, "but you carry the words in your flesh and breath. No fire can burn what lives in here." One line holds the story.
- **"The Honey Harvest"** — specific craft knowledge, a real danger, a niece learning — vivid and warm (the bear must go; the shape stays).
- **"The Saffron Gatherers"** — a small rite of passage, sensory and calm, grounded in a real fresco.
- **"The Village Chief"** — a boundary dispute settled by compromise; small stakes, felt strongly.
- **"Harvest Under the Citadel"** — labour owed to the palace, rain, a lost harvest, land slipping to the powerful; honest and unsentimental.
- **"Night Watch"** — the fall of a city seen by one guard on one night; strong shape and ending.
- **Light links** — a beekeeper from one story appears in another; a workshop supervisor visits the scribe. Readers enjoyed them.
- **Historical Notes as a feature** — readers value knowing what is real. Keep them; make them true.
