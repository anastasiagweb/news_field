# Story Craft — Writing a Living History Story

Read before the framework (P2), before drafting each story (P5), before revising (P7), and before the publish-ready edit (P8). This is the drafter's craft document. It assumes the five tests in `quality_standard.md`, the forms in `story_forms.md`, the bans in `anti_formula.md`, the mind rules in `mentalite_doctrine.md`, and the register in `cefr_register_<LANG>.md`.

---

## The core idea

A Living History story is a short story first. It is about one person, on one stretch of time, in one kind of trouble, in a world the reader can touch. History is the ground it stands on, not the subject it explains. A native reader should finish it because they had to know what happened; a learner should finish it without a struggle; a historian should finish it without wincing.

## 1. The first 200 words

They do four jobs at once, through concrete detail and action — never through an information paragraph:
- **Body:** we are inside one person, doing something.
- **World:** at least two concrete details that place us in this time and place (an object, a sound, a custom, a material).
- **Engine:** the question the story will answer is posed or clearly coming.
- **Voice:** the story's tone is set (quiet, tense, comic, eerie).

Wrong: "The Minoan civilisation of Crete was famous for its palaces. Dikta was fourteen years old and lived near the palace of Knossos, where she…"
Right: begin with her hands, a flower, a rule she must not break, the priestess watching.

## 2. Scenes

- Three to seven scenes per story. Each scene changes something: information, power, a relationship, a danger, a decision.
- Prefer scene (moment by moment, with dialogue and action) to summary; use summary to jump time, briefly.
- Enter late, leave early. No scene starts with waking up and walking somewhere unless the walk matters.
- Each scene has one concrete detail that could belong only to this story.
- Vary scene modes: action, dialogue, stillness, ritual, travel. No more than two scenes of the same mode in a row.

## 3. Turns and the engine

- The engine question stays alive until near the end; feed it, complicate it, delay it.
- At least two turns: moments where the direction changes because of a choice, a discovery, a reversal, an outside event.
- The protagonist acts. Things do not simply happen to them; they make at least one decisive choice.
- The resolution comes from the protagonist's action, knowledge, or choice — not from an authority's decision handed down, not from luck.

## 4. Plotted stories — keep them true and tight

Mystery, theft, intrigue, dispute, journey, disaster, chase, contest are welcome. Apply the plausibility contract (`story_forms.md` §6) in every scene:
- The trouble is the kind of trouble this world had.
- The protagonist's reach is the reach of an ordinary person.
- Clues are things people of the time could notice: a seal impression, a weight that is too light, footprints in a field, a dog that knows someone, a tool mark, a smell from a dye-vat, who was absent from a rite.
- Resolution uses the time's means: witnesses, oaths before gods, a lord's or elders' judgement, the fear of a curse, a public test, a bargain, flight.
- One coincidence at most, and it makes trouble.
- The ending costs something.

## 5. Character

- Each named character: one physical detail, one habit of speech or gesture, one want. Keep the cast small (≤5 named).
- Secondary characters are people, not lesson-deliverers. The old woman does not exist to explain the theme; she exists because she wants something too.
- Let the protagonist's taken-for-granted belief (from the card) drive at least one choice.

## 6. Dialogue

- Short, natural, period-neutral English (`cefr_register_<LANG>.md`).
- Characters speak from their rank and role: the slave does not lecture the master; the master does not explain himself to the slave.
- Subtext: people say less than they mean; they answer a different question; they change the subject.
- No dialogue that informs the reader of things both speakers know ("As you know, the palace takes a sixth of our grain…"). Find another way: an argument about the sixth, a basket being counted.
- Oaths, greetings, and blessings in the period's manner, rendered simply.

## 7. Showing the period without explaining it

- Use things, not definitions. The world is present in what people handle, eat, fear, and say.
- Period terms within the budget; English first (`cefr_register_<LANG>.md`).
- When a custom must be understood, show someone obeying, breaking, or arguing about it.
- Never stop the story to explain. If the reader truly needs context, it goes in the Note.
- No narrator from the future: no "in those days", no "as people believed then", no modern place names, no period labels (Minoan, Bronze Age).

## 8. Feeling

- Show feeling through body, action, silence, and objects. Label rarely.
- Frame strong feeling in the period's terms where possible (the gods, honour and shame, the body) — but simply enough for the band.
- Grief, fear, love, anger, shame, and joy are universal; their expression and their reasons are not. Get the reasons right.

## 9. Endings

- End on an image, an act, or a line of dialogue (`story_forms.md` §4).
- Do not explain the ending. Do not add a paragraph about what it all meant, what tomorrow would bring, or how the generations continue.
- The last sentence is the strongest sentence in the story. Read it aloud.

## 10. The Note

Written after the story, from the dossier and the framework's Note plan (`historical_note_craft.md`). It never repeats the story; it tells the reader what was real.

## 11. Length and rhythm

- Within the band's word budget (`book_format.md`), and varied across the book.
- Sentence-length variety at every band; paragraph-length variety; single-line paragraphs for weight, sparingly.

## 12. The drafter's checklist (after each story)

1. Engine posed within 200 words? Two turns? Protagonist's decisive choice?
2. Orientation done through detail, without an explaining paragraph?
3. Every fact traceable to a dossier ID — or listed as a new claim?
4. Mentalité scan: any modern concept, value, idiom, or foresight? (`mentalite_doctrine.md` tests)
5. Formula scan: banned moves and phrases (`anti_formula.md`)?
6. Names: only registry names; ≤5 named; no confusable pair?
7. Period terms within budget, each clear from context?
8. Register sample: sentence lengths, subordination, tenses in band?
9. Standalone: understandable with no other story read?
10. Ending on an image/act/line, with a cost?
11. Darkness and respect within limits?
