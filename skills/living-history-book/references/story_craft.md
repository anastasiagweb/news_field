# Story Craft — Writing a Living History Story

Read before the framework (P2), before drafting (P5), before revising (P7), and before the publish-ready edit (P8). Read with `quality_standard.md`, `story_forms.md`, `anti_formula.md`, `mentalite_doctrine.md`, and `cefr_register_<LANG>.md`.

---

## The core idea

A Living History story is a short story first: one person, one stretch of time, one kind of trouble, in a world the reader can touch. History is the ground it stands on, not the subject it explains. The native reader finishes it because they have to know what happens; the learner finishes it without a struggle; the specialist finishes it without wincing.

## 1. The first 200 words

They do four jobs at once, through action and concrete detail, never through an information paragraph:
- **Body:** we are inside one person, doing something.
- **World:** at least two concrete details that place us in this time and place.
- **Engine:** the question the story will answer is posed or clearly coming.
- **Voice:** the tone is set.

## 2. Scenes

- Three to seven scenes. Each scene changes something: knowledge, power, a relationship, a danger, a decision.
- Scene over summary; summary only to move time.
- Enter late, leave early.
- Each scene holds at least one detail that belongs only to this story.
- No more than two scenes of the same mode in a row.

## 3. Turns and agency

- The engine stays alive until near the end; it is fed, complicated, delayed.
- At least two turns.
- The protagonist makes at least one decisive choice, and the resolution comes from the protagonist's action, knowledge, or choice.

## 4. Plotted stories

Apply the plausibility contract (`story_forms.md` §6) in every scene: the trouble is of the period's kind; the protagonist's reach is an ordinary person's reach; clues are things people of the time could notice; resolution uses the time's means; one coincidence at most, making trouble; the ending costs something; in mysteries, every clue the solution needs is on the page before the solution.

## 5. Character

- Every named character has one physical detail, one habit of speech or gesture, one want. At most five named characters.
- Secondary characters have their own wants; they never exist to deliver a lesson.
- The protagonist's taken-for-granted belief drives at least one choice.

## 6. Dialogue

- Short, natural, period-neutral English (`cefr_register_<LANG>.md`).
- Rank and role shape speech: who may speak first, who explains, who stays silent.
- Subtext: people say less than they mean, answer another question, change the subject.
- No dialogue that tells the reader what both speakers already know.
- Oaths, greetings, and blessings in the period's manner, rendered simply.

## 7. The period without explanation

- The world is present in what people handle, eat, fear, and say.
- Period terms within budget, English first, meaning carried by context.
- A custom the reader must understand is shown being obeyed, broken, or argued over.
- The story never stops to explain. Context the reader truly needs goes in the Note.
- No narrator from the future: no retrospective framing, no modern place names, no scholarly period labels inside the story.

## 8. Feeling

- Through body, action, silence, and objects; labels rarely.
- Strong feeling framed in the period's terms where possible, simply enough for the band.
- The feelings are universal; their reasons and their expression belong to the period. Get the reasons right.

## 9. Endings

- On an image, an act, or a line of dialogue (`story_forms.md` §4).
- No explanation of the ending; no paragraph about what it meant; no coda of any kind listed in `anti_formula.md`.
- The last sentence is the strongest sentence in the story.

## 10. The Note

Written after the story, from the dossier and the framework's Note plan (`historical_note_craft.md`).

## 11. Length and rhythm

Within the band's budget (`book_format.md`), varied across the book. Variety of sentence length, paragraph length, and scene mode.

## 12. The author's checklist after each story

1. Engine posed within 200 words; two turns; a decisive choice; an ending on an image, act, or line, with a cost.
2. Orientation through detail; no explaining paragraph.
3. Every fact traceable to a dossier ID, or listed as a new claim.
4. Mentalité tests passed (`mentalite_doctrine.md`).
5. No banned move or phrase (`anti_formula.md`).
6. Only registry names; at most five named; no confusable pair.
7. Period terms within budget, each clear from context.
8. Three register samples inside the band.
9. Standalone.
10. Word count within budget.
11. Darkness and respect within limits.
