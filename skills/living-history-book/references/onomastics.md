# Onomastics — Names That Belong

Read before allocating any name (series bible, slate, book pitch, framework, draft) and before every onomastics audit. Names are the first thing a reader meets and the cheapest place to lose their trust. A Bronze Age weaver called *Penthesilea*, a Dark Age bard's apprentice called *Orpheus*, a 10th-century settler called *Solon*: each one tells the native reader that nobody checked.

---

## The five tests every name must pass

1. **Period and place.** The name was used (ideally attested) in this civilisation, in this window, in this region, in this language. A name common in Classical Athens is not automatically right for Mycenaean Pylos, Archaic Ionia, or Hellenistic Egypt.
2. **Sex and status.** The name fits the person's sex and social position. Many cultures marked slaves, foreigners, priests, or elites with distinctive name types.
3. **No celebrity shadow.** The name does not carry a famous person, god, hero, or myth into the reader's mind — unless the name was demonstrably common in that world *and* the bible allows it. Ordinary people do not walk around called Daedalus, Ariadne, Minos, Achilles, Orpheus, Solon, Aristotle, Pericles, Cleopatra, Ramesses, Ragnar, or Montezuma.
4. **Not a god, title, or place.** No god's name as a personal name unless the culture did that (theophoric names — names *built from* a god's name, such as Greek *Apollonios* or Egyptian *Amenemhat* — are fine where attested). No titles as names (*Pythia* was a title, not a name). No place names as personal names (*Melos*, *Dikta*) unless attested as such.
5. **Unique in the series.** No name is used twice in the same series, for any character, in any book, unless the bible declares a deliberate exception (e.g., a very common name used for two clearly different people in books centuries apart, flagged in the registry). The **name registry** enforces this.

## Readability rules for learners and natives

- **Name budget.** At most five named characters per story (protagonist included). Others are referred to by role: "the steward", "the boy with the goats", "her mother's sister".
- **Distinct shapes.** No two named characters in one story share an initial letter, and no two have similar sound shapes (Myron/Mikon, Theron/Thero). Different lengths help.
- **Pronounceable.** Prefer names of two or three syllables for protagonists. Longer names are allowed when they are the period norm (many Egyptian, Nahuatl, or Mesopotamian names); then give a short form the characters themselves would use, if attested, or let the narrator use the full name consistently.
- **One spelling.** The series bible fixes a transliteration convention (for Greek: *k* or *c*, *-os* or *-us*; for Egyptian: Egyptological spelling conventions; for Chinese: pinyin; for Nahuatl: a standard orthography). Never mix within a series.
- **No diacritic overload.** Use the simplest standard transliteration the bible allows; reserve diacritics for the Historical Note if needed.

## Where names come from — sources by tradition

Each series bible must name its onomastic sources. Typical starting points (verify availability and use per run; the list is illustrative, not exhaustive):

| Tradition | Typical sources of attested names |
|---|---|
| Mycenaean Greek | Linear B personal names (e.g., in standard Mycenaean lexica and corpora of Pylos, Knossos, Mycenae, Thebes tablets) |
| Minoan Crete | No readable native names (Linear A undeciphered); Egyptian New Kingdom records include a writing board with "Keftiu names" (British Museum); otherwise rule-based invention, declared |
| Greek, Archaic to Roman | *Lexicon of Greek Personal Names* (LGPN), inscriptions, papyri (for Egypt) |
| Ancient Egypt | Ranke's *Die ägyptischen Personennamen* and later prosopographies; tomb and stela inscriptions |
| Mesopotamia | Cuneiform archives and prosopographies (e.g., for Neo-Assyrian, Old Babylonian, Ur III periods) |
| Rome | Inscriptions (epigraphic databases), nomenclature handbooks; tria nomina rules for citizens, single names for slaves |
| Norse / Viking Age | Runic inscriptions (runic-text databases), Icelandic sagas and *Landnámabók* (with care for date of writing) |
| Anglo-Saxon England | Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England (PASE) |
| Medieval Europe | Charters, tax rolls, parish and court records of the region |
| China, Japan, Korea | Period-appropriate naming conventions from dynastic and local records; respect surname-first order |
| Nahua (Aztec) | Names recorded in early colonial codices and documents; calendrical names |
| Maya | Mostly elite names from inscriptions; ordinary names largely unknown → declared invention by rule |
| Andes (Inca) | Quechua/Aymara names in early colonial administrative records |

Always check the date of the source against the story's window. A saga written down in the 13th century is a source for 13th-century naming, and only cautiously for the 10th.

## When names are unknown

For civilisations whose ordinary names are lost (Minoan Crete, the Indus Valley, Olmec, much of prehistoric Europe), the bible must choose and declare one of these strategies, in this order of preference:

1. **Names recorded by neighbours** (e.g., Egyptian lists of Keftiu names for Minoan Crete), used as a small, honest pool.
2. **Bynames and descriptive names** rendered in plain English, used sparingly ("Red-Hand", "the Fisher's Girl") where the culture plausibly used such names. Good for minor characters; tiring for protagonists.
3. **Rule-based invented names** that follow the sound patterns of attested words or neighbouring languages, declared in the bible with the rule, and acknowledged once per book (in the "About this book" introduction): "We do not know what these people called themselves; the names in this book are invented to sound right, not taken from records."

Never present a reconstructed or invented name as attested in a Historical Note. Never borrow the names of a later culture that conquered or replaced this one (no Classical Greek names for Minoans) without saying so — and prefer not to.

## Name registry

Every series keeps a registry (`name_registry.md`, schema in templates). Each entry records:

- Name (as spelled in the text)
- Sex
- Book and story
- Role (protagonist / secondary / mentioned)
- Status (free / unfree / foreign / elite / priestly …)
- Attestation: source and date range, or "invented by rule <X>"
- Meaning, if known and useful

The slate allocates all protagonist and main secondary names; book pitches and frameworks add minor names from the reserve pool; drafts may not introduce a new name without adding it to the registry and passing the onomastics check.

## Forbidden-name list

Each series bible maintains a forbidden list for its civilisation: names of gods, heroes, mythical figures, famous historical persons, famous artists and writers, and titles. Examples for Greece: Achilles, Agamemnon, Ajax, Andromache, Ariadne, Athena, Daedalus, Helen, Hector, Icarus, Jason, Medea, Minos, Nestor, Odysseus, Oedipus, Orestes, Orpheus, Penelope, Penthesilea, Perseus, Pythia, Theseus; Aristotle, Euphronios, Homer, Pericles, Phidias, Plato, Sappho, Socrates, Solon, Terpander. Exception only if attested as a common name in the window **and** approved in the bible.

## Severity guidance

- **Critical:** a famous person's or god's name given to an ordinary protagonist; a name from the wrong civilisation or a much later period; a name reused for a different character within the same series without declared exception.
- **Major:** an unattested name presented as attested in a Note; a name of the wrong sex or status type; a mythic-hero name on a secondary character; two characters in one story with confusable names; inconsistent spelling convention.
- **Minor:** a plausible but unverified name not yet in the registry; a name that is hard to pronounce where an easier attested alternative exists.
- **Nit:** a single spelling slip.

The onomastics gate in every audit is **zero Critical, zero Major**.
