# Mentalité Doctrine — No Modern Minds in Ancient Heads

Read before drafting any premise, story card, framework, or prose, and before every audit. This is a **hard rule** of the Living History line: characters and narrator must think inside their world. A single modern thought placed in a premodern head breaks the spell for the native reader faster than a wrong object does.

*Mentalité* is the historians' word for the habits of mind of a period: what people took for granted, feared, hoped, valued, found funny or shameful, and could not even imagine. Objects can be checked against a museum record; minds have to be checked against this document and against what sources say about the period's values.

---

## Why this matters more than objects

A wrong object is a mistake. A wrong mind is a lie about the people. When a character of a premodern world reasons like a present-day professional, the reader no longer meets a person of that world — they meet a modern person in costume, and the story becomes flattery of the present.

The opposite failure is just as serious: making past people stupid, superstitious in a sneering way, or uniformly brutal. They were as intelligent, funny, and complicated as we are. They thought with different furniture.

## The core principle

**The world as they saw it is the world of the story.** What the characters' culture held to be true — about gods, spirits, omens, pollution, fate, the dead — is true inside the story, for the characters and for the narrator speaking from inside their view. The narrator does not wink, explain, or correct. Explanation, where needed, belongs in the Historical Note, in plain words, after the story is over.

## The modern-leak catalogue

Every audit role checks premises and prose against these families. The series bible extends this list for its civilisation.

### 1. Progress, innovation, and the future
- **Leak:** novelty valued for itself; talk of improving systems, new ideas, innovation, the future of a craft; a protagonist who invents something that later history made famous; any narrator remark that looks ahead to later history or to modern discovery of the past.
- **Period-true:** in most premodern cultures the old way is the good way; authority comes from ancestors, gods, and custom. Change happens, but people justify it in their own terms — as a return to the proper way, a divine gift, a necessity, a thing learned from strangers. No narrator foresight, ever.

### 2. Economics and management
- **Leak:** efficiency, productivity, profit margin, investment, capital, percentages, supply and demand, market forces, business plan, budget, proposal, projections, sustainability, resource management, deadline and schedule in the modern sense.
- **Period-true:** obligations, shares, portions, gifts, debts, oaths, tribute, rations, what is owed to the god, the house, the lord — in the period's own terms and units. Calculation existed and could be sharp.

### 3. Politics and rights
- **Leak:** speeches about equality, human rights, individual freedom, representation, fair taxation, accountability, "having a voice" as a value in itself; institutions that appear before their attested date; citizens of states that were in fact kin groups or chiefdoms.
- **Period-true:** loyalty to kin, house, lord, community, god; honour and shame; precedent and custom; the duties of rank. Ordinary people resist, complain, bargain, flee, and rebel — for bread, land, honour, justice as their world understood it — never in the vocabulary of modern political theory.

### 4. Gender and social-justice rhetoric
- **Leak:** characters who argue against their society's rules in modern terms; men who reflect on equality as a principle; enslaved characters who speak of universal rights; narrators who editorialise on injustice.
- **Period-true:** women and unfree people had real agency, skill, influence, cunning, and inner lives — exercised **inside** the period's structures: through kin, property rights where they existed, religious roles, crafts, alliances, persuasion, refusal, endurance. Injustice is shown, never preached; the reader draws the conclusion. When someone breaks a rule, the reasons are practical, personal, or religious in the period's terms — never a theory.

### 5. Religion, scepticism, and the supernatural
- **Leak:** characters who doubt the gods as a default stance; ritual treated as merely useful; divine signs explained in natural terms; a narrator who treats belief as quaint; any debunking of the sacred.
- **Period-true:** gods, spirits, ancestors, omens, and pollution are real features of the world for the characters. Scepticism existed in some periods and milieux; where the sources attest it, it may appear — in that period's own terms, and never as the default mind of an ordinary person.

### 6. Psychology and the self
- **Leak:** therapy vocabulary (processing, closure, trauma, boundaries, self-esteem, identity, finding oneself, healing journey); characters who treat their own personality as a project; motivational aphorisms that summarise a lesson.
- **Period-true:** feelings are real and strong, but understood through the period's frame — the body, the gods, honour and shame, duty, fate. Most of the time, show feeling through action and body and leave it unnamed.

### 7. Childhood, age, and the life course
- **Leak:** childhood as a universally protected stage of play and schooling; adolescence as a social category; careers chosen by personal passion; retirement; romantic love as the normal basis of marriage where marriage was arranged.
- **Period-true:** the life course as the sources show it for this world — work, rites, inheritance, alliance. Love stories are welcome, but they must feel the weight of the period's structures.

### 8. Time, number, and measurement
- **Leak:** minutes, seconds, clock hours where no clocks existed; a seven-day week where none existed; our-era dates in dialogue or narration; statistics, averages, percentages; modern or foreign units in narration; birthdays and exact ages where ages were not counted that way.
- **Period-true:** the period's own divisions of the day and year, or neutral English descriptions of them; the period's units or neutral English quantities. Our-era dates appear only in front matter and Historical Notes.

### 9. Knowledge of the world
- **Leak:** germs and hygiene as disease prevention, modern science, modern geography and country names, continental identities, awareness of the scholarly names and labels later given to their own period or culture, awareness of which technology "has a future".
- **Period-true:** their geography, their names for peoples and places (or neutral descriptions), their medicine, their cosmos. Scholarly labels and modern names appear only in front matter and Notes.

### 10. Institutions that did not exist yet
- **Leak:** institutions placed before their attested date or outside their region — guild-like bodies, police, detectives, intelligence services, banks, universal schooling, hospitals, prisons as punishment where other penalties were used, licences and certifications, organised crime.
- **Period-true:** the institutions attested for that window, described in the period's own terms or plain English.

### 11. Language register of narrator and dialogue
- **Leak:** modern idiom and slang; modern sarcasm patterns and jokes that need modern knowledge; therapy, corporate, and bureaucratic vocabulary; equally, fake-archaic English.
- **Period-true:** plain, timeless English; images taken from the characters' own world; humour that works from inside their life. See `cefr_register_<LANG>.md` → *Period-neutral English*.

### 12. Morality and sentiment
- **Leak:** modern attitudes to animals, to nature as fragile, to violence the period considered normal, to death as unusual; modern cynicism presented as worldly wisdom.
- **Period-true:** their ethics — sometimes harsh, sometimes tender, often surprising. Characters can be shocked, kind, or cruel, but for their own reasons.

## How to write the different mind well

1. **Start from what they took for granted.** Before drafting, list five things the protagonist would never question. Let the story rest on them.
2. **Let belief do work.** Rituals, omens, curses, vows change what characters do. Belief is a motor of plot, not decoration.
3. **Give them their own intelligence.** Characters are shrewd in their own terms; cleverness is shown through the period's tools and knowledge.
4. **Find period-true conflict.** The best stories turn on conflicts the period itself recognised — documented in the bible and dossier.
5. **Translate feelings into the period's body and words** — simply enough for the band. Do not modernise feeling; show it.
6. **Keep the narrator inside.** The narrator knows only what a person of that world could know, plus the immediate physical scene. No commentary from outside, no foresight, no irony at the characters' expense for not knowing what we know.
7. **Let the Note do the outside work.** Where a modern reader needs context, the Historical Note gives it, plainly and briefly.

## Tests for auditors

- **The consultant test.** Could this line be spoken in a modern office, therapy session, or political rally without sounding odd? If yes, it is probably a leak.
- **The costume test.** Remove names and objects. Does the character still think like a person of that world?
- **The hindsight test.** Does anyone — character or narrator — know or hint at what history will bring?
- **The sneer test.** Does the text treat the period's beliefs as foolish, or its people as primitive?
- **The sermon test.** Does any passage explain the moral of the story or of history?

## Severity guidance

- **Critical:** a premise or resolution that only works with a modern mind; narrator foresight of later history in the story text; a sermon ending.
- **Major:** a character's speech or thought built on a modern concept; modern idiom in narration; a sneer at belief; repeated minor leaks in one story.
- **Minor:** an isolated modern-tinged word that does not carry a concept.
- **Nit:** borderline word choice a careful editor would vary.

The mentalité gate in every audit is **zero Critical, zero Major**.
