# Series Architecture — From a Civilisation to a Set of Books

Read before the survey (P1), the series concept (P2), the bible (P3), the slate (P6), and every audit of series architecture.

---

## The unit: a book is a world-window

A book is never a theme. A book is a **world-window**: one civilisation, one period, one region or social world, at a scale where ten ordinary people could plausibly have lived within reach of one another's stories.

A world-window has:

- **A date window** — usually 50 to 150 years; longer only for slow-changing periods with thin evidence; never so long that technology, religion, or politics change materially inside it.
- **A place** — a region, a city and its countryside, an island group, a river valley, a network seen from one end.
- **A social world** — the kind of society this window was, so that the ten stories share rules.
- **An evidence base** — enough attested material to anchor ten stories and a reserve. The survey rates evidence density 1–5 per window.
- **A distinct flavour** — something the reader gets here and nowhere else in the series.

Themed cross-period volumes are **not used by default**: they duplicate the chronological books and blur dates. A themed book is admitted only if the user asks for it and the slate proves no overlap.

## How many books

The number follows the civilisation, not a habit. The concept proposes N between **8 and 20**:

- Long, well-documented civilisations with sharp internal changes can sustain the upper range.
- Short or thinly documented civilisations sustain the lower range, or fold into a neighbouring series.
- For each candidate book the concept states evidence density and the number of solid anchors found. A book with fewer than about 15 solid anchors is merged or dropped.

## Periodisation craft

1. **Start from the scholars' periods**, without being ruled by them: split a period that is too long, merge one that is too thin.
2. **Split by region** where region changes the world.
3. **Split by social world** where it changes the stories.
4. **Respect chronology debates.** The bible records them and chooses a dating convention for front matter; stories avoid depending on a contested date.
5. **Cover the arc** from emergence to transformation or end, with the main turning points inside or between books.
6. **Mind the gaps.** A thinly documented window may be one honest book anchored in the little that is known, with the bible marking the higher share of invention.

## What makes books distinct

Each book differs from every other in at least three of: date window, region, social world, dominant economy, cult landscape, technology level, language and script situation, political situation, key evidence types. Two books that differ only by a few decades are one book.

## Coverage of people

Across the series, the slate tracks protagonist distribution so that the series as a whole includes women and men; children, adults, and elders; free, unfree, and dependent people; the few rich, the many ordinary, and the poor; insiders and outsiders; every major setting type; every major occupation group the evidence documents. Per-book quotas are in `story_forms.md`.

## The series title and promise

The title is short and evocative, never a slogan. The promise is one paragraph about what the reader gets across all books, without superlatives.

## The line registry (optional)

When the user keeps `line_registry.md` across all Living History series, P0 reads it and P12 appends to it. It records, per series, the civilisation, the books, and the story concepts, so that concepts common across civilisations are not repeated in the same shape.

## Legacy material

When the user supplies an older plan or older books for the same civilisation, treat them as a **quarry, not a blueprint**: audit them in P2; keep the concepts that are strong and say why; never copy their facts without verification; never copy their names; never copy their prose. The book pipeline writes everything fresh.
