# Series Architecture — From a Civilisation to a Set of Books

Read before the survey (P1), the series concept (P2), the bible (P3), the slate (P6), and every audit of series architecture. This document explains how a civilisation is cut into books, how many books, and what makes each book distinct.

---

## The unit: a book is a world-window

A book is not a theme ("Women's Worlds", "Gods and Mortals", "The Craftsman's Quarter"). A book is a **world-window**: one civilisation, one period, one region or social world, at a scale where ten ordinary people could plausibly have lived within reach of one another's stories.

A good world-window has:

- **A date window** — usually 50 to 150 years; longer only for slow-changing periods with thin evidence, never so long that technology, religion, or politics change materially inside it.
- **A place** — a region, a city and its countryside, an island group, a river valley, a trade network seen from one end.
- **A social world** — what kind of society it is in this window (palace economy; village chiefdoms; city-states; an empire's province; a frontier), so the stories share rules.
- **An evidence base** — enough attested material (sites, objects, texts, images) to anchor ten stories. The survey rates evidence density 1–5 per window.
- **A distinct flavour** — something the reader gets here and nowhere else in the series.

Themed cross-period volumes (all the children, all the festivals, all the sea stories) are **not used by default**: they duplicate the chronological books and blur dates. A series may include one themed book only if the bible justifies it and the slate proves no overlap.

## How many books

The number of books follows the civilisation, not a fixed number. The series concept proposes N between **8 and 20**:

- Long, well-documented civilisations with sharp changes (Ancient Greece, Egypt, Rome, China) can sustain 15–20.
- Shorter or more thinly documented ones (Minoan Crete alone, the Etruscans, the Indus Valley) may sustain 6–10, or be folded into a neighbouring series.
- The concept phase states, for each candidate book, its evidence density and how many solid anchors the survey found. A book with fewer than about 15 solid anchors (for ten stories plus reserve) is merged or dropped.

## Periodisation craft

1. **Start from the scholars' periods**, but do not be ruled by them. A conventional period can be too long (split it by region or sub-period) or too thin (merge it).
2. **Split by region where the region changes the world**: Classical Athens and Classical Sparta are different books; Egypt's Delta and Upper Egypt may be.
3. **Split by social world where it changes the stories**: a capital and its frontier; a palace and its villages.
4. **Respect the debates.** Where chronology is contested (high and low chronologies; disputed collapse dates), the bible records the debate and chooses a convention for front matter, and books avoid stories that depend on the contested date.
5. **Cover the arc.** The series as a whole should let a reader travel from the civilisation's emergence to its end or transformation, with the main turning points inside or between books.
6. **Mind the gaps.** Where evidence is thin (a "dark age"), one book may honestly cover a long window with stories anchored in the few things we know — and the bible marks the higher share of invention.

## What makes books distinct

The concept and slate audits check that each book differs from every other in at least three of: date window, region, social world, dominant economy, dominant religion/cult landscape, technology level, language/script situation, political situation, key evidence types. Two books that differ only in date by fifty years are one book.

## Coverage of people

Across the series, the slate tracks protagonist distribution so the series as a whole includes: women and men; children, adults, elders; free, unfree, and dependent; rich (few), ordinary (most), and poor; insiders and outsiders; city, village, sea, road, sanctuary, and wild land; every major occupation group the evidence documents. Per-book quotas are in `story_forms.md`.

## The series title and promise

The series title is short and evocative, not a slogan: the bible fixes it. The series promise (one paragraph) says what the reader gets across all books — never "an unprecedented intimate portrait", never superlatives.

## The line registry (optional)

When the user keeps a registry of all Living History series (`line_registry.md`), P0 reads it and P12 appends to it. It records, per series: civilisation, books, story concepts (occupation + form + central object), and counts of forms. The slate uses it to avoid repeating the most common concepts in the same shape across civilisations (see `story_forms.md` → *Series-level variety*).

## Legacy material

When the user supplies an older plan or older books for the same civilisation (as with the Ancient Greece material that seeded this line), treat it as a **quarry, not a blueprint**:

- Audit it in P2 with the failure gallery in mind.
- Keep concepts that were strong (and say why); fix or drop the rest.
- Never copy its facts without verification; never copy its names.
- Its strong stories can inspire story cards, but the new book pipeline writes everything fresh.
