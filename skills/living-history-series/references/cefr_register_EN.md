# CEFR Register Pack — English (EN-UK default) — B1, B1–B2, B2

Read before drafting (book P5), before every CEFR audit, and before the publish-ready edit. This pack governs sentence architecture, tenses, cohesion, vocabulary, period terms, and the English of the past. It is written to be read alongside `quality_standard.md`: the register is the instrument, not the cage. Real literature is written inside it.

The default band for Living History is **B1–B2**. B1 and B2 books are supported; the spec declares the band.

---

## Spelling and typography

- **British spelling** by default: colour, harbour, civilisation, organise, travelled, grey, mould, plough, jewellery, centre, defence.
- Use "learned/burned/dreamed" or "learnt/burnt/dreamt" consistently — the bible decides; default **learned, burned, dreamed** (more learner-friendly).
- Avoid "whilst" and "amongst" (old-fashioned for learners); use "while", "among".
- Single quotation marks for dialogue are acceptable in UK publishing, but this line uses **double quotation marks** for dialogue for consistency across languages and annotation tools, with single marks inside.
- Spaced en dash or unspaced em dash: the bible picks one; default unspaced em dash (—).
- Numbers in narration as words up to one hundred; dates (BCE/CE) only in front matter and Notes, written "c. 1450 BCE".

## Sentence architecture

### B1
- Mean 9–13 words per sentence. ~60% short (5–12), ~30% medium (13–20), ~10% longer (21–28). Nothing over 28.
- One level of subordination per sentence ("She waited until the boat came in."). A rare two-level sentence must read cleanly.
- Vary openings: subject first; time or place phrase; subordinator first; dialogue first; single word.

### B1–B2 (default)
- Mean 11–15 words. ~50% short (5–12), ~35% medium (13–20), ~15% longer (21–32). Rare sentences up to 35.
- One level of subordination is the norm; two subordinate elements allowed when one is light (a prepositional or adverbial phrase).
- Light participle openings ("Knowing nothing, he waited.") and fronted objects ("That much, she understood.") allowed.

### B2
- Mean 13–18 words. ~40% short, ~40% medium (13–22), ~20% longer (23–38). Very rare sentences up to 42.
- Two levels of subordination freely; three only when the sentence reads cleanly.
- Participle clauses, absolute phrases, occasional inversion for emphasis.

At every band: **variation is the voice.** A short sentence after two longer ones lands hard. Monotone length is a Major finding.

## Tenses

- **Narrative tense:** past simple and past continuous, with past perfect for one-step backreference. This is the default for all bands.
- **B1:** present/past simple and continuous, present perfect, past perfect (light), *will* and *going to*, common modals; first and second conditionals. Past perfect continuous and third conditional rarely.
- **B1–B2:** adds past perfect continuous, third conditional (once or twice per story), *used to / would* for past habits, passives in past perfect.
- **B2:** full repertoire; free indirect discourse.
- **Present-tense narration** is allowed in at most one story per book (see `story_forms.md`) and must still keep the band's architecture.

## Cohesion devices

- **B1:** and, but, or, so, because, when, while, after, before, if, although; then, later, after that, meanwhile.
- **B1–B2:** adds however, even though, unless, whereas, besides, on the other hand; *therefore / moreover* only once every few pages.
- **B2:** full repertoire, used in measure; still avoid the academic connectives (*notwithstanding, insofar as*) in fiction.

## Vocabulary

### The rule of the concrete
Concrete nouns — tools, foods, plants, animals, clothes, materials, parts of a ship or a loom — may go beyond the band's core vocabulary when they make the world real. Annotated editions and glossaries will be produced later, and context should make the meaning clear on first use. Abstract vocabulary above the band is not allowed: prefer *sad, afraid, ashamed, tired* to *melancholy, apprehensive, mortified, enervated* at B1 and B1–B2.

### Anglo-Saxon first
Prefer *walk, look, take, give, keep, burn, pour, grind, weave* to Latinate equivalents. Prefer *ask* to *enquire*, *start* to *commence*, *help* to *assist*.

### Low-frequency density
Even when every hard word is concrete, a sentence with three rare words is tiring. Aim for no more than one low-frequency word per sentence on average, and no more than two in any sentence. Cluster rare words only where a scene needs a dense physical moment (the inside of a workshop), then return to plain language.

### Phrasal verbs and idioms
Common phrasal verbs (look for, put on, take off, give up, come back, go on, set out, put out) are welcome; they are the natural grammar of English narrative. Idioms must be **period-neutral** and transparent (see below). Avoid opaque or modern idioms entirely.

## Period terms — how many and how to introduce them

A period term is a word from the culture's language or a specialist word for its things: *wanax, pithos, rhyton, agora, peplos, ka, shabti, jarl, thing, tlatoani, chinampa*.

**Budget.** At most **6 new period terms per story** at B1–B2 (4 at B1, 8 at B2). A term reused in later stories of the same book does not count again.

**The English-first rule.** If plain English carries the meaning without loss, use English: "the king" not "the wanax", "a storage jar" not "a pithos", "the market square" not "the agora". Use the period term only when:
- it names something with no English equivalent (a *ka*, a *thing* assembly), or
- the difference matters to the story (the *wanax* is not quite a king, and the story turns on that), or
- the characters would use it as a name for a specific thing (the *Agora* as a place name).

**Introduce by context, never by definition.** Wrong: "The pithos, which is a large storage jar, stood in the corner." Right: "She lifted the lid of the great pithos. It was taller than her son, and it smelled of last year's oil." The reader understands; nothing is explained.

**Consistency.** Each book has a rendering table (in the dossier) fixing how each thing is named. One thing, one name, all book long.

**Spelling of period terms.** As in the bible's transliteration convention. Italics: none in the running text (cleaner for annotation tools and learners); the term is introduced by context.

## Period-neutral English — the language of the past

The narrator and the characters speak modern, plain, standard English — not fake-archaic and not contemporary-colloquial.

**Avoid fake-archaic:** thee, thou, ye, hath, doth, forsooth, methinks, 'tis, prithee, mayhap, verily, "Hark!", "Pray tell".

**Avoid contemporary-colloquial and modern-marked:** OK, okay, guys, cool, kids (for children — use "children", "boys", "girls"), "no way", "you know what I mean", "big deal", "awesome", "stressed", "focus", "team", "deal with it", "boss", "job" (in the modern sense — prefer "work", "task"), "weekend", "minutes", "seconds", "hours" for clock time (see `mentalite_doctrine.md`).

**Images from their world.** Similes and metaphors draw on things the characters know: animals, weather, crops, craft, the gods, the body, the sea. Not "like a machine", "like clockwork", "like a camera", "like electricity", "like a factory", "like a battery", "like a robot".

**Swearing and oaths.** Characters swear by their gods and their dead, in the period's manner: "By the Lady of the mountain", "By my father's grave". No modern swear words; no generic "Gods!" if the culture swore by specific deities.

**Forms of address.** As the period used them, rendered plainly: "lord", "lady", "master", "mistress", "mother", "uncle", "old man" (as respectful address where appropriate). No "Sir" and "Madam" with modern flavour; no "Mr" or "Mrs".

## Dialogue

- People speak in short, natural sentences; contractions are fine in dialogue and allowed in narration.
- Each speaker should be recognisable without tags: an old man repeats himself, a child asks direct questions, a trader bargains in numbers, a priestess speaks in formulas.
- Tags mostly "said" and "asked". No adverb tags ("she said angrily").
- Long speeches are rare and simple; a character who explains the theme is a Major finding.

## Historical Notes — register

Notes are written in plain, clear English at the top of the book's band or one step above (B2 for B1–B2 books; B1–B2 for B1 books; B2 for B2 books). They may use words like *archaeologists, evidence, excavation, tablet, inscription, scholars, record* — each a high-value word for any reader of history. They avoid academic jargon (*hypobranchial gland, enzymatic, carburisation, synthesised, Neopalatial*) unless the term is immediately and simply explained, and even then sparingly. Sentences average 14–18 words, nothing over 30. See `historical_note_craft.md`.

## What the CEFR auditor samples

- Three passages of ~200 words per story (opening, middle, ending): sentence-length distribution against the band.
- Every sentence over the band's maximum length.
- Subordination above the band's cap.
- Tenses outside the band's repertoire.
- Cohesion devices above the band.
- Period-term count per story against the budget; introduction by context.
- Abstract vocabulary spikes.
- Fake-archaic and modern-colloquial English.
- Note register.

**Severity:** a single over-band sentence is Minor; repeated drift in a story is Major; a story that reads as a band higher (or lower) than declared is Critical.

## Adding another language

To run the line in another language, create `cefr_register_<LANG>.md` with the same sections: spelling and typography conventions; sentence architecture per band; tenses; cohesion devices; vocabulary rules; period-term policy (with the language's own conventions for rendering foreign terms); period-neutral register (that language's fake-archaic and modern-colloquial traps); dialogue; Note register; audit sampling. The pipeline must not draft in a language without its pack.
