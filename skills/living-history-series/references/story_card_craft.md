# Story Card Craft — From Evidence to Stories

Read before the slate (P6), the book pitches (P9), and their audits. A story card is the seed of one story. The book pipeline will build a dossier, a framework, and a draft from it; a weak card produces a weak story no matter how good the drafting.

---

## Where stories come from: anchors

Every story grows from at least one **anchor** — a specific piece of evidence that makes the story's world concrete and checkable:

- **An object:** a seal, a broken jar, a toy, a loom weight, a curse tablet, a set of weights, an amulet, a shipwreck's cargo.
- **A document:** a tablet listing women workers and their children; a letter; a law; a contract; a list of offerings; a court speech.
- **A place:** a workshop floor, a house, a harbour, a shrine, a fortification, a road, a mine.
- **An image:** a fresco, a vase painting, a tomb painting, a relief.
- **A practice:** transhumance, a festival, a funeral rite, a form of oath, a way of making something.
- **An event:** a destruction, a storm season, a migration, a festival year — at the scale an ordinary person lived it.

The survey (P1) collects anchors; the slate assigns them. One anchor per story is the minimum; two or three make a richer world. No anchor may be the centre of more than one story in a series.

**Good anchors produce questions.** The Linear B tablets that list women textile workers with their children make you ask: who were these women, where were they taken from, what did their children become? That question is a story. A fresco that everyone knows produces fewer questions; use famous anchors sparingly.

## From anchor to engine

An anchor is not a story. The engine is the question the reader wants answered. Move from the anchor to a person, and from the person to trouble:

1. **Who is near this thing?** Not the king who owned the seal — the boy who carried it, the woman who pressed it into clay, the slave who dusted the room.
2. **What does this person want today?** Concrete: to keep a job, to marry someone, to get a brother back, to pay a debt, to be chosen, to hide a mistake, to see the sea.
3. **What stands in the way?** A person, a rule, a god, the weather, a lie, a lack.
4. **What form does that trouble take?** Choose from `story_forms.md` — and choose against the book's other stories.
5. **How does it turn?** Two turns minimum.
6. **How does it end?** An image, an act, a line — and a cost.

## The protagonist

- An invented, ordinary person of the window (`line_doctrine.md`).
- Name from the registry's reserve pool (`onomastics.md`).
- Age, sex, status, household, occupation — specific.
- **One want** (concrete, today), **one fear**, **one physical detail**, **one habit of speech or gesture**.
- **One thing they take for granted** that a modern reader would not (`mentalite_doctrine.md`) — and ideally the story leans on it.

## The plausibility check for plotted forms

Every card with a plotted form (mystery, theft, intrigue, dispute, journey, disaster, chase, contest) states, briefly:
- the **realism anchor** (the attested kind of trouble);
- the **scale** (local; or elite-level seen from below — max one per book);
- the **motive** (period motive);
- the **means** of discovery and resolution (period means);
- the **coincidence** (at most one, creating trouble only);
- the **cost**.
See `story_forms.md` → *Plausibility contract*.

## What a slate entry contains (short form)

One line per field (P6):
- Title (working).
- Protagonist: name, sex, age, status, occupation.
- Place and date (c.), season.
- Form, shape.
- Engine question (one sentence).
- Anchor(s) with evidence status and source key.
- Ending type.
- Link (optional).

## What a pitch card contains (full form)

In the book pitch (P9), each story card runs to 600–900 words and adds:
- Logline (one sentence that would make a native reader want the story).
- Protagonist sheet (want, fear, physical detail, speech habit, taken-for-granted belief).
- Up to four other named characters (from the reserve), each with one specific detail.
- Setting specifics (what the reader will see, hear, smell — from the bible's palette, all checked).
- Beats: 5–8 turns from opening to ending; where the engine question is posed; the two turns; the ending image.
- Plausibility block (for plotted forms).
- Mentalité note: what the protagonist believes that drives or colours the story.
- Opening device and ending type (checked against the book's variety table).
- Anchor block: each anchor with evidence status, what is ATTESTED/INFERRED, what is invented, source keys.
- Traps: the three to five anachronism or mentalité traps most likely in this particular story.
- Note plan: what the Historical Note will say (evidence named, known, debated, invented).
- Period terms planned (within budget).
- Link (optional, invisible to single-story readers).

## Card failure modes

- **Theme instead of story:** "A woman experiences the role of religion in daily life." No engine.
- **Task list:** "She gathers saffron, eats lunch, sees a snake, returns." No turns.
- **Template:** "The palace orders twenty pieces in four days." (S2 in `anti_formula.md`.)
- **Celebrity drift:** "Socrates' last day", "Learning from Phidias" — the famous person takes over.
- **Modern premise:** "A woman fights for the right to…" in modern terms; "a farmer proposes a fairer tax system".
- **Contrivance:** a commoner uncovers a royal conspiracy; a prince visits the workshop at night to talk philosophy.
- **Famous-anchor pile-up:** three stories built on the series' three most famous artefacts.
- **Telegraphic card:** "Wine sweeter knowing ending." A card must be written in full sentences that a book run can act on.
