# Story Card Craft — From Evidence to Stories

Read before the slate (P6), the book pitches (P9), and their audits. A story card is the seed of one story. A weak card produces a weak story no matter how good the drafting.

---

## Anchors

Every story grows from at least one **anchor**: a specific piece of evidence — an object, a document, a place, an image, a practice, or an event at human scale — that makes the story's world concrete and checkable.

- The survey collects anchors; the slate assigns them.
- One anchor per story is the minimum; two or three make a richer world.
- No anchor is the centre of more than one story in a series.
- At most two famous anchors per book. Lesser-known evidence usually raises better questions about ordinary lives.
- A good anchor produces questions about the people near it. Those questions are where stories start.

## From anchor to engine

An anchor is not a story. The engine is the question the reader wants answered. Move from the evidence to a person, and from the person to trouble:

1. **Who is near this thing?** An ordinary person who made, carried, used, guarded, cleaned, sold, stole, or feared it.
2. **What does this person want, now?** Something concrete.
3. **What stands in the way?** A person, a rule, a god, the weather, a lie, a lack.
4. **What form does the trouble take?** Chosen from `story_forms.md`, and chosen against the book's other stories.
5. **How does it turn?** Two turns at least.
6. **How does it end?** On an image, an act, or a line — with a cost.

## The protagonist

- An invented, ordinary person of the window (`line_doctrine.md`).
- Name from the registry's reserve pool (`onomastics.md`).
- Age, sex, status, household, occupation — specific.
- One concrete want, one fear, one physical detail, one habit of speech or gesture.
- One belief they take for granted that a modern reader would not (`mentalite_doctrine.md`), on which the story leans.

## Plausibility block (plotted forms)

Every card with a plotted form states: the realism anchor (the attested kind of trouble, with source); the scale (local, or elite-level seen from below — at most one per book); the period motive; the period means of discovery and resolution; the single coincidence, if any, and that it creates trouble; the cost. See `story_forms.md` §6.

## Slate entry (short form, P6)

One line per field: working title; protagonist (name, sex, age, status, occupation); place, date, season; form and shape; engine question; anchors with status and source keys; opening device; ending type; link (optional).

## Pitch card (full form, P9)

600–900 words in full sentences, adding: a logline that would make a browsing native reader want the story; the protagonist sheet; up to four other named characters, each with one detail; setting specifics (all checked); five to eight beats with the engine posed early, two turns, and the planned ending; the plausibility block; a mentalité note; an anchors table (attested versus invented, with keys); three to five story-specific traps; the Note plan (evidence, known, debated, invented); period terms planned within budget; an optional link.

## Card failure modes

- **Theme instead of story:** a subject without a question.
- **Task list:** activities without turns.
- **Template premise:** anything on the banned-move list of `anti_formula.md`.
- **Celebrity drift:** a famous person or famous artefact taking over the story.
- **Modern premise:** a want, conflict, or resolution that needs a modern mind.
- **Contrivance:** a breach of the plausibility contract.
- **Famous-anchor pile-up:** several stories leaning on the series' best-known evidence.
- **Telegraphic card:** fragments a book run cannot act on.
