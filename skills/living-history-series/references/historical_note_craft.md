# Historical Note Craft

Read before planning a Note (book framework), before writing one (book draft), and before fact-checking or auditing one. The Note is where the line keeps its promise of honesty. It is also the most dangerous place in the book: a false sentence in a story is a mistake inside fiction; a false sentence in a Note is a lie told as history.

---

## What a Note is for

The reader has just finished a story and wants to know: *how much of that was real?* The Note answers in 120–180 words, in plain language, at the story's level or slightly above it (B2 for a B1–B2 book).

A good Note does four things:

1. **Names the evidence.** What we actually have: a fresco, a set of clay tablets, a shipwreck, a law code, a skeleton, a workshop floor, a later writer's account. Where useful, it says how far that evidence is from the story's date.
2. **Says what is known** — plainly, without superlatives.
3. **Says what is uncertain or debated** — honestly, without hedging every sentence.
4. **Says what was invented**, in one sentence, when the invention is central (a festival, a law, a custom, a technique, a name). Minor invented details (a character's name, a dog, a quarrel) need no comment.

It may end with one concrete detail that gives the reader something to carry — an object they can see in a museum today, a word that survived, a site they could visit — as long as it is checked and not a sales pitch.

## What a Note is not

- Not a summary of the story.
- Not a lecture on what the period teaches us.
- Not a list of superlatives ("the finest", "the first", "unique in the world", "unchanged for three thousand years").
- Not academic prose. No jargon without a plain explanation; no footnotes; no citations in the text.
- Not a place to smuggle in facts the story got wrong. If the story is wrong, fix the story.
- Not a place for speculation stated as fact. "May have", "probably", "some scholars think", "we do not know" are honest words; use them where the evidence requires, and not as decoration where it does not.

## Shape

There is no fixed template — ten identical Notes are another formula — but a reliable shape is:

- **Sentence 1–2:** the anchor — the evidence the story grew from.
- **Sentences 3–5:** what that evidence tells us, plainly; one telling detail.
- **Sentence 6:** what remains uncertain or debated.
- **Sentence 7 (if needed):** what the story invented.
- **Last sentence:** a concrete echo (optional).

Vary the openings across a book: some Notes start with an object, some with a place, some with a question, some with a person who found the evidence, some with a word.

## Language

- 120–180 words. Never more than 200.
- Average sentence 14–18 words; nothing over 30.
- Period terms: only those already met in the story, plus at most two new ones, each explained in a few plain words.
- Dates in the form "around 1450 BCE" or "c. 1450 BCE" (the bible decides; be consistent). Centuries as words ("the fifth century BCE").
- Modern place names may be used in Notes to help the reader ("the island of Thera, today's Santorini"; "in modern Turkey") — never in the story.
- Our scholarly names for periods and cultures (Minoan, Mycenaean, Bronze Age, Geometric) belong in Notes and front matter, not in stories.

## Checking

Every sentence of a Note is a claim (see `claim_taxonomy.md`). The fact-check must find each one VERIFIED, VERIFIED-WITH-CAVEAT (and fixed), or honestly framed as uncertain. Any OVERSTATED or CONTRADICTED sentence in a Note is **Critical**.

Typical failures from earlier books (see `failure_gallery.md`):
- "Chemical analysis of pigments reveals … lapis lazuli from Afghanistan" — false for Minoan frescoes.
- "The technology … remained essentially unchanged from the Bronze Age" — false.
- "The famous 'Treasury of Atreus'" used as evidence for treasuries — it is a tomb.
- "Scribes appear to have been exclusively male" — the evidence cannot show this.
- "Modern rodeo athletes confirm [the leap] is theoretically possible" — inverts the actual debate.
- A Note that praises an "innovation" the story invented as if it were real art history.

## Example (illustrative only — facts must be verified in any real run)

> **Historical Note**
> The idea for this story comes from a wall painting found on the island of Thera, today's Santorini, buried by a volcanic eruption around 3,600 years ago. It shows young women picking crocus flowers and bringing them to a seated goddess. The red threads inside the crocus are saffron, used as a dye, a spice, and probably a medicine. Saffron was costly then as now: it takes many thousands of flowers to make even a small amount. Somewhat later clay tablets from Knossos, written in an early form of Greek, list saffron among the goods the palace counted. Who picked it, and with what ceremonies, we do not know. The harvest ritual in this story, and the old priestess who leads it, are imagined. Wild crocuses of the same family still flower on Greek hillsides every autumn.

Note how it names the evidence, keeps to plain words, marks what is unknown, admits the invention, and ends on something concrete. Any real Note is written from the book's verified dossier, not from this example.
