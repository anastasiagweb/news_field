# Anti-Formula — Banned Phrases, Banned Moves, Quotas

Read before framework design, before drafting each story, before every audit, and before the publish-ready edit. This is the line's defence against the one-template book.

Formula is the silent killer of a collection. One story with "the eternal dance of fire and metal" is a weak sentence; ten stories with the same arc, the same coda, and the same phrases are a dead book. The list below was built from the failures of the previous generation of this kind of collection (see `failure_gallery.md`) and from the habitual tics of machine-written prose. It is enforced mechanically — scan for it — and judged by ear.

---

## Part 1 — Banned structural moves

Each of these may appear **at most the number of times shown per book** (0 = never).

| # | Move | Max per book |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Protagonist wakes before dawn / opening at waking or first light | 1 |
| S2 | An authority (palace, temple, lord) demands an impossible task by a near deadline ("three days to do the work of seven") | 1 |
| S3 | Midday crisis solved by a sudden clever improvisation that turns out better than the plan | 1 |
| S4 | Authority figure arrives, watches, and praises the protagonist ("Better," which was high praise from him) | 1 |
| S5 | Payment doubled / official recognition / seal of approval as reward | 0 |
| S6 | Knowledge passed to the next generation as the closing beat | 1 (only if it is the story's actual subject) |
| S7 | Coda about cycles, seasons, generations, chains, "as it always had, as it always would" | 0 |
| S8 | "Tomorrow there would be new orders/challenges… But tonight…" closing | 0 |
| S9 | Narrator foresight of later history ("none of them knew…", "archaeologists not yet born", "a technique that would become the signature of…", "the last great days of…") | 0 |
| S10 | Sneering bureaucrat or scribe with a tablet as antagonist | 1 per series |
| S11 | The wise elder's aphorism that explains the theme ("Fear makes you run. Respect makes you sing.") | 0 as ending; 1 per book in the body |
| S12 | Character explains the story's moral or the meaning of their work in a speech | 0 |
| S13 | Mentor shows apprentice "the secret", then apprentice succeeds | 2 (as central relationship, see `story_forms.md`) |
| S14 | Night scene of the protagonist alone reflecting on the day | 1 |
| S15 | Rival who becomes a friend because of shared craft at the end | 1 |
| S16 | Foreign merchant/expert who "admits" the local product is better | 0 |
| S17 | Ending where the protagonist "understood something" / "was no longer a child but…" / "felt the deep satisfaction of…" | 0 |
| S18 | Rhetorical list of everything the story showed ("They had won their race against weather, salvaged…") | 0 |

## Part 2 — Banned phrases and phrase families

Never use, in narration or dialogue. Variants and paraphrases count. Each occurrence is a finding (Minor; Major if two or more in one story; Critical if the book shows a pattern).

**Generations and continuity**
- "generation after generation", "passed down through generations", "from hand to hand", "his father's father", "back beyond counting", "a link in a chain", "the chain must not break", "as it always had, as it always would", "the ancient rhythm", "the cycle continued"

**Eternity and grandeur**
- "eternal" (any use as decoration), "the eternal dance", "timeless", "immortal" (as praise), "for centuries to come", "older than any palace", "outlasting queens and palaces"

**Value clichés**
- "worth more than its weight in gold/silver", "worth more than most families saw in a year/lifetime", "worth a fortune", "priceless"

**Work and time clichés**
- "three days to do the work of seven", "the palace wanted double", "the impossible order", "Production is down", "there was no time", "every moment counted"

**Machine-prose tics**
- "a testament to", "a tapestry of", "a symphony of", "a dance of/between", "the weight of [abstract noun]", "something shifted", "for the first time in his life" (as a cliché beat), "a breath she didn't know she was holding", "couldn't help but", "little did they know", "in that moment", "the air was thick with", "the scent of X mixed with Y", "a mixture of X and Y" (for emotions), "eyes widened", "his heart pounded/raced", "a smile tugged at", "silence stretched", "time seemed to slow", "every fibre of", "palpable", "intricate", "delicate balance", "deep satisfaction", "a sense of [noun]", "she realised that…" (as an epiphany device), "it was more than X; it was Y", "not just X but Y" (as a reflex), "whispered secrets", "sacred trust", "ancient compact"

**Aphorism factories**
- "X is the mother of Y", "The difference is what you do with it", "Every X teaches you something", "X is just Y's way of Z" ("Pain is just the bees' way of testing your commitment"), "Fame is like perfume…"

**Modern idiom** (also a mentalité issue; see `mentalite_doctrine.md`)
- "no pressure", "deal with it", "focus", "stress", "process", "team", "manage", "handle", "issue", "efficient", "efficiency", "proposal", "projections", "percent", "deadline", "schedule", "upgrade", "feedback"

## Part 3 — Repetition limits across a book

- No distinctive image or simile may appear in two stories (two rivers "like a silver ribbon" is a pattern).
- No distinctive word may appear more than three times across the book unless it is a plain everyday word or a necessary period term (e.g., "secret", "impossible", "sacred", "ancient", "precious", "mysterious" are watched words: max 3 each per book).
- No two stories may share a closing-paragraph structure.
- No two stories may share a first-sentence structure ("[Name] [verb]ed [object] before dawn").
- Names of the gods and invocations are period vocabulary, not repetition — but a book should not end three stories with a prayer.

## Part 4 — How to scan

The drafter scans each story after writing it; the auditors scan the whole book; the publish-ready edit scans again.

1. Search the text for every phrase in Part 2 (and obvious variants).
2. Count each structural move in Part 1 across the book, story by story, in a table.
3. List the first sentence and last paragraph of every story side by side; compare structures.
4. Count watched words (Part 3) across the book.
5. Read every ending aloud: does it end on an image, an act, or a line — or on a summary, a reflection, or a moral?

## Part 5 — What to do instead

- Instead of a coda about generations: end on a concrete object or gesture that holds the story's feeling.
- Instead of praise from authority: let the outcome be ambiguous, costly, private, or noticed only by someone who matters to the protagonist.
- Instead of an impossible order: let the trouble come from inside the protagonist's life — a debt, a lie, a love, an accident, a stranger, a god.
- Instead of the elder's aphorism: let the elder do something unexpected.
- Instead of "worth its weight in gold": say what it would buy in this world — "three goats", "a year of oil for the house".
- Instead of "eternal": cut it.
