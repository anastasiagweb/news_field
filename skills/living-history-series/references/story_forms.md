# Story Forms — Genre Palette, Shapes, Openings, Endings, Links, Plausibility

Read before designing slates and story cards (series), before frameworks (book), and before every variety or plausibility audit.

The Living History line will hold thousands of stories. The reader must never be able to guess the next one. Variety is not decoration; it is the reason a native reader keeps going after the third story. Plotted genres — intrigue, theft, mystery, danger — are among the line's pleasures and are wanted. What ruins them is contrivance. This document gives the palette and the contract that keeps plotted stories plausible.

---

## 1. The form palette

Twenty forms. Each story has one primary form (and may borrow a flavour from a second).

### Quiet and human forms
1. **Craft and mastery.** Skill meets material; something is at stake beyond the object.
2. **Rite of passage.** A first time, an initiation, a threshold of life.
3. **Family drama.** Inheritance, alliance, obligation, rivalry, care within a household or kin group.
4. **Courtship and love.** Love inside the period's structures of marriage, honour, and kin.
5. **Friendship and rivalry.** Two people bound or set against each other.
6. **The stranger.** The meeting of two worlds, seen from one side.
7. **Departure and homecoming.** Leaving, returning, finding home changed.
8. **Loss and mourning.** A death, its rites, and the living who go on — shown through ritual and body.
9. **Comedy and trickster.** A ruse, a misunderstanding, a boast punctured — humour that works from inside the period's life.
10. **The choice.** A moral dilemma with no clean answer, in the period's own terms.

### Plotted forms
11. **Mystery.** Something has happened; someone must find out who or why, using the means of the time. Fair to the reader.
12. **Theft and the small heist.** A theft committed, planned, prevented, or uncovered.
13. **Intrigue and deception.** A lie, a hidden plan, a rivalry — at a scale that fits an ordinary person.
14. **Dispute and judgement.** A quarrel brought before whatever authority the period used.
15. **Journey and adventure.** A road, river, sea, or mountain crossing and its dangers.
16. **Survival and disaster.** Natural or human catastrophe and the hours in which ordinary people decide what to save.
17. **Chase and escape.** Flight, pursuit, hiding, a race against time.
18. **Contest.** A competition as frame; the person is the story.

### Framing forms
19. **The uncanny.** The supernatural told entirely from inside the culture's beliefs, never explained away.
20. **The told tale and the document.** The story reaches us through a voice inside the world — a teller, a letter, a testimony, a prayer — only where the culture had such voices.

## 2. Shapes

A form says *what kind of story*; a shape says *how it moves*. Each story declares one.

- **Single day.** One day that changes something. (The default trap — limit it.)
- **Countdown.** Hours or days to a fixed event.
- **Journey.** From one place to another; each stage a test.
- **Investigation.** A question, a trail, an answer.
- **Reversal.** The situation turns over at the midpoint.
- **Circle.** The ending returns to the opening image, changed.
- **Single scene.** One continuous scene in nearly real time.
- **Seasons.** Months or a year in four or five moments.
- **Then and now.** A present moment opening into one remembered event and back.
- **Told tale / document.** A framing voice.

## 3. Openings

The first sentence does work: it raises a question, places us in a specific body, or shows a specific thing. Opening devices:

- An action already under way.
- A line of dialogue.
- A striking object.
- A sound or smell.
- A problem stated plainly.
- A rule or custom, as the character knows it.
- A place at a precise moment (not a weather report).
- A character's habit or gesture.
- A question the character asks themselves.
- A first line of a told tale or document.

Orientation (who, where, roughly when, what world) is clear within the first 150–200 words — through concrete detail, never through an information paragraph.

## 4. Endings

The last paragraph lands on an image, an act, or a line of dialogue — never on a moral, a summary, or a reflection about the passing of generations. Ending types:

- **Quiet image.**
- **Decision.**
- **Reversal.**
- **Gain with a cost.**
- **Loss accepted.**
- **Comic payoff.**
- **Open question** — deliberate, not accidental.
- **Circle.**
- **Revelation.**
- **Departure.**

Banned codas are listed in `anti_formula.md`.

## 5. Links between stories

Light links reward the reader of the whole book and are invisible to the reader of one story. Permitted kinds: a shared place; an object that appears in two stories; a secondary character glimpsed in another story without needing introduction; one event seen from two sides.

Rules: each link is optional to understanding; no story spoils another; no link requires the reader to remember earlier details; at most four stories per book carry links; links are recorded in the framework.

## 6. The plausibility contract for plotted stories

Every plotted story card and framework states how it meets each clause; auditors check each clause on the page.

1. **Rooted in period reality.** The trouble is a kind of trouble the record shows for this world. The card names this **realism anchor** and its source (from the survey, bible, or dossier).
2. **Scale fits the protagonist.** Ordinary people meet local trouble. Affairs of state, rulers' plots, spy networks, and battles are almost never plausible as an ordinary person's plot; they may appear only as seen from far below. At most one story per book touches elite-level intrigue, and then from below.
3. **Period motives.** The motives the period recognised — material need, debt, honour and shame, revenge, inheritance, marriage, fear of the gods, loyalty to a house, ambition within one's rank. Never modern motives.
4. **Period means — of discovery and of resolution.** Only means attested for this world in the dossier: testimony, oaths, records where writing existed, physical traces people of the time could read, craft and local knowledge, ritual and divine sanction where attested, and the judgement of the authorities the period actually had. No modern investigative logic, no detective profession where none existed.
5. **Coincidence budget: one.** At most one coincidence per story, and only to create trouble — never to solve it.
6. **Proportionate consequences.** Outcomes follow period norms. Victories cost something. No automatic rewards by decree.
7. **Authorities act like authorities.** The powerful do not explain themselves to commoners, do not seek out commoners for confidences, do not reason like modern managers, and do not change their minds because a commoner made a good speech.
8. **No speech solves everything.** Climaxes are actions, tests, choices, objects, or silences.
9. **Antagonists are people.** Their motives are understandable in the period's terms. No villain monologues, no cynical one-liners about power.
10. **Fair play.** In mysteries and intrigues, every clue the solution depends on is on the page before the solution.
11. **Violence mostly off the page.** See `respect_and_darkness.md`.

## 7. The variety contract — quotas for one book of ten stories

The framework and the audits check these numbers. A slate or framework that breaks one is a Major finding; breaking three or more is Critical.

**Forms**
- At least 6 different forms; no form more than twice.
- At least 3 plotted forms (11–18) and at least 3 quiet forms (1–10).
- At most 1 story touching elite-level intrigue (seen from below).
- At most 2 craft-and-mastery stories.

**Shapes**
- At least 4 different shapes; single day at most 3 times.

**Openings and endings**
- At least 7 different opening devices across the ten stories; at most 1 story opens with waking or daybreak.
- At least 5 different ending types; no type more than twice; at most 1 ending in which an authority praises or rewards the protagonist.

**People**
- At least 3 female and 3 male protagonists (where the evidence allows; the bible records exceptions).
- At least 1 child or young adolescent protagonist and at least 1 old protagonist.
- At least 2 unfree or dependent protagonists where the society had slavery, serfdom, or bondage.
- At most 2 elite protagonists; the majority are ordinary.
- At least 1 outsider where plausible.
- No occupation category more than twice; craftspeople of any kind at most 3.
- A mentor–apprentice relationship central to at most 2 stories.

**Premises and settings**
- The premise "an authority demands an impossible task by a deadline" at most once per book.
- At least 4 setting types (seat of power or temple, town, village and fields, water, road or mountain, household, workshop, sanctuary, wild land).
- At least 3 seasons.

**Narration**
- Default: past tense, third person, one point-of-view character per story.
- First person at most 2 stories per book; present tense at most 1.

## 8. Series-level variety

Across a series, the slate also checks:

- No two stories in the series share the combination *protagonist occupation + primary form + central object or event*.
- Every form in the palette appears at least once across any run of three consecutive books, unless the bible excludes it for this civilisation (with reason).
- No more than one story per series built on the same famous artefact or site.
- The line registry (if supplied) is checked so that concepts common across civilisations are not repeated in the same shape: if a series uses one, it must use a different form or shape from the last three series that did.
